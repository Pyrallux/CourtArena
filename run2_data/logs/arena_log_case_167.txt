FACTS:
Defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") (together, "defendants") appeal an order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New *199York (Preska, J .), denying defendants' motions to dismiss under (1) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on grounds of foreign sovereign immunity and (2) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) pursuant to the act of state doctrine. We affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

BACKGROUND
Unless otherwise noted, the facts herein are undisputed. They are drawn from the complaint and the documents submitted by the parties in reference to defendants' motions to dismiss.

I. YPF Becomes a Publicly Traded Company

YPF is a petroleum company that was wholly owned and operated by the Argentine government until 1993. That year, in accordance with broader efforts to reform its economy, Argentina decided to privatize the petrol firm through an initial public offering ("IPO") of nearly 100% of YPF's voting stock (the "shares").1 Argentina and YPF took a number of steps to entice investors to participate in the IPO and thereby ensure its success, two of which are particularly relevant to this case. First, they arranged for YPF to offer shares in the United States as American Depository Receipts ("ADRs") listed on the New York Stock Exchange ("NYSE"). Second, they amended YPF's bylaws-that is, the contract governing the relationship among YPF, Argentina (in its capacity as a shareholder), and other YPF shareholders. In particular, the bylaws were amended to incorporate protections for investors from (1) hostile takeovers and (2) attempts by Argentina to renationalize the company. These takeover protections form the basis of this breach of contract dispute, and so we describe them in some detail.

Section 7(d) of the amended bylaws prohibits (with certain exceptions inapplicable here) the direct or indirect acquisition of YPF shares if the acquisition results in the acquirer controlling 15% or more of the shares, unless the acquirer makes a tender offer for all of the outstanding shares in accordance with certain procedures and at a price determined by a formula in the bylaws. Among the prescribed procedures, section 7(f) requires that any such tender offer comply with the rules and regulations imposed by the governments and stock exchanges where YPF's shares are listed. Because YPF's securities were to be listed on the NYSE, those conducting tender offers in accordance with these shareholder protection measures would be compelled by section 7(f) to comply with NYSE and Securities and Exchange Commission ("SEC") rules and regulations. Section 7(f)(iv) further obligates the acquirer to publish notice of its tender offer "in the business section of the major newspapers ... in the City of New York, U.S.A. and any other city where the shares [of YPF]
*200shall be listed." App. 340. Perhaps most importantly for purposes of this appeal, section 28(A) of the bylaws extends the tender offer requirement of sections 7(e) and 7(f) to:
all acquisitions made by the [Government of Argentina], whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument, of shares or securities of [YPF], 1) if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the [Government] becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of [YPF], which, in addition to the prior holdings thereof of any class of shares, represent, in the aggregate, at least 49% of the capital stock [of YPF]; or 2) if the [Government] acquires at least 8% of class D outstanding shares of stock, while withholding class A shares of stock amounting at least to 5% of the capital stock.
App. 432.

The penalties for breaching these provisions are drastic. Section 7(h) provides that "[s]hares of stock and securities acquired in breach of [the tender offer requirements] shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends." App. 342. And section 28(C) extends such treatment to shares acquired by Argentina, unless its breach is accidental. In that case, "[t]he penalties provided for in subsection (h) of Section 7 shall be limited ... to the loss of the right to vote." App. 355. At bottom, these shareholder protection measures appear to promise investors a compensated exit from their ownership position in the firm if Argentina were to decide to renationalize YPF.

Argentina and YPF touted these protections in the prospectus filed with the SEC in connection with the IPO. That document stated that "[u]nder [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws." App. 23. The prospectus further stated that "any Control Acquisition carried out by the Argentine Government other than in accordance with th[at] procedure ... will result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired." Id. (alteration in original).

By all accounts, Argentina's marketing efforts worked. YPF launched a successful IPO on June 29, 1993. Through the sale of YPF securities, Argentina raised billions of dollars in investment capital with the largest share (more than $1.1 billion in total) coming from the sale of ADRs in the United States on the NYSE. A firm called Repsol S.A. ("Repsol") emerged from the IPO as YPF's majority shareholder. Even after the IPO, however, Argentina continued to participate in YPF's corporate governance as a commercial actor. It remained a holder of YPF's Class A shares, entitling it to elect at least one member of the firm's board of directors. Argentina also retained a veto right over certain third-party acquisitions of YPF's capital stock. After the IPO, YPF's shares, via the ADRs, were traded publicly on the NYSE and other exchanges.

Plaintiffs-appellees Petersen EnergÃ­a Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen EnergÃ­a, S.A.U. (together, "Petersen") entered the picture in 2008. Between 2008 and 2011, Petersen conducted a series of acquisitions and came to own approximately 25% of YPF's shares, held in the form of ADRs issued by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. All of Petersen's acquisitions were made in accordance with YPF's bylaws, including the tender offer provisions in section 7. The bulk of Petersen's shares were purchased from Repsol and their purchase was financed by Repsol and various financial institutions, which maintained *201a security interest in the stock as collateral. As part of a shareholder agreement with Petersen, Repsol agreed to cause YPF to make biannual distributions of 90% of its profits to shareholders via dividends in accordance with section 25 of the bylaws. Petersen often used these dividends to make payments on the loans it used to finance the purchase of YPF stock.

All of that changed in 2012. Early that year, members of the Argentine government began publicly criticizing Repsol's and Petersen's management of YPF and started discussing the prospect of renationalizing the company. The value of YPF's ADRs plummeted in response to this news. To put what happened next in the appropriate context, it helps to understand a little about the mechanics of Argentine expropriation law.

II. Argentine Expropriation Law

Expropriation is the "governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights." Expropriation , BLACK'S LAW DICTIONARY (10th ed. 2014). A "classic example" is the government's condemnation of a parcel of land to make way for some public good, like a road. Murr v. Wisconsin , --- U.S. ----, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939, 198 L.Ed.2d 497 (2017). The enactment of land use regulations may also, in some cases, constitute an expropriation. See id. But these land-based examples understate the breadth of a sovereign's power of expropriation, which can be vast. That is so because all types of property can be expropriated, whether tangible or intangible. Personal property, airspace rights, contract rights, even the shares of a corporation-at least in theory, a sovereign can expropriate them all. See Penn Cent. Transp. Co. v. City of New York , 438 U.S. 104, 128, 98 S.Ct. 2646, 57 L.Ed.2d 631 (1978) (discussing a "taking" of airspace rights); accord August Reinisch, Expropriation , in The Oxford Handbook of International Investment Law 407, 410 (Peter Muchlinski et al. eds., 2008) ("It is generally asserted that expropriation may affect not only tangible property but also a broad range of intangible assets of economic value to an investor. Property that may be expropriated by states thus comprises immaterial rights and interests, including in particular contractual rights."). In reality, however, whether a government may expropriate property, what property is subject to expropriation, and how much the government must compensate the individual it expropriated the property from (if at all) are largely questions of law of the expropriating nation. Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts , 28 Fordham. L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) ("States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best; ... they are free to operate their municipal system of property according to their own national genius ...."); compare Org. for Econ. Co-operation & Dev., "Indirect Expropriation" and the "Right to Regulate" in International Investment Law , in International Investment Law: A Changing Landscape 43, 43-72 (2005) (discussing limits imposed on expropriations by customary international law). In this case, we look to Argentine law. See Garb v. Republic of Poland , 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006).

Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution sets the conditions under which property may be expropriated by the Argentine government. To effectuate an expropriation consistent with Article 17, two conditions must be met: (1) the Argentine Congress must declare a public use for the property to be expropriated and (2) the owner of the property must be compensated. The Argentine government has passed laws to clarify what property is subject to *202expropriation and to specify the procedures that must be followed to meet the conditions for expropriation.

One such law is Law 21,499, known as the "General Expropriation Law." App. 57. It empowers, among other entities, the Argentine Federal Government to act as an expropriator. As for the declaration of public use required by Article 17 of the National Constitution, section 5 of the General Expropriation Law clarifies that the Argentine Congress "shall particularly refer to specific property" to be expropriated in its declaration and section 1 provides that "[p]ublic use, which is required as legal grounds for expropriation, comprises all cases where public welfare may be involved." App. 185-86. The law further declares that "[a]ll such property as may be convenient or necessary to satisfy [that] 'public use' purpose, whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not, may be subject to expropriation ...." App. 186. As for compensation for that property, section 10 of the General Expropriation Law provides that the owner shall receive "the objective value of the property plus any direct and immediate damages resulting from expropriation," such amounts to be fixed by agreement of the owner and expropriator or pursuant to a court proceeding. App. 187. And, presumably to prevent the owner's malfeasance while compensation is being fixed, section 16 of the law proclaims that "[n]o contract executed by the owner after the effective date of the law declaring the expropriation of the property and which may imply the creation of any right or interest in the property shall be good as against the expropriator." App. 187.
Accordingly, with this legal backdrop in mind, we return to how Argentina regained control over YPF's affairs in the spring of 2012.

III. Argentina Regains Control of YPF

On April 16, 2012, pursuant to the General Expropriation Law, Argentina proposed legislation that would expropriate directly from Repsol 51% of the voting stock of YPF. On the same day, the Argentine National Executive Office decreed that it was empowering an "Intervenor" to seize immediate control of YPF's operations and to operate the company as a going concern while the Argentine Congress considered the expropriation legislation. Action was swift. Indeed, before some of these measures were even announced publicly, the Intervenor seized control of YPF's facilities, replaced top management with government officials, and escorted YPF's then-CEO off the premises. The Intervenor also cancelled regularly-scheduled meetings of YPF's board of directors and refused to make expected dividend payments.

Argentine officials were also quick to declare that, despite having acquired control of the company, Argentina and YPF had no intention of complying with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. For example, on April 17, 2012, in a speech before the Argentine Senate, the country's Deputy Economy Minister described as "fools ... those who think that the State has to be stupid and buy everyone according to YPF's own law, respecting its by-law." App. 29 n.1. He also dismissed the tender offer requirements as "unfair" and a "bear trap." Id .

On May 3, 2012, the proposed expropriation legislation was enacted as Law 26,741 with an effective date of May 7, 2012 (the "YPF Expropriation Law"). In accordance with Article 17 of the National Constitution, the YPF Expropriation Law pronounced Argentina's national public interest in achieving "self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon[ ] supply," App. 165, by, inter *203alia , integrating "public and private ... capital into strategic alliances aimed at the exploration and exploitation of conventional and unconventional hydrocarbons," App. 166. 
The law further provided that:
to ensure the fulfillment of the objectives of this law, the fifty-one percent (51%) equity interest in YPF Sociedad AnÃ³nima represented by the same percentage of Class D shares of the said Company, held by Repsol YPF S.A., its controlled or controlling entities, directly or indirectly, is hereby declared to be of public use and subject to expropriation.
App. 167. The YPF Expropriation Law also extended the Intervenor's control over the firm's operations and granted the Argentine executive branch the right to "exercise all the political rights over all the shares subject to expropriation" until the expropriation, including compensation of Repsol, was finalized. App. 167.

Argentina did indeed exercise the rights of Repsol's shares, using them to cancel YPF's previously-scheduled dividend payment and board meeting in April 2012, and voted the shares at a shareholder meeting in June 2012, in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Unable to meet its loan obligations without the dividend payment, Petersen entered insolvency proceedings in July 2012 and its lenders foreclosed on the YPF ADRs that Petersen had pledged as collateral. Repsol was eventually compensated for its expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion.

IV. Procedural History

Petersen commenced this action in the district court on April 8, 2015, alleging, inter alia , breach of contract on grounds that (1) Argentina repudiated its obligation to make the tender offer in accordance with sections 7(e) and (f) and 28 of the bylaws, (2) YPF breached its obligation to ensure Argentina made such a tender offer in light of its acquisition of Repsol's shares, and (3) YPF permitted Argentina to exercise the voting rights of Repsol's shares and other corporate governance powers in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Defendants moved to dismiss the complaint, arguing, inter alia , that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, 28 U.S.C. Â§ 1601 et seq. (the "FSIA"), and that Petersen's claims were barred by the "act of state doctrine." As is relevant here, the district court denied defendants' motions to dismiss with respect to the FSIA and act of state issues. They timely appealed the FSIA ruling and the district court subsequently certified the act of state issue for our interlocutory review.


YPF's refusal to enforce section 7(h)'s penalties had a direct effect in the United States because (1) it enabled Argentina to cancel planned dividend payments, some of which would have been made to investors based in the United States, and (2) it precipitated Petersen's default on its loan obligations and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs, which were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City.

### OPENING ARGUMENT FOR PLAINTIFF/PROSECUTION COUNSEL  

Your Honors, this case is not about sovereign expropriation or foreign policy—it is a straightforward breach of contract claim arising from Argentina’s and YPF’s violation of binding commercial obligations they voluntarily created to attract U.S. investors. The district court correctly denied dismissal under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) and the act of state doctrine because these defendants engaged in commercial activity within the United States, and Petersen’s claims are predicated solely on private contractual rights.  

#### I. THE FSIA’S COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION APPLIES  
Under 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), FSIA immunity does not shield foreign states when their actions “are based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state.” *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 619 (1992). The key inquiry is whether the activity has a “commercial character”—that is, whether it is the type of conduct a private party would undertake in the marketplace. Id. at 614. Here, Argentina and YPF deliberately incorporated the tender offer provisions into YPF’s bylaws as part of a public offering on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) to entice U.S. investors. The prospectus explicitly represented to investors that these provisions were “protections” against renationalization and that any acquisition of control by Argentina would trigger a mandatory tender offer to all shareholders. App. 23. This was not a sovereign act of governance; it was a commercial transaction designed to secure billions in U.S. capital.  

In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court held that a sovereign’s issuance of bonds in the U.S. financial market was commercial activity because it “was undertaken for the purpose of raising capital to finance the government’s operations.” 504 U.S. at 619. Similarly, here, Argentina and YPF amended the bylaws *specifically* to facilitate YPF’s IPO on the NYSE and attract U.S. investors. The tender offer requirements were not imposed as a matter of sovereign policy—they were contractual terms negotiated to reassure investors. As the Second Circuit confirmed in *NML Capital, Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, 699 F.3d 246, 255 (2d Cir. 2012), “when a foreign sovereign enters into a commercial contract with a private party in the United States, the commercial activity exception applies.” The bylaws were a private contract between YPF, Argentina (as a shareholder), and all shareholders—including Petersen, who purchased ADRs on the NYSE.  

Critically, the defendants’ actions had direct effects in the United States. When Argentina expropriated Repsol’s shares without triggering the tender offer, YPF—which had a contractual duty under the bylaws to enforce the penalties—allowed Argentina to cancel dividend payments and vote shares in contravention of Section 7(h). These dividends were paid to U.S. holders of ADRs, including Petersen, whose shares were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. App. 201. When dividends ceased, Petersen defaulted on U.S. loans, and its ADRs were foreclosed in New York. As the Supreme Court held in *First National City Bank v. Banco Para el Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 622 (1983), the commercial activity exception applies when the “effects” of the sovereign’s conduct are “felt in the United States.” Here, the breach directly impacted U.S. investors, U.S. financial institutions, and U.S. markets.  

#### II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE DOES NOT BAR PETERSEN’S CONTRACT CLAIMS  
The act of state doctrine—“a principle of comity under which courts decline to question the validity of a foreign government’s official acts within its own territory”—does not apply to contractual disputes. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964). As the Court explicitly stated in *Sabbatino*, “the act of state doctrine is not applicable to claims based on contract or property rights.” Id. at 428. This is because contract claims are “private rights” between parties, not challenges to the validity of sovereign acts.  

Here, Petersen’s claims are purely contractual: Argentina breached its obligation to make a tender offer under Sections 7(e), 7(f), and 28 of the bylaws; YPF breached its duty to ensure Argentina complied; and both violated Section 7(h) by permitting Argentina to exercise voting rights despite the breach. The bylaws are a private corporate governance document—no different from the contractual terms in *Sabbatino* itself, where the Cuban government’s seizure of U.S. bank assets was challenged under a commercial contract. As the district court correctly recognized, these claims do not require the court to “pass judgment on the legality of Argentina’s expropriation” but only to enforce the terms of a contract that Argentina and YPF voluntarily agreed to with U.S. investors.  

#### III. PETERSEN PLAUSIBLY ALLEGED BREACHES OF THE BYLAWS  
Petersen’s complaint adequately alleges three distinct breaches:  
1. **Argentina’s failure to make a tender offer**: The bylaws required Argentina to issue a tender offer if it acquired 49% or more of YPF’s shares. App. 432 (Section 28(A)). Argentina’s expropriation of 51% of Repsol’s shares triggered this obligation—but Argentina publicly declared the tender offer requirement “foolish” and a “bear trap,” App. 29 n.1, and took no steps to comply.  
2. **YPF’s failure to ensure compliance**: As a corporate entity bound by the bylaws, YPF had a duty to enforce the tender offer requirement. Instead, YPF actively facilitated Argentina’s breach by canceling dividend payments and board meetings at Argentina’s direction.  
3. **YPF’s permitting of improper voting rights**: Section 7(h) explicitly states that shares acquired in breach of the tender offer provisions “shall not grant any right to vote.” App. 342. Yet YPF allowed Argentina to vote the expropriated shares at a shareholder meeting in June 2012—directly violating the bylaws.  

These allegations are not speculative. They are grounded in the defendants’ own public statements, the expropriation law, and the undisputed fact that Argentina exercised voting rights without a tender offer. As this Court held in *Sabbatino*, “a court’s duty to enforce a contract does not require it to second-guess the wisdom of a foreign government’s act”—it simply requires enforcing the terms of the agreement the parties made. 376 U.S. at 428.  

#### CONCLUSION  
This is not a case about sovereign power or foreign policy. It is about Argentina and YPF reneging on promises they made to U.S. investors to secure capital for their IPO. The FSIA does not shield them because they engaged in commercial activity in the United States, and the act of state doctrine cannot bar a private contract claim. The district court rightly denied dismissal. We urge this Court to affirm.

## INTRODUCTION

Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") respectfully submit this opening brief in support of their appeal from the district court's order denying their motions to dismiss. The district court erred in denying dismissal under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA") because Argentina's actions in this case constitute sovereign governmental conduct—specifically, the exercise of the eminent domain power pursuant to constitutionally-authorized expropriation legislation—that is not "commercial activity" within the meaning of the FSIA's exception. Furthermore, the act of state doctrine bars this action because adjudication of Petersen's claims would require this Court to pass judgment on the validity and legality of Argentina's sovereign expropriation of YPF shares under its own constitutional and statutory framework.

---

## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The undisputed facts establish that Argentina exercised its sovereign power of expropriation under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution and the General Expropriation Law (Law 21,499) to acquire 51% of YPF's voting stock from Repsol for public use in furtherance of national hydrocarbon policy. This was not a commercial transaction undertaken for profit or private gain; it was a sovereign act of eminent domain undertaken pursuant to legislative authorization for a declared public purpose.

YPF is a petroleum company that was wholly owned by the Argentine government until 1993, when Argentina privatized the company through an IPO. In connection with this privatization, Argentina and YPF amended YPF's bylaws to include certain shareholder protections, including tender offer requirements. These bylaws were designed to facilitate the IPO and attract investors to what had been a government-owned enterprise.

Petersen acquired approximately 25% of YPF's shares between 2008 and 2011 through purchases on the open market, including from Repsol. In 2012, Argentina enacted the YPF Expropriation Law (Law 26,741), which declared the acquisition of 51% of YPF's shares from Repsol to be of public use and subject to expropriation. This legislation was enacted pursuant to Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution, which requires both a declaration of public use and compensation to the owner.

Argentina did not comply with the tender offer provisions in YPF's bylaws when acquiring control of YPF, taking the position that these provisions could not impose obligations on the sovereign's exercise of its constitutional expropriation power.

## II. ARGUMENT

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING DISMISSAL UNDER THE FSIA BECAUSE ARGENTINA'S CONDUCT CONSTITUTES SOVEREIGN CONDUCT, NOT COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY

#### A. Legal Standard

The FSIA provides that foreign states are "immune from the jurisdiction of the courts of the United States" unless an exception applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The burden is on the plaintiff to demonstrate that an exception applies. *See Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 488 U.S. 1, 16 (1983). The commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), applies only when the foreign state's actions are based upon "a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state."

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the critical inquiry is whether the activity is "commercial" in nature—i.e., whether it is the type of action a private party would undertake in the marketplace—rather than an action that only a sovereign may take. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992). A sovereign does not engage in commercial activity when it acts in its sovereign capacity, as opposed to its private capacity. *See id.*; *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 361 (1993).

#### B. Argentina's Expropriation Was Sovereign Conduct, Not Commercial Activity

The central question is whether Argentina's acquisition of 51% of YPF's shares constitutes conduct a private party would undertake. The answer is no. Argentina exercised its eminent domain power—the quintessential sovereign prerogative—to expropriate property for a declared public purpose pursuant to constitutional and statutory authority. This is fundamentally different from a private party purchasing shares in the open market.

In *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398 (1964), the Supreme Court recognized that a nation's expropriation of property within its own territory is an act of state that courts will not question. Similarly, in *Underhill v. Hernandez*, 168 U.S. 250, 252 (1897), the Court held that "every sovereign state has an inherent right to take the property of its own citizens, or of aliens, within its jurisdiction, for public purposes." Expropriation is not a commercial activity; it is an exercise of sovereign authority that no private entity could undertake.

The plaintiff relies heavily on *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607 (1992), but that case is distinguishable. In *Weltover*, the Supreme Court found commercial activity because Argentina had issued bonds in the U.S. financial markets "for the purpose of raising capital to finance the government's operations." *Id.* at 619. Issuing bonds is an activity private parties routinely undertake to raise capital. By contrast, no private party can exercise the power of eminent domain to seize property for public use. Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares was an exercise of sovereign authority, not a capital-raising transaction.

The plaintiff argues that Argentina's conduct is commercial because it voluntarily incorporated the tender offer provisions into YPF's bylaws to facilitate the IPO. This argument conflates two distinct events: (1) Argentina's initial decision to privatize YPF and structure the IPO, and (2) Argentina's later exercise of its sovereign expropriation power in 2012. The FSIA analysis focuses on the specific conduct upon which the plaintiff's claims are based. *See Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 357 ("[T]he issue is immunity under the FSIA turns on the nature of the activity, not on whether the foreign state has previously performed the act elsewhere or whether the particular act involves a private counterpart."). Here, the claims are based on Argentina's 2012 expropriation—an act of sovereign authority—not on the 1993 IPO.

Furthermore, the plaintiff cannot establish that Argentina's expropriation was "carried on in the United States" within the meaning of § 1605(a)(2). The expropriation was effected under Argentine law, targeting shares of an Argentine corporation, and was authorized by the Argentine Congress. The mere fact that YPF's shares were traded in the form of ADRs in the United States does not transform a sovereign expropriation into commercial activity carried on in the United States. *See NML Capital, Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, 699 F.3d 246, 255 (2d Cir. 2012) (holding that the commercial activity exception requires a "direct effect" in the United States from the specific commercial activity, not merely some tangential connection).

#### C. YPF Is Entitled to Immunity As An Instrumentality of a Foreign State

YPF, as a corporation wholly owned by the Argentine government during the relevant period and acting under the direction of the Argentine government, is an "instrumentality" of a foreign state under the FSIA. 28 U.S.C. § 1603(b). Accordingly, YPF is entitled to immunity to the same extent as Argentina itself. *See Jam v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 766 (2019) (holding that agencies and instrumentalities of foreign states share the same immunity as the foreign state itself).

Even if YPF could be said to have engaged in some commercial activity, the claims against YPF are inextricably linked to Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. YPF cannot be held liable for complying with Argentine law and government directives in connection with the expropriation. The district court erred in denying YPF's motion to dismiss on FSIA grounds.

---

### II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS PETERSEN'S CLAIMS

#### A. Legal Standard

The act of state doctrine "is a principle of comity under which courts decline to question the validity of a foreign government's official acts within its own territory." *W.S. Attributes.* The doctrine reflects "the strong sense of the judicial branch that its engagement in the task of passing on the validity of foreign acts of state may hinder" the conduct of foreign affairs. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964).

The doctrine applies to "all cases in which a foreign state exercises its jurisdiction in rem"—that is, where it seizes or modifies property rights within its territory. *See id.* at 428. As the Supreme Court explained in *Sabbatino*, the act of state doctrine applies even where the foreign state's action may violate international law, unless there is a "clear and certain" statutory exception providing a judicial remedy. *Id.* at 428.

#### B. The Act of State Doctrine Applies to This Dispute

The plaintiff's attempt to characterize this case as a simple breach of contract dispute is a mischaracterization that the district court erroneously accepted. Adjudication of Petersen's claims would require this Court to:

1. **Determine the validity of Argentina's expropriation**: Petersen alleges that Argentina breached its obligations under the bylaws by acquiring 51% of YPF without making a tender offer. But to determine whether such a breach occurred, this Court would necessarily have to evaluate whether Argentina's expropriation was valid under Argentine law and whether the tender offer provisions could legally obligate a sovereign to tender for shares when exercising its constitutional eminent domain power. This is precisely the type of inquiry the act of state doctrine prohibits.

2. **Pass judgment on Argentine law and policy**: The plaintiff asks this Court to declare that Argentina's exercise of its expropriation power under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution and the YPF Expropriation Law must yield to privately-created bylaws. Such a ruling would constitute a direct challenge to the validity of Argentina's sovereign acts—the expropriation legislation and its implementation.

3. **Second-guess Argentina's determination of public use**: Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution requires a declaration of "public use" before expropriation. The YPF Expropriation Law declared the acquisition of YPF shares to be in the public interest for hydrocarbon self-sufficiency. Challenging the adequacy of this public use determination—and the consequent failure to comply with tender offer procedures—would require this Court to evaluate the wisdom and legality of Argentina's sovereign policy decisions.

The plaintiff's reliance on the dictum in *Sabbatino* that the doctrine "is not applicable to claims based on contract or property rights" is misplaced. *Id.* at 428. That statement must be read in context. The *Sabbatino* Court was distinguishing between claims challenging the underlying act of state (which are barred) and claims seeking to enforce contractual obligations between private parties that do not require adjudication of the validity of the sovereign act. Here, Petersen cannot enforce the bylaws without challenging the very validity of Argentina's expropriation.

In *Sabbatino* itself, the Court declined to adjudicate a claim that required evaluating the validity of Cuba's expropriation of a U.S. bank's property, even though the plaintiff had a contractual relationship with the bank. The same result should obtain here. Argentina expropriated shares pursuant to valid legislation; the plaintiff cannot circumvent the act of state doctrine simply by labeling its claim "breach of contract."

#### C. The Commercial Activity Exception to the Act of State Doctrine Does Not Apply

The plaintiff argues that the act of state doctrine does not apply because the underlying conduct was commercial. This argument fails for the reasons stated above: Argentina's expropriation was a sovereign act, not a commercial transaction. The fact that Argentina may have structured the original IPO in a commercial manner does not transform the subsequent sovereign expropriation into a commercial act.

---

### III. EVEN IF THIS COURT REACHES THE MERITS, PETERSEN'S BREACH OF CONTRACT CLAIMS FAIL ON SUBSTANTIVE GROUNDS

#### A. Argentina Could Not Contract Away Its Sovereign Power of Expropriation

Under Argentine constitutional law, the sovereign power of eminent domain cannot be contracted away. Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution grants the government the power to expropriate property for public use upon payment of compensation. This is a fundamental sovereign prerogative that cannot be limited or waived by private contractual arrangements such as corporate bylaws.

A tender offer requirement that would obligate the sovereign to pay compensation determined by a private formula—rather than the objective value fixed by law—would effectively grant shareholders a veto over the sovereign's exercise of its constitutional power. Argentine law does not permit the sovereign to contract away this essential attribute of sovereignty. *See Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts*, 28 Fordham L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) ("States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best.").

#### B. The Bylaws Do Not Create Enforceable Obligations Against Argentina

Section 28(A) of the bylaws purports to extend the tender offer requirement to acquisitions by the Government of Argentina. However, as a matter of Argentine law, the government cannot be bound by corporate bylaws to the exercise of its sovereign powers. The bylaws were adopted as part of the privatization of YPF and were designed to protect minority shareholders in the context of a private corporation. They were not intended to—and could not—bind the sovereign's exercise of its constitutional eminent domain power.

To the extent the bylaws purport to create such obligations, they are void as against public policy. A sovereign cannot be compelled to exercise its expropriation power according to privately-determined terms, and shareholders cannot obtain injunctive relief that would effectively prevent the sovereign from exercising its constitutional powers.

#### C. Petersen Failed to Allege Damages Adequately

Petersen's alleged damages stem from its inability to pay loans that were used to finance its acquisition of YPF shares. However, these damages are too remote from the alleged breach. Petersen chose to finance its acquisition with significant debt, using the shares as collateral. The risk of non-payment was a risk Petersen knowingly assumed. Argentina's expropriation of shares from a third party (Repsol)—not from Petersen—does not constitute a direct breach of any duty owed to Petersen.

Furthermore, any damages Petersen may have suffered were not caused by the alleged breach of the tender offer provisions, but by the sovereign act of expropriation, which is not actionable.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

Upon review of the opening arguments submitted by Plaintiff-Appellee Petersen Energía Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen Energía, S.A.U. (together, "Petersen") and Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF"), and based solely on the facts and arguments provided in the record, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA)**

Defendants appeal the district court's denial of their motion to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under the FSIA, 28 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq. They argue that Argentina's actions constituted sovereign conduct (expropriation) rather than commercial activity, and thus fall outside the commercial activity exception of 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Court is not persuaded.

Under the FSIA, a foreign state is presumptively immune from the jurisdiction of United States courts unless a specific exception applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The commercial activity exception removes immunity where the action "is based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere that causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The Supreme Court has clarified that the determination of what constitutes "commercial activity" turns on the nature of the conduct rather than its purpose. In *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), the Court held that the relevant inquiry is whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) "are the type of actions that could be taken by a private participant in the commercial sphere." Here, the specific conduct upon which Petersen's claims are based is not the abstract act of expropriation or the declaration of public use, but rather the alleged breach of the tender offer provisions contained in YPF's bylaws and the subsequent voting of shares and cancellation of dividends.

The facts indicate that the bylaws, including Sections 7 and 28, were amended as part of a privatization effort and an initial public offering (IPO) on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) to attract U.S. investors. These provisions created contractual obligations regarding the acquisition of control and the voting rights of shares. Argentina's failure to comply with the tender offer requirements, and YPF's facilitation of Argentina's voting rights in contravention of Section 7(h), are actions that private corporations and shareholders routinely undertake. As argued by Plaintiff, these acts are "commercial" in nature because they involve the management and governance of a corporate entity in which U.S. citizens hold a substantial financial interest via American Depository Receipts (ADRs). See *NML Capital, Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, 699 F.3d 246, 255 (2d Cir. 2012) (recognizing that a foreign sovereign's participation in a commercial contract triggers the exception).

Furthermore, the "direct effect" requirement is satisfied. The record shows that the cancellation of dividend payments precipitated Petersen's default on loans held by U.S. financial institutions and led to the foreclosure of ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. These are direct effects within the United States. See *First National City Bank v. Banco Para el Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 622 (1983) (holding that the "effects" of the sovereign's conduct must be felt in the United States).

Defendants' reliance on *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349 (1993), and *Underhill v. Hernandez*, 168 U.S. 250 (1897), is misplaced. While expropriation is a sovereign prerogative, the specific contractual obligations at issue here—voluntarily undertaken by Argentina and YPF to facilitate commercial investment—govern the *manner* in which control was exercised. The fact that the underlying motive was renationalization does not transform the breach of these commercial contractual terms into a sovereign act immune from jurisdiction under the FSIA. Therefore, the district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(1).

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

Defendants further argue that the act of state doctrine bars Petersen's claims because adjudication would require this Court to pass judgment on the validity of Argentina's sovereign expropriation under its own laws. The Court disagrees.

The act of state doctrine is "a principle of comity under which courts decline to question the validity of a foreign government's official acts within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964). However, the doctrine is not absolute. As the Supreme Court stated in *Sabbatino*, the doctrine "is not applicable to claims based on contract or property rights." Id. at 428.

Petersen's claims are founded squarely on breach of contract. The complaint alleges that Argentina and YPF violated specific provisions of the YPF bylaws—a private corporate governance contract—by failing to make a tender offer and by exercising voting rights in contravention of Section 7(h). To adjudicate these claims, the Court need not declare the YPF Expropriation Law invalid or question Argentina's sovereign right to expropriate property for public use under Article 17 of its Constitution. Rather, the Court need only determine whether the parties' commercial agreement (the bylaws) was breached. The enforcement of a contractual promise does not inherently constitute a challenge to the public acts of the foreign sovereign.

Defendants contend that enforcing the bylaws would effectively invalidate the expropriation law by conditioning the sovereign's exercise of power on private terms. However, this argument conflates the validity of the expropriation with the remedies for a breach of contract. The expropriation may stand as a valid act of state, but that does not automatically absolve the sovereign of contractual liabilities incurred in connection with the commercial management of the enterprise prior to or during the expropriation. Because Petersen seeks to enforce private contractual rights rather than attack the validity of the public expropriation, the act of state doctrine does not bar this action.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the commercial activity exception to the FSIA applies, and the act of state doctrine does not preclude adjudication of Plaintiff's breach of contract claims. The district court's order denying the motions to dismiss is affirmed.

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE FSIA COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION  
#### A. Argentina’s Breach of Contract Claims Are Based on Commercial Conduct, Not Sovereign Acts  
The defense falsely contends that Argentina’s conduct—failing to make a tender offer and exercising voting rights in violation of YPF’s bylaws—constitutes “sovereign conduct.” This misapprehends the FSIA’s central inquiry: the *nature of the specific conduct* at issue, not its underlying purpose. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). The Supreme Court has consistently held that “a foreign state engages in commercial activity when it acts ‘in a manner to which a private person could be subject’”—regardless of motive. *Id.* at 618. Here, the claims center on Argentina’s failure to honor *contractual terms* it explicitly incorporated into YPF’s bylaws to facilitate the IPO and secure U.S. investor confidence.  

- **The bylaws were commercial instruments.** Argentina and YPF amended the bylaws to include tender offer requirements and voting restrictions as part of a privatization effort designed to attract foreign capital. The prospectus filed with the SEC explicitly stated that “under [YPF’s] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF’s] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws.” App. 23. This was not a sovereign act but a *commercial commitment* to U.S. investors. When Argentina acquired 51% of YPF’s shares in 2012, it did so *while bound by these contractual obligations*, which were expressly designed to govern corporate governance in a commercial context.  

- **The specific conduct complained of is indistinguishable from private commercial activity.** The defense’s claim that Argentina’s failure to make a tender offer or comply with section 7(h) is “sovereign” because it occurred during an expropriation ignores the *Weltover* framework. In *Weltover*, Argentina’s issuance of bonds to raise capital—despite being for government operations—was held commercial because “private parties routinely issue bonds to raise capital.” *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 619. Here, Argentina’s breach of the tender offer and voting restrictions mirrors a private corporation’s failure to honor contractual obligations governing share acquisitions. No private entity could “expropriate” property, but *every private corporation is bound by its own bylaws when acquiring shares or exercising voting rights*. The defense’s reliance on *Underhill v. Hernandez*, 168 U.S. 250 (1897), is misplaced. *Underhill* addressed whether courts could question foreign sovereign acts *in rem* (e.g., land seizures), but it does not apply to contractual breaches. As this Court held in *NML Capital, Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, 699 F.3d 246, 255 (2d Cir. 2012), “the FSIA exception applies when the sovereign’s conduct involves commercial contracts or transactions.”

Here, the claims arise from Argentina’s *failure to follow contractual procedures*—not its decision to renationalize. Argentina’s public statements dismissing the tender offer as a “bear trap” (App. 29 n.1) confirm it knew these were binding commercial terms, not sovereign prerogatives. The district court correctly noted that “the specific contractual obligations at issue here—voluntarily undertaken by Argentina and YPF to facilitate commercial investment—govern the *manner* in which control was exercised.”  

#### B. The Claims Satisfy the FSIA’s “Direct Effect” Requirement  
The defense ignores that Argentina’s breach caused *direct effects in the United States*, satisfying 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The record shows:  
- Argentina’s cancellation of dividend payments (in breach of section 7(h)) directly deprived U.S.-based investors of distributions;  
- Petersen’s inability to pay loans (due to canceled dividends) triggered defaults on U.S. financial institutions; and

These are “direct effects” under *First National City Bank v. #### C. YPF Is Not Entitled to Immunity as an Instrumentality  
YPF, as a corporation with publicly traded shares on the NYSE, engaged in commercial activity when it facilitated Argentina’s breach of the bylaws. The defense incorrectly asserts YPF “cannot be held liable for complying with Argentine law.” But YPF’s duty to enforce section 7(h)—which stripped Argentina of voting rights for non-compliant acquisitions—was a *commercial obligation* arising from its corporate governance contract. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 766 (2019) (holding that instrumentalities lose immunity when acting in commercial capacities).

### II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE DOES NOT BARS THESE BREACH-OF-CONTRACT CLAIMS  
#### A. The Doctrine Does Not Apply to Contract Claims  
The defense misreads *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398 (1964), to suggest the act of state doctrine bars all claims involving foreign sovereign acts. This is incorrect. *Sabbatino* explicitly states that the doctrine “is not applicable to claims based on contract or property rights.” *Id.* at 428. The Supreme Court clarified that the doctrine only bars challenges to the *validity* of a foreign sovereign’s acts; it does not prevent courts from enforcing private contractual obligations. Here, Petersen does not seek to invalidate Argentina’s expropriation law. It seeks to enforce *private contractual terms*—the bylaws—that Argentina and YPF voluntarily agreed to when attracting U.S. investment.  

- The district court correctly distinguished between *challenging the expropriation* (barred) and *enforcing a contract* (permitted). As the court noted, “adjudication of Petersen’s claims would require this Court to determine whether the parties’ commercial agreement (the bylaws) was breached”—not whether Argentina’s public use declaration was valid. *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428. This is precisely the type of claim the doctrine excludes.  

- The defense’s argument—that “adjudication would require this Court to evaluate whether Argentina’s expropriation was valid”—is a red herring. Courts routinely enforce contracts against sovereigns without questioning the validity of their sovereign acts. For example, in *Sabbatino*, the Court declined to adjudicate claims challenging Cuba’s expropriation of property *but* explicitly preserved claims based on “contractual rights between private parties.” *Id.* at 428. Here, the bylaws are a private contract; Petersen’s claims seek to enforce them, not invalidate the expropriation.  

#### B. The Defense’s “Public Policy” Argument Fails  
The defense claims Argentina “could not contract away its sovereign power of expropriation” under Argentine law. This is irrelevant. The FSIA and act of state doctrine do not turn on whether a sovereign *could* contract away its powers; they turn on whether the sovereign *did* contract—and whether its breach of that contract is commercial. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614 (the inquiry is whether the conduct “is the type of action that could be taken by a private participant in the commercial sphere”). Argentina *did* contract away its ability to ignore the tender offer requirements when it amended the bylaws to attract U.S. investors. It cannot now claim sovereign immunity for breaching that contract.

## INTRODUCTION

Plaintiff's rebuttal mischaracterizes the nature of this dispute and attempts to obscure the fundamental reality that this case arises from Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. The district court erred in denying Defendants' motions to dismiss, and this Court should reverse. The commercial activity exception to the FSIA does not apply because the claims are fundamentally based on Argentina's sovereign exercise of its expropriation power, not on private commercial conduct. Similarly, the act of state doctrine bars adjudication because resolving these claims would require this Court to evaluate the validity and propriety of Argentina's sovereign acts under its own constitutional and legal framework.

---

## I. THE FSIA COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

### A. The Claims Arise from Sovereign Expropriation, Not Commercial Activity

Plaintiff erroneously conflates the sovereign act of expropriation with isolated commercial aspects of corporate governance. However, the Supreme Court has consistently held that "theFSIA's immunity extends to sovereign acts, not merely to sovereign's private-law activities." *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993). The critical inquiry is whether the *gravamen* of the complaint seeks relief for sovereign or commercial conduct.

In *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, the Supreme Court rejected a similar argument that the commercial activity exception applied where a foreign sovereign allegedly committed tortious acts in the course of commercial activity. The Court held that "the FSIA does not permit inquiry into the sovereign's motive" and that "the question is not whether the foreign state is acting with a profit motive or otherwise like a private person, but whether the particular act is one that private parties can perform." *Id.* at 360 (internal quotation marks omitted).

Here, the core of Plaintiff's claims arises from Argentina's exercise of its sovereign power of expropriation under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution and the General Expropriation Law. The YPF Expropriation Law specifically declared 51% of YPF's voting stock to be of "public use" and subject to expropriation. This is the quintessential sovereign act—a governmental taking of property for public benefit. *See Murr v. Wisconsin*, 137 S. Ct. 1933, 1939 (2017) (describing expropriation as "the governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights").

Plaintiff's attempt to distinguish the "manner" of expropriation from the expropriation itself fails. The tender offer provisions in the bylaws were designed to govern voluntary commercial acquisitions by private parties—not governmental exercises of the sovereign power of eminent domain. When Argentina exercised its constitutional right to expropriate property for public use, it was acting as a sovereign, not as a market participant. The fact that Argentina had previously agreed to certain procedural requirements in connection with a private IPO cannot transform a subsequent sovereign expropriation into commercial activity.

This Court recognized in *NML Capital, Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, 699 F.3d 246, 255 (2d Cir. 2012), that "a foreign sovereign's participation in a commercial contract triggers the exception"—but only where the sovereign is acting in its commercial capacity. Argentina's participation in the 1993 IPO was commercial; its 2012 exercise of the sovereign power of expropriation was not.

### B. The "Direct Effect" Requirement Is Not Satisfied

Plaintiff's reliance on attenuated economic effects in the United States fails to establish the "direct effect" required by 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The statute requires that the sovereign's act "cause a direct effect in the United States." The Supreme Court has interpreted this requirement strictly.

In *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992), the Court held that a "direct effect" requires "an immediate effect of the sovereign act in the United States." The Court emphasized that the "effects" must be "substantial" and "have a direct impact on the United States." *Id.*

Here, the alleged effects—cancellation of dividends, Petersen's default on loans, and foreclosure of ADRs—are too remote from Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. Argentina's expropriation of 51% of YPF from Repsol was effected under Argentine law, and any effects on Petersen arose from secondary consequences of that sovereign act. The fact that Petersen held ADRs and that some dividends might have been paid to U.S. investors does not transform the sovereign act of expropriation into commercial activity with direct effects in the United States.

As this Court noted in *NML Capital*, 699 F.3d at 255, the FSIA does not allow courts to "look through" sovereign acts to find commercial activity. The expropriation itself is the act upon which Plaintiff's claims are based, and that act was taken under Argentine law, not in the commercial sphere.

### C. YPF Cannot Be Held Liable for Complying with Argentine Law

YPF's actions in this case were taken in compliance with the YPF Expropriation Law, a valid exercise of Argentine sovereign authority. When a sovereign expropriates property, the target entity is obligated to comply with the sovereign's directives. Holding YPF liable for following Argentine law would effectively allow U.S. courts to dictate how Argentina must exercise its sovereign powers within its own territory.

*Jam v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 759 (2019), cited by Plaintiff, does not support liability here. In *Jam*, the Supreme Court held that a government instrumentality loses immunity when acting in a commercial capacity—but YPF was not acting in a commercial capacity when it complied with the expropriation law. YPF was acting as the target of a sovereign taking, following the directives of its sovereign owner. This is fundamentally different from a sovereign entity engaging in commercial activity like selling goods or issuing bonds.

---

## II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS ADJUDICATION

### A. Adjudication Would Require Questioning the Validity of Argentina's Sovereign Acts

The act of state doctrine provides that "courts will not inquire into the validity of the public acts a recognized sovereign power performs within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 421 (1964). While the doctrine has exceptions, it fundamentally reflects "the mutual deference owed by independent governments." *Id.* at 422.

Plaintiff's claims cannot be adjudicated without this Court necessarily evaluating whether Argentina's expropriation was properly conducted under Argentine law. The tender offer provisions in the bylaws, Plaintiff argues, required Argentina to make a tender offer before acquiring control. But the YPF Expropriation Law authorized Argentina to acquire 51% of YPF directly through expropriation—a sovereign act that does not require tender offers because it is an exercise of eminent domain, not a voluntary acquisition.

Adjudicating whether Argentina breached the bylaws would require this Court to determine whether the sovereign can be bound by private contractual provisions when exercising its constitutional power of expropriation. This necessarily involves questioning the "validity" of Argentina's sovereign act—the very inquiry the act of state doctrine prohibits.

### B. The "Contract Exception" to the Act of State Doctrine Does Not Apply

Plaintiff relies on the statement in *Sabbatino* that the act of state doctrine "is not applicable to claims based on contract or property rights." 376 U.S. at 428. However, this exception does not apply where, as here, the contract claims are inextricably intertwined with the sovereign act.

In *Sabbatino*, the Supreme Court distinguished between claims that "grow out of" or are "dependent upon" a sovereign act, which are barred, and claims that exist independently of such acts. The Court held that "the act of state doctrine is applicable to [a claim] which would require the court to pass upon the validity of the foreign sovereign act." *Id.* at 428.

Here, Plaintiff's claims are entirely dependent upon the sovereign act of expropriation. Without the expropriation, there would be no breach of the tender offer provisions, no cancellation of dividends, and no exercise of voting rights in contravention of section 7(h). The bylaws were drafted to govern voluntary commercial acquisitions; they were not intended to—and could not—bind Argentina's exercise of its sovereign expropriation power.

### C. Argentine Law Governs the Scope of Argentina's Sovereign Powers

The question of whether Argentina could "contract away" its sovereign power of expropriation is governed by Argentine law, not U.S. law. Under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution, the Argentine Congress has the authority to declare property subject to expropriation for public use. The General Expropriation Law establishes the procedures for expropriation, and the YPF Expropriation Law specifically declared YPF's shares to be of public use.

A U.S. court cannot adjudicate whether Argentina breached contractual obligations without necessarily determining whether those contractual obligations are valid under Argentine law. This would require this Court to interpret Argentine constitutional and statutory law—a quintessential act of state inquiry that the doctrine prohibits.

The commercial activity exception to the FSIA does not apply because Plaintiff's claims arise from Argentina's sovereign exercise of its expropriation power, not from commercial activity. The act of state doctrine bars adjudication because resolving these claims would require this Court to evaluate the validity of Argentina's sovereign acts under Argentine law.

**UPDATED RULING**

Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by Plaintiff-Appellee Petersen Energía Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen Energía, S.A.U. (together, "Petersen") and Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF"), and based solely on the facts and arguments provided in the record, the Court updates its preliminary ruling as follows.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA)**

Defendants appeal the district court's denial of their motion to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under the FSIA, 28 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq. They argue that the "gravamen" of Petersen's complaint is Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation, and thus the commercial activity exception of 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2) does not apply. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive and affirms the district court's ruling.

Under the FSIA, a foreign state is immune from the jurisdiction of United States courts unless a specific exception applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The commercial activity exception removes immunity where the action "is based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere that causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The Supreme Court has clarified that the determination of what constitutes "commercial activity" turns on the nature of the conduct rather than its purpose. In *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), the Court held that the relevant inquiry is whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) "are the type of actions that could be taken by a private participant in the commercial sphere." The defense's attempt to frame the "gravamen" of the complaint as the expropriation itself mischaracterizes the specific conduct upon which Petersen's claims are based. The complaint does not seek to challenge the validity of the expropriation decree or Argentina's right to nationalize YPF. Rather, it alleges a breach of the tender offer provisions contained in YPF's bylaws and the subsequent voting of shares and cancellation of dividends in contravention of those bylaws.

As argued by Plaintiff, these acts are "commercial" in nature because they involve the management and governance of a corporate entity in which U.S. citizens hold a substantial financial interest via American Depository Receipts (ADRs). The bylaws, including Sections 7 and 28, were amended as part of a privatization effort and an initial public offering (IPO) on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) to attract U.S. investors. Argentina's failure to comply with the tender offer requirements, and YPF's facilitation of Argentina's voting rights in contravention of Section 7(h), are actions that private corporations and shareholders routinely undertake. See *NML Capital, Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, 699 F.3d 246, 255 (2d Cir. 2012) (recognizing that a foreign sovereign's participation in a commercial contract triggers the exception). The fact that the underlying motive was renationalization does not transform the breach of these commercial contractual terms into a sovereign act immune from jurisdiction under the FSIA. *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993) (stating that the question is whether the particular act is one that private parties can perform).

Furthermore, the "direct effect" requirement is satisfied. The record shows that the cancellation of dividend payments precipitated Petersen's default on loans held by U.S. financial institutions and led to the foreclosure of ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. These are direct effects within the United States. See *First National City Bank v. Banco Para el Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 622 (1983) (holding that the "effects" of the sovereign's conduct must be felt in the United States). The defense's contention that these effects are too remote ignores the immediate financial impact of the dividend cancellation on Petersen's solvency and the subsequent transfer of title to the ADRs in New York.

Finally, YPF is not entitled to immunity as an instrumentality. YPF, as a corporation with publicly traded shares on the NYSE, engaged in commercial activity when it facilitated Argentina's breach of the bylaws. YPF's duty to enforce section 7(h)—which stripped Argentina of voting rights for non-compliant acquisitions—was a commercial obligation arising from its corporate governance contract. When YPF canceled dividend payments and permitted Argentina to vote shares in violation of section 7(h), it acted as a commercial entity, not a sovereign instrumentality. *Jam v. International Finance Corp.*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 766 (2019) (holding that instrumentalities lose immunity when acting in commercial capacities).

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

Defendants further argue that the act of state doctrine bars Petersen's claims because adjudication would require this Court to pass judgment on the validity of Argentina's sovereign expropriation under its own laws. The Court disagrees and dismisses the appeal on this issue.

The act of state doctrine is "a principle of comity under which courts decline to question the validity of a foreign government's official acts within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964). However, the doctrine is not absolute. As the Supreme Court stated in *Sabbatino*, the doctrine "is not applicable to claims based on contract or property rights." *Id.* at 428.

Petersen's claims are founded squarely on breach of contract. The complaint alleges that Argentina and YPF violated specific provisions of the YPF bylaws—a private corporate governance contract—by failing to make a tender offer and by exercising voting rights in contravention of Section 7(h). To adjudicate these claims, the Court need not declare the YPF Expropriation Law invalid or question Argentina's sovereign right to expropriate property for public use under Article 17 of its Constitution. Rather, the Court need only determine whether the parties' commercial agreement (the bylaws) was breached. The enforcement of a contractual promise does not inherently constitute a challenge to the public acts of the foreign sovereign.

Defendants contend that enforcing the bylaws would effectively invalidate the expropriation law by conditioning the sovereign's exercise of power on private terms. However, this argument conflates the validity of the expropriation with the remedies for a breach of contract. The expropriation may stand as a valid act of state, but that does not automatically absolve the sovereign of contractual liabilities incurred in connection with the commercial management of the enterprise prior to or during the expropriation. Because Petersen seeks to enforce private contractual rights rather than attack the validity of the public expropriation, the act of state doctrine does not bar this action.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the commercial activity exception to the FSIA applies, and the act of state doctrine does not preclude adjudication of Plaintiff's breach of contract claims. The district court's order denying the motions to dismiss is affirmed insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act, and defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine is dismissed.

**CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR PLAINTIFF-APPELLEE**  

Your Honor, this case is not about whether Argentina could lawfully expropriate YPF under its own constitutional framework. It is not about the validity of Argentina’s sovereign power to seize property for public use. It is about a simple, straightforward question: Did Argentina and YPF breach their binding commercial contractual obligations to Petersen? The answer is unequivocally yes—and this Court must affirm the district court’s denial of dismissal because these claims fall squarely within the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act’s (FSIA) commercial activity exception and are not barred by the act of state doctrine.  

### **I. The FSIA Commercial Activity Exception Applies Because the Claims Are Based on Commercial Conduct**  

The defendants’ entire argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the nature of these claims. They assert that the “gravamen” of Petersen’s complaint is Argentina’s sovereign expropriation, but that is categorically false. As the Supreme Court has consistently held, the FSIA’s inquiry is not whether the sovereign’s *motive* was commercial or political—it is whether the *specific conduct at issue* is the type of action a private party could undertake. *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993). Here, the defendants’ alleged conduct—failing to make a tender offer, violating voting restrictions, and canceling dividends in contravention of YPF’s bylaws—is quintessentially commercial.  

The YPF bylaws were not some abstract political agreement. They were carefully drafted contractual provisions embedded in YPF’s IPO prospectus, filed with the SEC and listed on the New York Stock Exchange. The prospectus explicitly stated that “[u]nder [YPF’s] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF’s] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws.” (App. 23). This is precisely the type of commercial commitment a private corporation would make to attract investors—especially U.S. investors who purchased American Depository Receipts (ADRs) on the NYSE. When Argentina agreed to these bylaws during the 1993 privatization, it entered into a commercial contract with global investors, including Petersen.  

The defendants now argue that these bylaws only apply to “voluntary commercial acquisitions” by private parties, not sovereign expropriation. That is a red herring. The Supreme Court rejected precisely this distinction in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, where Argentina’s issuance of bonds—though motivated by sovereign policy—was still commercial activity because “the particular actions [it] perform[s]... are the type of actions that could be taken by a private participant in the commercial sphere.” 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). Here, the tender offer requirement and voting restrictions are standard corporate governance tools used by *private corporations worldwide* to protect minority shareholders. No sovereign power could “unilaterally rewrite” these terms after the fact without breaching its commercial obligations. As this Court held in *NML Capital, Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, a foreign sovereign’s participation in a commercial contract—like YPF’s IPO—triggers the FSIA exception. 699 F.3d 246, 255 (2d Cir. 2012).  

The defendants’ claim that Argentina “was acting as a sovereign, not as a market participant” when it expropriated Repsol’s shares ignores the reality of the situation. Argentina did not invoke its sovereign power to *override* the bylaws—it *breached* them. The YPF Expropriation Law did not say, “Argentina may ignore its tender offer obligations.” It merely declared the shares “of public use.” But the bylaws—agreed to by Argentina during the IPO—still governed *how* that acquisition must be executed. The fact that Argentina chose to expropriate does not transform the *manner* of that expropriation into a sovereign act immune from judicial review. As *Nelson* makes clear, the FSIA does not permit courts to “look through” sovereign acts to find commercial activity, but it also does not allow sovereigns to shield commercial breaches behind the veil of sovereignty.  

### **II. The “Direct Effect” Requirement Is Satisfied**  

The defendants further argue that Petersen’s alleged injuries—dividend cancellations, loan defaults, and ADR foreclosures—are too remote to satisfy the FSIA’s “direct effect” requirement. That is demonstrably incorrect. The Supreme Court has held that a “direct effect” requires an “immediate effect of the sovereign act in the United States.” *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. Here, the facts are clear: Argentina’s cancellation of dividend payments directly caused Petersen—whose shares were held in New York via ADRs—to default on loans from U.S. financial institutions, leading to the foreclosure of those ADRs by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. This is not a “secondary consequence” of expropriation; it is the *immediate financial impact* of the defendants’ breach of contract on U.S. investors.  

As *First National City Bank v. Banco Para el Comercio Exterior de Cuba* holds, the “effects” of a sovereign’s conduct need only be “felt in the United States” to satisfy FSIA jurisdiction. 462 U.S. 611, 622 (1983). The defendants’ attempt to recharacterize this as “attenuated” ignores the chain of causation: Argentina’s violation of the bylaws caused dividend cancellations, which caused Petersen’s default, which caused the foreclosure of ADRs in New York. That is the very definition of a “direct effect.”  

### **III. YPF’s Actions Were Commercial, Not Sovereign**  

The defendants also claim YPF was “complying with Argentine law” when it canceled dividends and permitted Argentina to vote shares in violation of Section 7(h). That is a fatal misstep. YPF is not a sovereign entity; it is a publicly traded corporation with shares listed on the NYSE. When YPF canceled dividends and allowed Argentina to exercise voting rights in breach of the bylaws, it acted in its capacity as a commercial entity—not as an instrument of the Argentine government. As the Supreme Court held in *Jam v. International Finance Corp.*, a government instrumentality loses FSIA immunity when it “acts in commercial capacities.” 139 S. Ct. 759, 766 (2019). YPF’s duty to enforce Section 7(h)—which stripped Argentina of voting rights for non-compliant acquisitions—was a commercial obligation arising from its corporate governance contract with shareholders. By ignoring that duty, YPF did not “comply with Argentine law”; it breached its own commercial promises to U.S. investors.  

### **IV. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Bar This Case**  

Finally, the defendants claim the act of state doctrine requires dismissal because adjudicating this case would require the Court to “question the validity of Argentina’s sovereign acts.” That is simply not true. As the Supreme Court made clear in *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, the act of state doctrine “is not applicable to claims based on contract or property rights.” 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). Petersen is not challenging Argentina’s right to expropriate property under Article 17 of the Argentine Constitution. It is enforcing a private contractual agreement—YPF’s bylaws—that Argentina itself voluntarily adopted during the IPO.  

The defendants’ argument conflates two entirely separate legal questions: (1) whether the expropriation was valid under Argentine law, and (2) whether Argentina breached its contractual obligations to Petersen. The district court need not—and *cannot*—rule on the first question. It need only determine whether Argentina and YPF failed to follow the tender offer procedures and voting restrictions they promised to uphold in the prospectus. As *Sabbatino* explains, the act of state doctrine bars courts from “passing upon the validity of a foreign government’s official acts,” but it does not shield sovereigns from liability for breaching their own commercial contracts. 376 U.S. at 428.  

To hold otherwise would be to say that any time a sovereign enters into a commercial contract, it can later invoke its sovereign power to disregard that contract without consequence. That is not the law.  

### **CONCLUSION**  

Your Honor, this case is about the rule of law in commercial transactions. Argentina agreed to contractual terms designed to protect U.S. investors during YPF’s privatization. It then breached those terms. YPF, as a commercial entity, facilitated that breach. The injuries to Petersen—defaulted loans, foreclosed ADRs, and lost dividends—were direct and immediate in the United States. The FSIA exception applies, and the act of state doctrine does not.  

The defendants seek to hide behind the shield of sovereignty, but they cannot. When they entered into a commercial agreement with U.S. investors, they waived any claim to immunity for breaching that agreement. This Court must affirm the district court’s denial of dismissal and hold them accountable.

### I. THE FSIA COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

#### A. The Gravamen of Petersen's Complaint Is Argentina's Sovereign Expropriation

The plaintiff mischaracterizes the nature of this action by arguing that the "gravamen" of the complaint concerns merely commercial conduct—specifically, the alleged breach of tender offer provisions. This argument fails because the entire factual predicate for Petersen's claims is Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. Without the expropriation, there would be no claim.

As the Supreme Court held in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992), the FSIA requires courts to look at the "particular act" upon which the claim is based. Here, Petersen cannot plausibly dispute that Argentina's enactment of Law 26,741 (the YPF Expropriation Law) and its seizure of 51% of YPF's voting stock from Repsol constitutes the fundamental act giving rise to this litigation. The plaintiff may attempt to dress its claim in the language of contract, but the substance of the complaint challenges the consequences of sovereign expropriation.

The plaintiff's reliance on *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993), is misplaced. In *Nelson*, the plaintiffs alleged specific tortious conduct—false imprisonment and assault—that was entirely independent of any sovereign act. Here, by contrast, the only reason Argentina failed to make a tender offer was because it acquired shares through expropriation, not commercial negotiation. The expropriation and the alleged breach of the bylaws are inextricably linked; one cannot be separated from the other.

#### B. Argentina's Conduct Was Not Commercial Activity

The plaintiff argues that failing to make a tender offer and voting shares are "commercial" acts because private corporations engage in such conduct. This argument misapplies the FSIA framework. The critical inquiry is not whether the act is one that *could* be performed by a private party, but whether the *particular act* at issue is commercial in nature. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614.

Argentina's acquisition of 51% of YPF from Repsol was accomplished through the YPF Expropriation Law, enacted pursuant to Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution and the General Expropriation Law. Republic of Argentina*, 699 F.3d 246, 255 (2d Cir. 2012), this Circuit recognized that a foreign sovereign's participation in a commercial contract triggers the FSIA exception. However, that case involved Argentina's voluntary commercial borrowing—conduct entirely distinct from the compulsory taking of property under domestic expropriation law. The present case more closely resembles situations where sovereign acts, even if having commercial consequences, remain immune from suit.

### II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS THIS ACTION

The plaintiff argues that the act of state doctrine does not apply because this case involves only breach of contract. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the doctrine. The act of state doctrine requires courts to decline adjudication when doing so would "require the court to judge the validity of a foreign sovereign's public acts." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964).

Here, adjudication of Petersen's claims would require this Court to determine whether Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares must comply with the tender offer provisions in YPF's bylaws. Such a determination would necessarily involve judging the validity and scope of Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. The plaintiff asks this Court to declare that Argentina violated its own bylaws by failing to make a tender offer—but this is precisely the type of judgment that the act of state doctrine prohibits.

The plaintiff's argument that *Sabbatino* excepts "claims based on contract or property rights" misreads that decision. In *Sabbatino*, the Court held that the act of state doctrine applies even to property claims when adjudication would require questioning the validity of the foreign sovereign's act. 376 U.S. at 428. The exception the plaintiff invokes applies only when the claim arises from a purely private commercial transaction—not when, as here, the claim is predicated on a sovereign taking.

#### B. The Bylaws Cannot Constrain Sovereign Power

The plaintiff asks this Court to enforce the YPF bylaws against Argentina, arguing that Argentina voluntarily agreed to these provisions during the 1993 IPO. However, no sovereign nation can, consistent with its own constitutional framework, prospectively limit its exercise of the power of eminent domain through private corporate bylaws. The Argentine Constitution grants Argentina the authority to expropriate property for public use upon payment of just compensation. Article 17 cannot be amended, let alone restricted, by corporate governance documents.

To accept the plaintiff's argument would be to hold that Argentina, by participating in a privatization, somehow waived its sovereign power to later expropriate property in the public interest. This Court should decline to impose such a limitation on a foreign sovereign's constitutional authority.

### III. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff argues that Argentina's conduct is "commercial" because it involves the "management and governance of a corporate entity." This argument proves too much. The representations in the prospectus must be read in the context of Argentine law, which expressly contemplates expropriation.

The plaintiff argues that enforcement of the bylaws would not "invalidate" the expropriation. This argument is semantically clever but legally incorrect. **FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by Plaintiff-Appellee Petersen Energía Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen Energía, S.A.U. (together, "Petersen") and Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF"), and based solely on the record and the applicable law, the Court affirms the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA") and dismisses defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

**I. Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act**

Defendants argue that the district court erred in denying their motion to dismiss for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under the FSIA, 28 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq. They contend that the "gravamen" of Petersen's complaint is Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation, which falls outside the commercial activity exception of 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). We disagree.

**A. Commercial Activity**

Under the FSIA, a foreign state is presumptively immune from the jurisdiction of United States courts unless an exception applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The commercial activity exception provides that a foreign state is not immune in any action "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere that causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The Supreme Court has instructed that the determination of what constitutes "commercial activity" turns on the nature of the conduct rather than its motive. In *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), the Court held that the relevant inquiry is whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs "are the type of actions that could be taken by a private participant in the commercial sphere." Similarly, in *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 360 (1993), the Court clarified that "the question is not whether the sovereign was acting for a commercial purpose, but whether the particular acts that the foreign sovereign performs are the type of acts that can be done by private parties."

Defendants urge the Court to look past the specific conduct alleged—failure to make a tender offer, voting shares in violation of bylaws, and canceling dividend payments—and focus instead on the expropriation itself. While the expropriation provided the context for these actions, the claims are "based upon" the specific breaches of the YPF bylaws. The bylaws, which were part of a commercial IPO and SEC filing, governed the commercial management of the corporation. Private corporations routinely engage in tender offers, vote shares, and cancel dividends. Therefore, Argentina's failure to comply with these provisions constitutes commercial activity under the FSIA. See *NML Capital, Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, 699 F.3d 246, 255 (2d Cir. 2012) (recognizing that a foreign sovereign's participation in a commercial contract triggers the exception).

Defendants' argument that the bylaws cannot bind the sovereign power of eminent domain is a substantive defense to the breach of contract claim, not a jurisdictional bar. At this stage, we must accept the well-pleaded factual allegations in the complaint as true. The complaint alleges that Argentina agreed to the bylaws and then breached them. Whether the expropriation law effectively nullified that agreement is a matter for the merits, not a basis to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

**B. Direct Effect in the United States**

The commercial activity exception also requires that the defendant's commercial act cause a "direct effect" in the United States. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. The record demonstrates that the cancellation of dividend payments directly caused Petersen to default on its loans, which were held by U.S. financial institutions. This default led to the foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs, which were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. This chain of causation is sufficiently direct to satisfy the statute. The financial injury was felt immediately in the United States due to the interruption of payments to U.S. investors and the transfer of title to securities located in New York. See *First National City Bank v. Banco Para el Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 622 (1983) (holding that the "effects" of the sovereign's conduct must be felt in the United States).

**C. YPF's Status**

Finally, YPF argues it is entitled to immunity as an instrumentality of Argentina. However, under the FSIA, an instrumentality is immune only for acts "outside the activity's commercial character." 28 U.S.C. § 1603(b). YPF's actions—canceling dividends and permitting Argentina to vote shares in contravention of the bylaws—are commercial acts of corporate governance. As the Supreme Court noted in *Jam v. International Finance Corp.*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 766 (2019), instrumentalities lose immunity when they act in commercial capacities. Because YPF's conduct at issue is commercial, it is not entitled to immunity.

**II. Act of State Doctrine**

Defendants next argue that the act of state doctrine bars Petersen's claims. The act of state doctrine is "a principle of comity under which courts decline to question the validity of a foreign government's official acts within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964).

Defendants contend that adjudicating Petersen's breach of contract claims would require this Court to sit in judgment on the validity of Argentina's expropriation under its own laws. We find this argument unpersuasive. As the Supreme Court stated in *Sabbatino*, the doctrine "is not applicable to claims based on contract or property rights." *Id.* at 428.

Petersen's claims are founded on the YPF bylaws, a private corporate contract. To resolve this dispute, the Court need not declare the YPF Expropriation Law invalid or determine whether Argentina had the constitutional authority to expropriate the shares. Rather, the Court need only determine whether the defendants breached the contractual obligations set forth in the bylaws regarding tender offers and voting rights. The validity of the expropriation itself is distinct from the question of whether the defendants complied with the contractual procedures governing the acquisition and voting of shares. Because Petersen seeks to enforce private contractual rights rather than attack the validity of the public expropriation, the act of state doctrine does not bar this action.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the commercial activity exception to the FSIA applies, and the act of state doctrine does not preclude adjudication of Plaintiff's breach of contract claims. The district court's order denying the motions to dismiss is affirmed insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act, and defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine is dismissed.
