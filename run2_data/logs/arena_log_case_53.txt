Your Honors, the Director committed reversible legal error by misapplying the Flor test to this case. This is not a claim for a new disease "resulting from the nature of employment" but rather an aggravation of a pre-existing medical condition—Van Ness's mild persistent asthma. Under Hawaii law, the Flor test applies *only* to injuries by disease that "result from the nature of the employment," while claims for aggravation of pre-existing conditions are governed by the unitary test for proximate causation. The Director's error compels reversal.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 120 Haw. 308, 204 P.3d 1126 (2009), this court explicitly limited the Flor test to cases where the disease itself "arises from the nature of the employment." The court stated: "The Flor test applies when the claimant seeks compensation for a disease that is directly caused by the employment, rather than an aggravation of a pre-existing condition." Id. at 319, 204 P.3d at 1137. Crucially, *Flor* distinguishes between "diseases resulting from the nature of employment" (subject to Flor) and "aggravation of pre-existing conditions" (subject to the unitary test). Here, Van Ness's asthma was pre-existing and controlled for years—his claim centers on the *aggravation* of that condition due to workplace exposures, not the development of a new disease. Thus, the unitary test, not Flor, controls.

The unitary test requires only that the employment be a proximate cause of the aggravation. As confirmed in *Kama v. City & County of Honolulu*, 122 Haw. 429, 228 P.3d 371 (2010), "when a pre-existing condition is aggravated by employment, the unitary test applies: the claimant must show that the employment contributed to the aggravation." Id. at 441, 228 P.3d at 383. Under this standard, uniqueness of the hazard is irrelevant—only whether the employment conditions proximately caused the worsening. The Director erred by applying Flor's "nature of employment" standard and crediting Dr. Arora's irrelevant conclusion that "vog is not a factor associated with school uniquely." That analysis misstates the law: under the unitary test for aggravation, the hazard need not be unique to the workplace; it need only be a proximate cause of the aggravation.

The record establishes proximate causation under the unitary test. Van Ness's symptoms worsened *only* during his work at Lahainaluna: he required frequent inhaler refills, experienced severe coughing and wheezing during physical exertion on the steep campus, and had documented exacerbations during vog events. Crucially, his symptoms improved significantly when removed from the workplace environment—during a trip to Pennsylvania, his symptoms "essentially resolved," and after his transfer to Oahu, he "was relatively free of respiratory symptoms" and "rarely had need for albuterol." Dr. Mathison confirmed this causal link, diagnosing "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and stating that volcanic pollutants are a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients." This evidence satisfies the unitary test: the employment conditions—specifically the workplace vog exposure combined with the physical demands of climbing stairs in non-air-conditioned buildings with open windows—proximately caused the aggravation.

The Director's error is fundamental. By applying Flor to an aggravation claim, the Director imposed an incorrect legal standard that required Van Ness to prove vog was "unique" to his job—a standard expressly rejected by *Kama* and *Flor* for such claims. Under the correct unitary test, the Director's decision must be reversed. We request this court hold that Van Ness's asthma aggravation was proximately caused by his employment and remand for further proceedings consistent with this law.

## LEGAL ANALYSIS

### I. THE DIRECTOR CORRECTLY DETERMINED THAT THE CLAIMANT'S INJURY DID NOT ARISE OUT OF AND IN THE COURSE OF EMPLOYMENT

The Director's determination that the Claimant's respiratory condition was not work-related should be affirmed. Regardless of whether the *Flor* test or the unitary test applies, the evidence presented was insufficient to establish that the Claimant's employment was a proximate cause of his asthma aggravation.

### A. The Applicable Legal Standard

Hawaii workers' compensation law provides benefits for injuries "arising out of and in the course of employment." The courts have established two distinct analytical frameworks for determining compensability: the *Flor* test for diseases "resulting from the nature of employment" and the unitary test for aggravation of pre-existing conditions.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 120 Haw. 308, 204 P.3d 1126 (2009), the Hawaii Supreme Court established a test for determining whether a disease is compensable when the disease itself arises from the nature of the employment. Under *Flor*, a claimant must prove: (1) a disease or condition; (2) that is unique to or characteristic of the particular employment; and (3) a causal connection between the employment and the disease. *Id.* at 319, 204 P.3d at 1137.

For claims involving aggravation of pre-existing conditions, the unitary test applies. Under this test, the claimant must demonstrate that the employment "contributed to" or was a "proximate cause" of the aggravation. *See Kama v. City & County of Honolulu*, 122 Haw. 429, 441, 228 P.3d 371, 383 (2010).

### B. The Evidence Fails to Establish Proximate Causation Under Either Test

#### 1. Vog Was a General Environmental Condition, Not a Workplace Hazard

The Claimant's theory of compensability rests on his exposure to vog at Lahainaluna High School. However, the record establishes that vog was a general environmental condition affecting all of Maui, not a hazard unique to the Claimant's employment or workplace.

Van Ness testified that there was a "significant amount of vog on Maui from late October 2005 through April 2006." He described severe vog days when he was unable to see the administrative building "halfway down the campus" from the library. However, vog is a well-known environmental phenomenon resulting from volcanic emissions from the Kilauea volcano, which affects the entire island of Maui—and frequently other Hawaiian islands—during certain weather conditions.

The Claimant's own testimony demonstrates that his exposure to vog was not limited to the workplace. He lived on Maui, drove his car on Maui roads, and was exposed to vog whenever he was outdoors. His home in Kihei was air-conditioned with HEPA filters, which provided some protection, but he acknowledged spending "little time outside" while living on Maui "other than when he was at work."

Critically, Dr. Arora noted in his IME report that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." This observation, while characterized by the Claimant as "irrelevant," actually goes to the heart of the compensability analysis: whether the employment created a heightened risk beyond the general risks faced by all residents of Maui.

#### 2. The Claimant's Symptoms Improved Away From Work

The evidence demonstrates that the Claimant's symptoms improved significantly whenever he was removed from the general Maui environment—not specifically the workplace. During a trip to Pennsylvania in March 2006, Van Ness's symptoms "essentially resolved." He testified: "[W]hile in Philadelphia he was on prednisone. His wheezing and [shortness of breath] essentially resolved."

This improvement occurred despite the fact that Van Ness was still required to engage in physical activity during his trip. The fact that his symptoms resolved in Pennsylvania—far from any vog exposure—suggests that his condition was caused by general environmental factors on Maui, not specifically by his employment.

Similarly, after his transfer to Oahu in November 2006, Van Ness "has been relatively free of respiratory symptoms. He ... rarely has had need for albuterol and has not regularly taken the Advair." This improvement occurred after his employment with the DOE continued on Oahu, demonstrating that his respiratory issues were caused by his residence on Maui, not by his employment.

#### 3. The Physical Demands of the Job Were Not Unique to Employment

The Claimant also attributes his condition to the physical demands of navigating the steep Lahainaluna campus—specifically, climbing stairs between buildings. However, these physical demands existed regardless of vog exposure and were not unique to the Claimant's employment.

The evidence demonstrates that the Claimant's difficulty climbing stairs was due to his pre-existing asthma condition combined with the general environmental vog exposure. The physical exertion of climbing stairs would have caused breathing difficulties for any person with asthma, particularly in the presence of vog. The Claimant has not established that the physical demands of the job created a unique or heightened risk beyond what any person with asthma would face when climbing stairs in a voggy environment.

### C. The Director Properly Evaluated the Medical Evidence

The Director credited the report of Dr. Arora, who concluded that "the legal implications of [vog exposure] are not clear" because "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." While the Claimant characterizes this as an "irrelevant" conclusion, Dr. Arora's observation accurately reflects the legal standard for compensability.

Dr. Arora conducted an Independent Medical Examination on January 25, 2008, and noted that he did not have access to the Claimant's medical records at the time of his initial evaluation. He appropriately concluded that "it [would] be unwise to address the [DOE's] referral questions" until he had access to the relevant records.

Dr. Mathison's report, prepared on February 14, 2008, noted that Van Ness had "exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and recommended "avoidances" of volcanic products. However, Dr. Mathison did not conclude that the Claimant's employment specifically caused his condition. Rather, he noted that volcanic pollution is a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients" generally—not a risk unique to the workplace.

The Director's decision to credit Dr. Arora's analysis was within the Director's discretion as the finder of fact. The Director was not required to credit Dr. Mathison's conclusions, particularly where Dr. Mathison's report did not specifically address whether the Claimant's employment created a unique or heightened risk beyond general environmental exposure.

## REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

### I. THE PLAINTIFF MISCHARACTERIZES THE NATURE OF THE CLAIM

The Plaintiff argues that this is "not a claim for a new disease 'resulting from the nature of employment'" but rather an "aggravation of a pre-existing medical condition." This characterization, while technically accurate, does not resolve the legal analysis in the Plaintiff's favor.

### II. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON *KAMA* IS MISPLACED

The Plaintiff cites *Kama v. City & County of Honolulu*, 122 Haw. 429, 228 P.3d 371 (2010), for the proposition that "uniqueness of the hazard is irrelevant" under the unitary test. This argument misreads the *Kama* decision.

The court held that "when a pre-existing condition is aggravated by employment, the unitary test applies: the claimant must show that the employment contributed to the aggravation." *Id.* at 441, 228 P.3d at 383.

However, *Kama* does not stand for the proposition that any employment contribution to a pre-existing condition is sufficient for compensability. The employment contribution must be significant and proximate. Here, the evidence demonstrates that the Claimant's employment did not create a unique or heightened risk—the vog exposure was general environmental, and the physical demands of the job were not unusual for any employee required to navigate a steep campus.

Moreover, the *Kama* court specifically noted that the unitary test requires that the employment be "a" proximate cause—not merely "a" contributing factor. The court explained that "the test is whether the employment was a proximate cause of the injury or death." *Id.* at 440, 228 P.3d at 382.

Here, the evidence demonstrates that the Claimant's asthma was aggravated by general environmental vog exposure on Maui—not specifically by his employment. **I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the court on appeal from the Director of the State of Hawai‘i Department of Labor and Industrial Relations’ decision denying Claimant-Appellant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. **II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director Erred by Applying the *Flor* Test to an Aggravation Claim**

The Director denied Van Ness’s claim, finding that his "respiratory problem and aggravation of his asthma condition was not work related." In reaching this conclusion, the Director explicitly credited the report of Dr. Arora, who observed that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." By relying on the lack of a "unique" hazard to deny the claim, the Director applied the legal standard established in *Flor v. Holguin*, 120 Haw. 308, 204 P.3d 1126 (2009).

Under *Flor*, a claimant seeking compensation for a disease must demonstrate that the disease "result[s] from the nature of the employment," which requires proof that the risk or hazard is unique to the particular employment. *Id.* at 319, 204 P.3d at 1137. However, the *Flor* test is inapplicable to the present case. Van Ness does not claim a new disease arising from the nature of his employment; rather, he claims the aggravation of a pre-existing condition—mild persistent asthma.

As established in *Kama v. City & County of Honolulu*, 122 Haw. 429, 228 P.3d 371 (2010), when a claim involves the aggravation of a pre-existing condition, the "unitary test" governs. *Id.* at 441, 228 P.3d at 383. Under the unitary test, the claimant need only show that the employment "contributed to" or was a "proximate cause" of the aggravation. *Id.* The requirement that the hazard be "unique" to the employment is irrelevant in the context of an aggravation claim. *Id.*

The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s statement regarding the lack of unique vog exposure at the school demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of the applicable law. By requiring Van Ness to prove that vog was unique to his employment, the Director imposed a burden appropriate only for a *Flor* claim, not an aggravation claim governed by *Kama*. This constitutes reversible legal error.

**B. The Evidence Supports a Finding of Compensability Under the Unitary Test**

Applying the correct legal standard—the unitary test for proximate cause—the record supports a finding that Van Ness’s employment was a proximate cause of the aggravation of his asthma.

To establish proximate cause under the unitary test, Van Ness must show that his employment conditions contributed to his respiratory deterioration. *Kama*, 122 Haw. at 441, 228 P.3d at 383. The record reveals a significant distinction between the environmental conditions at Van Ness’s workplace and his home environment. While Van Ness lived in an air-conditioned home in Kihei equipped with HEPA filters, his workplace at Lahainaluna High School was characterized by non-air-conditioned buildings with open windows, significant dust accumulation in computer equipment, and a steep terrain requiring him to repeatedly climb stairs and exert physical effort.

Van Ness testified that his symptoms worsened specifically during the workday, requiring him to stop and catch his breath while climbing the stairs to his office. Furthermore, Dr. Mathison’s consultation report confirms that Van Ness experienced "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" during the period of vog exposure and noted that volcanic pollutants are a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients."

The Defense argues that vog was a general environmental condition affecting the entire island and that Van Ness’s symptoms improved when he left Maui, suggesting the general environment, not the employment, was the cause. However, this argument ignores the differential exposure inherent in Van Ness’s employment. The physical exertion required to navigate the steep campus, combined with prolonged exposure in non-air-conditioned, dusty classrooms during a vog event, distinguishes his work exposure from his exposure at home in his filtered, air-conditioned residence. Under the unitary test, the employment need not be the sole cause, nor must the risk be unique; it need only be a contributing proximate factor. *Kama*, 122 Haw. at 441, 228 P.3d at 383. The evidence that Van Ness’s respiratory distress was significantly triggered by the physical demands and environmental conditions of his job satisfies this standard.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Director’s decision is based on an erroneous application of the *Flor* test to a claim that is governed by the unitary test for aggravation of a pre-existing condition. Because the Director applied the wrong legal standard, the decision cannot stand. The court finds that the evidence in the record is sufficient to establish that Van Ness’s employment was a proximate cause of the aggravation of his asthma under the unitary test.

The Director denied Van Ness’s claim, finding that his "respiratory problem and aggravation of his asthma condition was not work related." In reaching this conclusion, the Director explicitly credited the report of Dr. Arora, who observed that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." Under *Flor*, a claimant seeking compensation for a disease must demonstrate that the disease "result[s] from the nature of the employment," which requires proof that the risk or hazard is unique to the particular employment. *Id.* at 319, 204 P.3d at 1137. However, the *Flor* test is inapplicable to the present case. Under the unitary test, the claimant need only show that the employment "contributed to" or was a "proximate cause" of the aggravation. *Id.* The requirement that the hazard be "unique" to the employment is irrelevant in the context of an aggravation claim. *Id.*

The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s statement regarding the lack of unique vog exposure at the school demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of the applicable law. By requiring Van Ness to prove that vog was unique to his employment, the Director imposed a burden appropriate only for a *Flor* claim, not an aggravation claim governed by *Kama*. The Defense argues that the Director’s error was harmless because the evidence is insufficient to establish that Van Ness’s employment was a "substantial factor" in causing his aggravation under the unitary test. The Defense contends that vog was a general environmental condition affecting the entire island of Maui and that Van Ness’s symptoms improved when he left Maui, regardless of his employment status. While the Defense raises factual arguments that may be relevant to the unitary test, the error was not harmless because the Director failed to make any findings of fact regarding the unitary test.

The Director’s decision was based exclusively on the *Flor* "unique hazard" analysis. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the State of Hawai‘i Department of Labor and Industrial Relations denying Claimant-Appellant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Director denied the claim, concluding that Van Ness’s respiratory aggravation was not work-related because the vog exposure was not unique to his employment. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both the Prosecution and the Defense, the court affirms its preliminary finding that the Director committed reversible error by applying the incorrect legal standard. The Defense’s assertion that the evidence is insufficient to establish compensability under the correct standard is rejected because the Director failed to make the necessary factual findings under that standard. Accordingly, the Director’s decision is reversed and the matter is remanded.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director Erred by Applying the *Flor* Test**

The parties agree, and the record confirms, that Van Ness’s claim is for the aggravation of a pre-existing condition—mild persistent asthma—rather than for a new disease. The Director, however, applied the standard set forth in *Flor v. Holguin*, 120 Haw. 308, 204 P.3d 1126 (2009). Under *Flor*, a claimant must prove that the disease "result[s] from the nature of the employment" and that the risk is "unique to the particular employment." *Id.* at 319, 204 P.3d at 1137.

As established in *Kama v. City & County of Honolulu*, 122 Haw. 429, 228 P.3d 371 (2010), the *Flor* test is inapplicable to aggravation claims. For such claims, the "unitary test" governs. *Id.* at 441, 228 P.3d at 383. Under the unitary test, the claimant need only show that the employment "contributed to" or was a "proximate cause" of the aggravation. *Id.* The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s statement that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" demonstrates a direct application of the *Flor* standard. This was legal error.

**B. The Error Was Not Harmless**

The Defense contends that even if the Director applied the wrong standard, the error was harmless because the evidence is insufficient to establish that Van Ness’s employment was a "substantial factor" in causing his aggravation under the unitary test. The court disagrees.

The Defense argues that vog was a general environmental condition affecting the entire island of Maui and that Van Ness’s symptoms improved when he left the island, regardless of his employment status. While these are relevant factual considerations, they do not render the Director’s legal error harmless. The Director did not weigh these facts under the unitary test; rather, the Director denied the claim solely on the basis that the hazard was not unique to the employment.

The record contains evidence that, if credited, could support a finding that the employment was a substantial factor in the aggravation. Van Ness testified to specific physical demands of his job—climbing steep stairs and working in non-air-conditioned, dusty environments—that differed significantly from his home environment, which was air-conditioned and equipped with HEPA filters. He testified that his symptoms worsened specifically during the workday due to the exertion required by his job duties. Dr. Mathison’s report corroborated that Van Ness experienced a "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" during the period of vog exposure and noted that volcanic pollutants are a "well-recognized risk factor."

The question of whether the combination of vog exposure and the physical demands of the job constituted a "substantial factor" in aggravating Van Ness’s asthma is a factual determination for the Director. Because the Director failed to analyze the evidence under the correct legal standard, the court cannot conclude that the Director would have necessarily found the evidence insufficient. The Defense’s arguments regarding the general nature of vog are factual contentions that must be evaluated by the Director in the context of the unitary test.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Director’s decision was based on an erroneous application of the *Flor* test to a claim governed by the unitary test. The Defense has not demonstrated that this error was harmless, as the Director failed to make findings of fact regarding whether the employment contributed to the aggravation. The court cannot affirm the decision on the ground that the evidence was insufficient, as that determination was never made by the Director under the correct legal framework.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim is **REVERSED**, and the matter is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. On remand, the Director shall apply the unitary test established in *Kama* to determine whether Van Ness’s employment was a substantial factor in the aggravation of his pre-existing asthma.
