FACTS
In July 2005, appellant Stacy Juetten went to the LasikPlus Vision Center in Edina for a preoperative assessment to determine if she was suited for vision-correcting eye surgery. Juetten’s medical history indicated that she was diabetic and had a hyperactive thyroid. Poorly controlled diabetes and hyperthyroidism can diminish a patient’s fitness for the surgery. But in Juetten’s case, both conditions were under control with medication, and neither condition had damaged her eyes.
A physician examined Juetten’s eyes and discovered superficial punctuate keratitis (SPK). SPK is a condition involving pinpoint inflammations on the eye’s surface. The presence of SPK makes surgery less advisable because surgery can aggravate existing SPK and cause dry-eye syndrome. But Juetten’s SPK was “trace” — the lowest level of SPK detectable — and could have resulted from the examination itself. Because Juetten’s SPK seemed minimal and she had no history of dry-eye problems, the examining doctor informed her that she was a good candidate for surgery. The doctor also discussed with Juetten the potential common complications of surgery, including dryness and vision problems.
Juetten returned to LasikPlus Vision Center for her surgery, which was performed by Dr. David Whiting. Before operating, Dr. Whiting generally reviews a patient’s medical records, examines her eyes, and determines whether she is a good candidate for surgery. Dr. Whiting was aware that Juetten had trace SPK in both eyes but did not discuss the potential cumulative effect of having SPK, diabetes, and a history of thyroid abnormality with Juetten before the surgery.
Within days after her surgery, Juetten developed severe SPK, dry eyes, and vision problems. After efforts to treat these problems failed, Juetten sued respondent LCA-Vision, which owned and operated LasikPlus Vision Center. Juetten based her claim against LCA-Vision on its vicarious liability for the alleged negligence of Dr. Whiting and the examining doctor, who she claimed had failed to recognize that her pre-existing conditions were contraindications to surgery and failed to treat her SPK or warn her of the risks of and alternatives to surgery.
*775A plaintiff in a medical malpractice suit must serve on the defendant, within 180 days after commencement of the suit, affidavits identifying the expert witnesses the plaintiff expects to testify on her behalf and the substance of the experts’ testimony. MinmStat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008). The penalty for noncompliance with the statutory requirement is mandatory dismissal with prejudice of “each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case.” Id., subd. 6(b) (2008).
 LCA-Vision therefore moved to dismiss. Before the district court ruled on the motion to- dismiss, however, Juetten served an amended complaint adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant based on his own alleged negligence. This amendment essentially changed Dr. Whiting’s status from nonparty tortfeasor to liable tortfea-sor for the same conduct that formed the basis of the procedurally faltering vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision.
The district court held that there was no good cause to extend the 180-day affidavit-service deadline and granted LCA-Vision’s motion to dismiss based on Juetten’s failure to timely serve her expert affidavits. That same month, Juetten served expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting. Dr. Whiting moved to dismiss, arguing that the content of Juetten’s expert affidavits did not comply with the substantive requirements of Minn.Stat. § 145.682. Dr. Whiting also moved for summary judgment on procedural grounds based on law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel since the deadline-related basis for dismissal had already been established.
In February 2009, the district court granted Dr. Whiting summary judgment based on law of the case but stated that the substance of Juetten’s expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. Juetten appeals the grant of summary judgment. Dr. Whiting filed notices of review and challenges the district court’s dicta approving the substance of Juetten’s affidavits and its rejection of his alternative arguments for res judicata and collateral estoppel.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**JUETTEN v. LCA-VISION**

**No. A09-1234**

**Preliminary Ruling**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of respondent Dr. David Whiting. Appellant Stacy Juetten appeals the dismissal of her medical malpractice claim, arguing that the addition of Dr. Whiting as a defendant after the statutory deadline for expert affidavits constituted a new cause of action. Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented, the Court affirms the district court’s judgment.

**I. BACKGROUND**

In July 2005, appellant Stacy Juetten underwent LASIK surgery performed by Dr. David Whiting at the LasikPlus Vision Center. Juetten subsequently sued respondent LCA-Vision, the owner of the center, alleging vicarious liability for the negligence of Dr. Whiting and the examining doctor. Under Minnesota law, a plaintiff in a medical malpractice suit must serve affidavits identifying expert witnesses and the substance of their testimony within 180 days of commencing the suit. Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008). The statute mandates dismissal with prejudice for noncompliance. Id., subd. 6(b).

Juetten failed to serve the required affidavits within the 180-day period. Although she amended her complaint to add Dr. Whiting as a defendant before the district court ruled on LCA-Vision’s motion to dismiss, the district court ultimately dismissed the claim against LCA-Vision based on the procedural default. Juetten then served expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting, who moved for summary judgment. The district court granted summary judgment for Dr. Whiting based on the law of the case, noting that while the affidavits were substantively sufficient, they were untimely.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The central issue is whether the addition of Dr. Whiting as a defendant after the expiration of the 180-day deadline created a new cause of action with a new deadline, or whether the claim against Dr. Whiting is part of the same cause of action that was subject to dismissal for failure to comply with Minn. Stat. § 145.682.

**A. Mandatory Nature of the Statutory Deadline**

Minnesota Statute § 145.682 establishes strict procedural requirements for medical malpractice actions. Subdivision 6(b) explicitly states that the penalty for noncompliance with the affidavit-service requirement is the mandatory dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case." The Minnesota Supreme Court has held that this statute requires strict compliance and that dismissal is automatic for failure to serve expert affidavits within the statutory period. *Kastens v. Clinic for Special Children*, 723 N.W.2d 575, 578 (Minn. 2006). There is no statutory provision allowing a plaintiff to circumvent this mandatory dismissal by adding new defendants after the deadline has passed.

**B. Definition of Cause of Action**

Juetten argues that adding Dr. Whiting created a new cause of action. This argument is unpersuasive under Minnesota law. A cause of action is defined by the facts giving rise to the claim, not by the particular defendant named. *Wenzel v. Schmitz*, 324 N.W.2d 228, 231 (Minn. 1982). Here, the facts giving rise to the claim against Dr. Whiting are identical to those giving rise to the claim against LCA-Vision: the LASIK surgery performed in July 2005, the alleged failure to recognize contraindications, and the resulting injuries. The amendment adding Dr. Whiting merely changed his status from a nonparty tortfeasor to a liable tortfeasor for the same conduct. Because the underlying facts remain unchanged, the cause of action remains the same.

**C. Application of Law of the Case and Res Judicata**

The district court correctly applied the doctrine of law of the case. Once the court determined that Juetten failed to comply with the 180-day deadline for the cause of action arising from the LASIK surgery, that determination governed subsequent stages of the same litigation. The addition of a defendant to an already extinguished cause of action cannot revive it. Furthermore, the principles of res judicata and collateral estoppel support this conclusion, as the issue of procedural compliance was fully litigated and decided in the motion to dismiss LCA-Vision.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The district court did not err in granting summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting. The mandatory dismissal provisions of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 apply to the cause of action as a whole, defined by the underlying facts rather than the identity of the defendants. *Wenzel v. Schmitz*, 324 N.W.2d at 231. Because Juetten failed to serve expert affidavits within the 180-day period, the cause of action was subject to mandatory dismissal. *Kastens v. Clinic for Special Children*, 723 N.W.2d at 578. The subsequent addition of Dr. Whiting did not create a new cause of action or reset the statutory clock.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of respondent Dr. David Whiting is **AFFIRMED**.

## INTRODUCTION

Respondent Dr. David Whiting respectfully submits this rebuttal brief in defense of the district court's grant of summary judgment. Appellant Stacy Juetten's appeal attempts to relitigate issues that were properly resolved in the prior dismissal of her claim against LCA-Vision. The district court correctly applied Minnesota Statute § 145.682 and the doctrines of law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel. This Court should affirm the judgment below.

---

## I. THE STATUTORY FRAMEWORK MANDATES DISMISSAL

Minnesota Statute § 145.682 establishes unambiguous procedural requirements for medical malpractice actions. Subdivision 6(b) expressly provides that failure to serve expert affidavits within 180 days results in mandatory dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case." The Minnesota Supreme Court has consistently held that this statute requires strict compliance and permits no equitable exceptions. *Kastens v. Clinic for Special Children*, 723 N.W.2d 575, 578 (Minn. 2006).

The legislative purpose behind § 145.682 is to ensure early screening of medical malpractice claims and to prevent frivolous litigation by requiring plaintiffs to obtain expert validation at the outset of their cases. *Id.* This Court has recognized that the statute reflects a deliberate policy choice to impose strict procedural obligations on plaintiffs to prevent procedural gamesmanship. *Id.* at 579.

---

## II. THE CLAIM AGAINST DR. WHITING IS THE SAME CAUSE OF ACTION

### A. Minnesota Law Defines Cause of Action by Facts, Not Defendants

Appellant's central argument—that adding Dr. Whiting created a new cause of action—fails as a matter of law. The Minnesota Supreme Court squarely addressed this issue in *Wenzel v. Schmitz*, 324 N.W.2d 228, 231 (Minn. 1982), holding that "a cause of action is defined by the facts giving rise to the claim, not by the particular defendant named." This principle is fundamental to Minnesota civil procedure.

The facts underlying the claim against Dr. Whiting are identical to those underlying the claim against LCA-Vision:

- The same July 2005 surgical procedure at the same facility
- The same alleged negligence in failing to recognize contraindications (diabetes, hyperthyroidism, trace SPK)
- The same postoperative injuries (severe SPK, dry eyes, vision problems)
- The same alleged failure to warn of risks and alternatives

The only difference is that Dr. Whiting was originally sued as a nonparty tortfeasor and was subsequently added as a defendant. This procedural adjustment does not alter the factual predicate of the claim. As the district court correctly found, adding Dr. Whiting "essentially changed Dr. Whiting's status from nonparty tortfeasor to liable tortfeasor for the same conduct that formed the basis of the procedurally faltering vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision."

### B. The Amendment Does Not Relate Back to Create a New Cause of Action

While appellant may argue that the amendment adding Dr. Whiting relates back under Minn. R. Civ. P. 15.02, this argument misses the point. The relation-back doctrine addresses whether an amendment changes the date from which statutes of limitations are measured. It does not create a new cause of action for purposes of procedural statutory requirements like those in § 145.682.

The statute explicitly applies to "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary." Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b). Because there is only one cause of action here—defined by the undisputed facts of the July 2005 surgery and resulting injuries—the statutory deadline applies to that cause of action as a whole. Adding Dr. Whiting cannot reset a clock that has already expired.

---

## III. THE DOCTRINE OF LAW OF THE CASE PROPERLY APPLIES

### A. The Prior Dismissal Established the Procedural Rule

The district court correctly applied the doctrine of law of the case. When the court dismissed Juetten's claim against LCA-Vision for failure to comply with § 145.682, that ruling established the procedural framework for all subsequent proceedings in the same litigation. The doctrine provides that "a court generally adheres to its earlier ruling in a case unless there is a compelling reason to reconsider." *Rienter v. Northwestern Mut. Life Ins. Co.*, 545 N.W.2d 642, 646 (Minn. Ct. App. 1996).

No compelling reason exists to reconsider the procedural ruling here. The facts giving rise to the claim against Dr. Whiting were known to appellant from the inception of the litigation. She made a strategic decision to proceed against LCA-Vision first, presumably on a theory of vicarious liability, and only added Dr. Whiting after the statutory deadline had passed. This strategic choice cannot override the mandatory statutory requirements.

### B. The District Court's Dicta Does Not Create a Conflict

Appellant may attempt to rely on the district court's statement that "the substance of Juetten's expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served." This observation, while favorable to appellant on the merits of her expert evidence, does not alter the procedural outcome.

The district court made clear that the affidavits were untimely. The court stated it was granting summary judgment "based on law of the case"—not based on the substantive adequacy of the affidavits. The dicta regarding the affidavits' substance merely acknowledges that appellant might have had a viable malpractice claim if she had complied with procedural requirements. This does not create a basis for circumventing the mandatory dismissal provisions.

---

## IV. RES JUDICATA AND COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL SUPPORT DISMISSAL

### A. The Same Issue Was Litigated and Decided

The principles of res judicata and collateral estoppel reinforce the district court's ruling. The issue of compliance with § 145.682 was fully litigated in the motion to dismiss LCA-Vision. The district court determined that there was "no good cause to extend the 180-day affidavit-service deadline" and granted dismissal based on Juetten's failure to timely serve her expert affidavits.

This determination is binding on appellant. Collateral estoppel precludes relitigation of issues already decided in prior proceedings between the same parties. *See* *Haas v. County of Stearns*, 328 N.W.2d 719, 721 (Minn. 1982). The procedural compliance issue was decided against appellant in the LCA-Vision dismissal and cannot be relitigated in the claim against Dr. Whiting.

### B. Policy Considerations Support Preclusion

Allowing appellant to circumvent the statutory deadline by adding new defendants would undermine the statute's purpose and encourage procedural gamesmanship. Plaintiffs could wait until after the deadline expired to name individual tortfeasors, effectively nullifying the mandatory screening mechanism that § 145.682 was designed to provide.

The Minnesota Supreme Court has emphasized that § 145.682 reflects a legislative judgment that medical malpractice claims must be substantiated by expert testimony early in litigation. *Kastens*, 723 N.W.2d at 578. Permitting plaintiffs to reset the statutory clock by adding defendants after the deadline would frustrate this legislative intent and reward procedural maneuvering.

---

## V. APPELLANT'S EQUITABLE ARGUMENTS FAIL

### A. No Good Cause Exists for Extension

Appellant may argue that she had good cause for failing to serve timely affidavits. However, the district court already determined that "there was no good cause to extend the 180-day affidavit-service deadline." This finding is supported by the record and should not be disturbed on appeal.

The record shows that appellant was aware of Dr. Whiting's role in the surgery from the beginning of the case. Her decision to delay naming him as a defendant was a tactical choice, not a result of circumstances beyond her control. The statute permits no equitable exceptions for strategic litigation decisions. *Kastens*, 723 N.W.2d at 578.

### B. The Statute Permits No Exceptions

As the Minnesota Supreme Court has held, the mandatory dismissal provisions of § 145.682 apply without exception. *Id.* ("There is no statutory provision allowing a court to extend the time for service of the expert affidavit."). Appellant's arguments for equitable relief fall outside the scope of the statute's safe harbors.

The legislature could have included exceptions for good cause or substantial compliance, but it chose not to do so. This Court must respect the legislature's choice and apply the statute as written. *See* *Oanes v. Alsover*, 698 N.W.2d 918, 923 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005) (holding that the mandatory dismissal provisions of § 145.682 apply regardless of the strength of the underlying claim).

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, respondent Dr. David Whiting respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment. The mandatory dismissal provisions of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 apply to the cause of action as defined by the underlying facts. Appellant failed to serve timely expert affidavits, and the subsequent addition of Dr. Whiting did not create a new cause of action or reset the statutory clock. The doctrines of law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel properly preclude relitigation of the procedural issue. The district court correctly applied Minnesota law, and its judgment should be affirmed.

**OPINION**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of respondent Dr. David Whiting. Appellant Stacy Juetten appeals the dismissal of her medical malpractice claim, arguing that the addition of Dr. Whiting as a defendant after the statutory deadline for expert affidavits constituted a new cause of action. Having reviewed the rebuttals submitted by the parties, the Court affirms the district court’s judgment.

**II. ANALYSIS**

The arguments presented reinforce the conclusion that the district court properly granted summary judgment. The mandatory dismissal provisions of Minn. Stat. § 145.682, the definition of a cause of action under Minnesota law, and the doctrines of law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel all support the dismissal of Juetten’s claim against Dr. Whiting.

**A. The Statutory Deadline is Mandatory and Non-Discretionary**

Minnesota Statute § 145.682, subd. 6(b), mandates dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case" if the plaintiff fails to serve expert affidavits within 180 days. The Minnesota Supreme Court has held that this statute requires strict compliance and permits no equitable exceptions. *Kastens v. Clinic for Special Children*, 723 N.W.2d 575, 578 (Minn. 2006). The legislature’s intent in enacting this provision was to ensure early screening of medical malpractice claims and to prevent frivolous litigation. *Id.* at 579.

The respondent argues, and the Court agrees, that the statute’s mandatory dismissal provisions apply regardless of the strength of the underlying claim. *Oanes v. Alsover*, 698 N.W.2d 918, 923 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005). The district court’s observation that the substance of Juetten’s affidavits might have been sufficient had they been timely served does not create an exception to the statutory mandate. As noted in *Kastens*, there is no statutory provision allowing a court to extend the time for service of the expert affidavit or to excuse noncompliance based on the merits of the case. *Kastens*, 723 N.W.2d at 578.

**B. The Claim Against Dr. Whiting Is Not a New Cause of Action**

Juetten contends that adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant created a new cause of action, thereby resetting the 180-day clock. This argument is unpersuasive. Under Minnesota law, a cause of action is defined by the facts giving rise to the claim, not by the particular defendant named. *Wenzel v. Schmitz*, 324 N.W.2d 228, 231 (Minn. 1982).

The facts underlying the claim against Dr. Whiting are identical to those underlying the previously dismissed claim against LCA-Vision: the same July 2005 surgical procedure, the same alleged negligence in failing to recognize contraindications, and the same postoperative injuries. The amendment adding Dr. Whiting merely changed his status from a nonparty tortfeasor to a liable tortfeasor for the same conduct. Because the factual predicate remains unchanged, the cause of action remains the same. *Wenzel*, 324 N.W.2d at 231.

The respondent correctly notes that the relation-back doctrine under Minn. R. Civ. P. 15.02 does not save Juetten’s claim. While relation back may affect statutes of limitations, it does not create a new cause of action for purposes of the distinct procedural requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682. The statute applies to "each cause of action," and because there is only one cause of action here, the statutory deadline applies to that action as a whole.

**C. Law of the Case, Res Judicata, and Collateral Estoppel Bar Relitigation**

The district court properly applied the doctrine of law of the case. Once the court determined that Juetten failed to comply with the 180-day deadline for the cause of action arising from the LASIK surgery, that determination governed subsequent stages of the same litigation. *Rienter v. Northwestern Mut. Life Ins. Co.*, 545 N.W.2d 642, 646 (Minn. Ct. App. 1996) (stating that a court generally adheres to its earlier ruling in a case unless there is a compelling reason to reconsider). No compelling reason exists here; Juetten was aware of Dr. Whiting’s role from the inception of the case, and her decision to delay naming him was a strategic choice that cannot override the statutory requirements.

Furthermore, the principles of res judicata and collateral estoppel support the dismissal. The issue of procedural compliance with § 145.682 was fully litigated and decided in the motion to dismiss LCA-Vision. Collateral estoppel precludes relitigation of issues already decided in prior proceedings. *Haas v. County of Stearns*, 328 N.W.2d 719, 721 (Minn. 1982). Because the procedural compliance issue was decided against Juetten in the prior dismissal, it cannot be relitigated in the claim against Dr. Whiting.

Allowing a plaintiff to circumvent the statutory deadline by adding new defendants after the deadline has expired would undermine the statute’s purpose and encourage procedural gamesmanship. The legislature’s clear intent was to require expert validation at the outset of litigation, and this intent must be honored. *Kastens*, 723 N.W.2d at 578.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The district court did not err in granting summary judgment in favor of respondent Dr. Whiting. The mandatory dismissal provisions of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 apply to the cause of action as a whole, defined by the underlying facts rather than the identity of the defendants. *Wenzel*, 324 N.W.2d at 231. Because Juetten failed to serve expert affidavits within the 180-day period, the cause of action was subject to mandatory dismissal. *Kastens*, 723 N.W.2d at 578. The subsequent addition of Dr. Whiting did not create a new cause of action or reset the statutory clock. The doctrines of law of the case and collateral estoppel further preclude Juetten from relitigating the procedural default.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of respondent Dr. David Whiting is **AFFIRMED**.

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts are not in dispute and demonstrate that Juetten's claim against Dr. Whiting arises from the identical factual predicate as her previously dismissed claim against LCA-Vision.

**Pre-Surgical Evaluation**: In July 2005, Juetten presented to LasikPlus Vision Center for a preoperative assessment. Her medical history included diabetes and hyperactive thyroid, both of which were controlled with medication. The examining physician identified trace superficial punctuate keratitis (SPK), described as the lowest detectable level, and informed Juetten she was a good candidate for surgery. **Procedural History**: Juetten sued LCA-Vision based on vicarious liability for Dr. Whiting's alleged negligence. The district court granted Dr. Whiting summary judgment based on law of the case.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Statutory Deadline Is Mandatory

Minnesota Statute § 145.682, subd. 6(b), mandates dismissal with prejudice when a plaintiff fails to serve expert affidavits within 180 days for any cause of action requiring expert testimony. The Minnesota Supreme Court has consistently held that this statute requires strict compliance and permits no equitable exceptions. *Kastens v. Clinic for Special Children*, 723 N.W.2d 575, 578 (Minn. 2006). The legislature's intent was to ensure early screening of medical malpractice claims and prevent frivolous litigation. *Id.* at 579.

The statute contains no provision allowing courts to extend the deadline or excuse noncompliance based on the merits of the case. *Id.* at 578. As this Court noted in *Oanes v. Alsover*, the mandatory dismissal provisions apply regardless of the strength of the underlying claim. 698 N.W.2d 918, 923 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005).

### B. The Claim Against Dr. Whiting Is Not a New Cause of Action

Under Minnesota law, a cause of action is defined by the facts giving rise to the claim, not by the particular defendant named. *Wenzel v. Schmitz*, 324 N.W.2d 228, 231 (Minn. 1982). The facts underlying the claim against Dr. Whiting are identical to those underlying the previously dismissed claim against LCA-Vision: the same July 2005 surgical procedure, the same alleged negligence in failing to recognize contraindications, and the same postoperative injuries.

The amendment adding Dr. Whiting merely changed his status from a nonparty tortfeasor to a liable tortfeasor for the same conduct. Because the factual predicate remains unchanged, the cause of action remains the same. *Wenzel*, 324 N.W.2d at 231.

The relation-back doctrine under Minn. R. Civ. P. 15.02 does not create a new cause of action for purposes of the distinct procedural requirements of § 145.682. The statute applies to "each cause of action," and because there is only one cause of action here, the statutory deadline applies to that action as a whole.

### C. Law of the Case, Res Judicata, and Collateral Estoppel

The district court properly applied the doctrine of law of the case. Once the court determined that Juetten failed to comply with the 180-day deadline for the cause of action arising from the LASIK surgery, that determination governed subsequent stages of the same litigation. *Rienter v. Northwestern Mut. Life Ins. Co.*, 545 N.W.2d 642, 646 (Minn. Ct. App. 1996). A court generally adheres to its earlier ruling in a case unless there is a compelling reason to reconsider. *Id.* No compelling reason exists here; Juetten was aware of Dr. Whiting's role from the inception of the case, and her decision to delay naming him was a strategic choice that cannot override the statutory requirements.

The principles of res judicata and collateral estoppel also support dismissal. The issue of procedural compliance with § 145.682 was fully litigated and decided in the motion to dismiss LCA-Vision. Collateral estoppel precludes relitigation of issues already decided in prior proceedings. *Haas v. County of Stearns*, 328 N.W.2d 719, 721 (Minn. 1982). Because the procedural compliance issue was decided against Juetten in the prior dismissal, it cannot be relitigated in the claim against Dr. Whiting.

### A. The "New Cause of Action" Argument Fails

Juetten contends that adding Dr. Whiting as a defendant created a new cause of action, thereby resetting the 180-day clock. This argument contradicts established Minnesota law. In *Wenzel v. The facts here are identical: the same surgery, the same alleged negligence, and the same injuries. The amendment merely changed Dr. Whiting's procedural status.

### B. The Policy Rationale Supports Strict Compliance

Allowing plaintiffs to circumvent the statutory deadline by adding new defendants after the deadline has expired would undermine the statute's purpose and encourage procedural gamesmanship. The legislature's clear intent was to require expert validation at the outset of litigation, and this intent must be honored. *Kastens*, 723 N.W.2d at 578.

If this Court were to accept Juetten's argument, plaintiffs could simply wait until after dismissal to name individual tortfeasors, thereby evading the mandatory screening mechanism. Such a result would frustrate the legislature's purpose and render the statutory deadline meaningless.

This observation, however, does not create an exception to the mandatory dismissal provision.
