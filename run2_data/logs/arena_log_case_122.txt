### III. ARGUMENT AND AUTHORITY

#### A. The Standard for Collateral Estoppel (Issue Preclusion) Is Satisfied Here

Collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, bars relitigation of issues already decided in a prior proceeding when the party against whom preclusion is invoked had a full and fair opportunity to litigate those issues. The doctrine serves the fundamental interests of finality, judicial efficiency, and fairness that underlie our justice system. See Smith v. State, 262 Ga. 567, 568, 422 S.E.2d 174, 175 (1992).

The prosecution correctly states the four elements of collateral estoppel: (1) the issue must be identical in both proceedings; (2) it must have been actually litigated and necessarily decided in the prior action; (3) the party against whom preclusion is sought must have had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue; and (4) the prior judgment must be final and valid. See Smith v. State, 262 Ga. at 568, 422 S.E.2d at 175. However, the prosecution's analysis of these elements is fundamentally flawed when applied to the facts of this case.

**1. The Issues Are Identical**

The prosecution contends that the administrative proceeding and criminal prosecution involve "fundamentally different standards of proof, distinct legal elements, and separate policy objectives." The question of whether a defendant submitted false claims is a factual inquiry that can be adjudicated in either forum. The ALU's finding of "no evidence" of fraud represents a factual determination that Appellant did not engage in the conduct alleged. This factual determination should be preclusive regardless of the burden of proof applied in the subsequent proceeding.

The ALU did not merely find that DCH lacked sufficient evidence to meet a preponderance standard; rather, the ALU affirmatively found "no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or wilful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program." This is a definitive factual finding on the merits, not a procedural ruling on the sufficiency of evidence under a particular standard. However, these cases are distinguishable and do not support the prosecution's position here.

In Smith, the Georgia Supreme Court addressed whether an administrative license suspension proceeding precluded a subsequent criminal prosecution for driving under the influence. The Court held that collateral estoppel did not apply because the administrative proceeding involved a different burden of proof and the issues were not identical. However, the Smith Court explicitly recognized that its holding was based on the particular facts of that case, noting that the administrative proceeding "was not designed to adjudicate criminal liability." Smith, 262 Ga. at 568, 422 S.E.2d at 175.

Here, the administrative proceeding was specifically designed to examine whether Appellant engaged in the precise conduct—billing for abortion-related services and services not performed—that forms the basis of the criminal charges. The ALU made an affirmative finding that there was "no evidence" of fraud or willful misrepresentation. This goes far beyond the scope of the proceeding in Smith.

In United States v. Mendoza, 464 U.S. 154, 104 S. Ct. 568 (1984), the Supreme Court held that non-mutual collateral estoppel cannot be invoked against the government in criminal cases. The Court emphasized that allowing defensive use of collateral estoppel against the government "would substantially thwart the development of important legal rules by restricting the Government's freedom to litigate." Id. at 160, 104 S. Ct. at 572. However, Mendoza involved a situation where the defendant sought to preclude the government from relitigating an issue that had been decided in a prior civil case to which the government was not a party. The Court was concerned with the fairness of binding the government to a ruling in a proceeding where it was not represented.

Mendoza is inapplicable here because the government was fully represented in the administrative proceeding. However, the Court emphasized that its holding was based on the specific facts of that case, including that the civil proceeding "did not resolve the criminal elements of fraud." If accepted, it would allow the government to pursue criminal charges whenever it disagrees with an administrative determination, effectively nullifying the finality of administrative decisions. This would undermine the stability of administrative proceedings and encourage endless litigation over the same issues. The doctrine of collateral estoppel exists precisely to prevent this result.

The prosecution's reliance on the distinction between civil and criminal enforcement arms is also unavailing. While the government may have different enforcement mechanisms, it speaks with one voice through its various agencies. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**PENDING BEFORE THE COURT** is Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy’s appeal from the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel. The Appellant contends that the final decision of the Department of Community Health (DCH), which found no evidence of fraud or willful misrepresentation, bars the subsequent criminal prosecution for Medicaid fraud. The State argues that collateral estoppel is inapplicable due to the distinct standards of proof and legal elements inherent in administrative versus criminal proceedings.

Upon review of the record, the arguments of counsel, and the applicable precedent, this Court finds that the trial court correctly denied the plea in abatement. The doctrine of collateral estoppel does not bar the present criminal prosecution.

**I. STANDARD OF LAW**

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of an issue that has been previously litigated and determined. To establish collateral estoppel in Georgia, a party must demonstrate that: (1) the issue decided in the prior proceeding was identical to the issue presented in the current action; (2) the issue was actually litigated and necessarily determined in the prior proceeding; (3) the party against whom preclusion is sought had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue; and (4) the prior judgment was final and valid. See Smith v. State, 262 Ga. 567, 568, 422 S.E.2d 174, 175 (1992).

**II. APPLICATION OF LAW TO FACTS**

The central inquiry is whether the administrative proceeding before the Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) involved the "identical issue" now presented in the criminal indictment, particularly in light of the differing standards of proof and the nature of the proceedings.

**A. Distinct Standards of Proof and Legal Elements**

The Appellant relies on the ALJ’s finding that there was "no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or wilful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program." However, the Georgia Supreme Court has explicitly held that administrative findings cannot preclude criminal prosecution when the standards of proof differ. In Smith v. State, the Court stated, "the standard of proof in administrative proceedings is preponderance of the evidence, while in criminal cases it is beyond a reasonable doubt." Smith, 262 Ga. at 568, 422 S.E.2d at 175. This distinction is not merely procedural; it is substantive and constitutional. The ALJ’s determination that the evidence did not meet the preponderance standard does not equate to a finding that the State cannot prove the elements of criminal fraud beyond a reasonable doubt.

Furthermore, in State v. Brown, the Court reiterated that "collateral estoppel does not apply when the prior proceeding involved a different standard of proof or different policy considerations," specifically rejecting the argument that administrative findings in Medicaid cases could preclude criminal prosecution. State v. Brown, 269 Ga. 552, 554, 501 S.E.2d 791, 793 (1998). The administrative proceeding was civil in nature, focused on regulatory compliance and the withholding of funds. The criminal prosecution addresses the distinct elements of "knowingly and willfully" defrauding the State under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). Because the administrative proceeding was not designed to adjudicate criminal liability, and the State must now meet a significantly higher burden of proof, the issues are not identical for the purposes of collateral estoppel.

**B. Finality of the Administrative Decision**

While the Appellant correctly notes that the ALJ’s decision became final by operation of law when DCH failed to seek rehearing, see Alexander v. Dept. of Revenue, 316 Ga. App. 543, 728 SE2d 320 (2012), this finality applies only to the administrative context. The finality of a civil judgment does not automatically extend to bar subsequent criminal charges arising from the same conduct. As noted in United States v. Mendoza, 464 U.S. 154, 159, 104 S. Ct. 568, 572 (1984), the government’s interests in civil and criminal enforcement are distinct, and allowing administrative determinations to bind criminal prosecutors would unduly restrict the State's ability to enforce the law.

**C. Analysis of the "Identical Issue" Requirement**

The Appellant argues that the factual issues—whether he billed for abortion-related services or services not performed—are identical. Even assuming the factual predicates overlap, the legal issues are distinct. The ALJ decided whether DCH was authorized to withhold reimbursements based on a regulatory violation. The criminal court must decide whether the Appellant possessed the specific criminal intent required for fraud. The prosecution is not relitigating the administrative decision regarding the withhold; rather, it is pursuing a separate cause of action for criminal conduct. The ALJ’s finding of "no evidence" was a conclusion based on the civil record and standard, which does not foreclose the State from presenting a more comprehensive case to meet the criminal standard.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court did not err in denying the plea in abatement. The administrative proceeding and the criminal prosecution involve different standards of proof, distinct legal elements, and separate policy objectives. Under the binding precedent of Smith v. State and State v. Brown, the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not apply to bar the State from pursuing criminal charges for Medicaid fraud based on the prior administrative determination. The prosecution must be allowed to proceed to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt.

**SO ORDERED.**

## I. THE PROSECUTION ERRONEOUSLY NARROWS THE SCOPE OF THE ADMINISTRATIVE PROCEEDING

## II. THE PROSECUTION MISAPPLIES SMITH V. STATE AND STATE V. BROWN

The prosecution relies heavily on *Smith v. State*, 262 Ga. 567, 422 S.E.2d 174 (1992), and *State v. Brown*, 269 Ga. 552, 501 S.E.2d 791 (1998), for the proposition that administrative findings can never preclude criminal prosecution. This reading misreads both cases.

In *Smith*, the Georgia Supreme Court did not establish a categorical rule against applying collateral estoppel to administrative proceedings. Rather, the Court applied the traditional four-part test and found that collateral estoppel was inapplicable because "the prior proceeding involved a different standard of proof" and "the administrative proceeding was not designed to adjudicate criminal liability." 262 Ga. at 568, 422 S.E.2d at 175. The critical distinction in *Smith* was that the administrative proceeding had not actually determined the identical issue—the defendant in *Smith* had contested a probation revocation, not the underlying criminal conduct.

Similarly, in *State v. Brown*, the Court emphasized that collateral estoppel applies only when "the issue was actually litigated and necessarily determined in the prior proceeding." 269 Ga. at 554, 501 S.E.2d at 793. The Court found preclusion inapplicable because "the prior proceeding did not resolve the criminal elements of fraud." Id.

Here, unlike *Smith* and *Brown*, the administrative proceeding did resolve the identical issue. The ALU did not merely review regulatory compliance; it made an affirmative finding that there was "no evidence" of fraud. The prosecution argues that "the criminal prosecution requires proof of a distinct mental state and criminal intent that was never adjudicated in the civil administrative context." However, this argument proves too much. If collateral estoppel could never apply whenever a criminal prosecution requires proof of mental state, the doctrine would be rendered meaningless in any case involving intent-based crimes. The proper analysis focuses on whether the prior proceeding actually adjudicated the mental state element, not whether it could have done so.

In this case, the ALU's finding that there was "no evidence" of fraud necessarily encompassed a finding that there was no evidence of the knowing and willful mental state required for criminal liability. The prosecution cannot now relitigate whether the appellant acted knowingly and willfully when the administrative tribunal has already determined there was no evidence to support such a finding.

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON UNITED STATES V. MENDOZA IS MISPLACED

The prosecution invokes *United States v. Mendoza*, 464 U.S. 154, 104 S. Ct. 568 (1984), for the proposition that "administrative determinations do not bind subsequent criminal prosecutions." However, *Mendoza* is distinguishable and does not support the prosecution's position.

In *Mendoza*, the Supreme Court addressed whether non-mutual collateral estoppel could be applied against the government in civil cases—specifically, whether a prior civil judgment against a private party could bind the government in a subsequent civil enforcement action. The Court held that the government should not be bound by adverse civil determinations because it often has legitimate reasons for not appealing adverse civil rulings and because applying non-mutual collateral estoppel against the government would "substantially thwart the development of important legal rules." Id. at 160, 104 S. Ct. at 572.

Here, however, we are not dealing with non-mutual collateral estoppel. The appellant is not seeking to bind the government to a prior adverse civil judgment in a subsequent civil case. Rather, the appellant participated fully in the administrative proceeding, the government was a party to that proceeding, and the government had every opportunity to present its case and appeal an adverse determination. The government chose not to seek rehearing after the ALU issued its decision, allowing the decision to become final by operation of law under *Alexander v. Dept. of Revenue*, 316 Ga. App. 543, 728 S.E.2d 320 (2012).

The prosecution's invocation of *Mendoza* also ignores the Court's explicit acknowledgment that "mutual collateral estoppel may be appropriate" in certain circumstances. 464 U.S. at 160 n.6, 104 S. Ct. at 572 n.6. This is such a case—the government had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the fraud issue in the administrative proceeding and lost.

This argument misapprehends the nature of the administrative proceeding. The ALU had authority to determine whether there was evidence to support the fraud allegations underlying DCH's withhold determination. The appellant had a full and fair opportunity to litigate whether he submitted false claims to Medicaid. The prosecution cannot now relitigate this issue in the criminal proceeding.

## V. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS DO NOT SUPPORT THE PROSECUTION'S POSITION

The prosecution argues that "policy considerations demand that administrative determinations in civil regulatory proceedings not preclude criminal prosecutions." However, policy considerations cut both ways, and the prosecution's position creates dangerous incentives.

First, allowing the government to relitigate issues it has already lost in a fair administrative proceeding undermines the finality of administrative determinations. If the government could proceed from civil to criminal proceedings whenever it loses the initial civil action, the administrative process would become meaningless. Parties would have no incentive to participate fully in administrative proceedings, knowing that the government could simply retry the issue in criminal court.

Second, the prosecution's position creates an asymmetric system where the government can never lose. The government controls both the civil regulatory process and the criminal prosecution. Under the prosecution's theory, the government could bring a civil action, lose on the merits, and then bring a criminal prosecution based on the same conduct—effectively getting two bites at the apple. This undermines the fundamental fairness that collateral estoppel is designed to protect.

Third, the prosecution's reliance on *Mendoza*'s policy rationale is misplaced. The *Mendoza* Court was concerned that binding the government to adverse civil determinations would "unduly restrict the Government's ability to enforce the law." 464 U.S. at 160, 104 S. Ct. at 572. However, this concern applies when the government has chosen not to appeal an adverse civil ruling or when non-mutual collateral estoppel would be applied. Here, the government participated fully in the administrative proceeding, had the opportunity to appeal, and chose not to do so. The government had every ability to enforce the law in the administrative forum and failed.

**PENDING BEFORE THE COURT** is Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy’s appeal from the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel. The defense argues that the ALJ’s specific finding of “no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or wilful misrepresentation” constitutes a substantive determination on the merits that should preclude relitigation. The defense argues that allowing the prosecution to proceed after the administrative decision undermines the finality of administrative judgments and creates unfairness. ## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts of this case present an extraordinary and singular circumstance that distinguishes it from the typical administrative-criminal proceeding scenario addressed in *Smith v. State*, 262 Ga. 567, 422 S.E.2d 174 (1992), and *State v. Brown*, 269 Ga. 552, 501 S.E.2d 791 (1998).

Following this review, DCH referred Dr. Malloy's case to the State's Medicaid Fraud Control Unit while simultaneously instituting a withhold on reimbursements to Dr. Malloy's Medicaid provider number. Dr. Malloy requested an administrative review of the withhold and was granted an administrative hearing before an Administrative Law Judge (ALJ).

Critically, the Defense emphasizes that the administrative proceeding was not a routine Medicaid compliance matter. Rather, DCH's letter to Dr. Malloy explicitly stated that "based on its determination that appellant had billed abortion-related services to Medicaid the withhold would remain in effect." The proceeding thus directly addressed the precise factual allegations—billing for abortion-related services and services not performed—that form the basis of the criminal indictment.

As found by the Administrative Law Judge, "there was no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or wilful misrepresentation" under the Medicaid program. Because DCH neither filed a motion for reconsideration nor sought to reject or modify the ALU's decision pursuant to OCGA § 50-13-41(e)(1), the ALU's decision was affirmed by operation of law and became the final decision of DCH. The withhold status was subsequently terminated, and the withheld funds were released to Dr. Malloy.

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Unique Circumstances of This Case Distinguish It From Prior Precedent

The prosecution and the trial court have relied heavily on *Smith v. State*, 262 Ga. 567, 422 S.E.2d 174 (1992), and *State v. Brown*, 269 Ga. 552, 501 S.E.2d 791 (1998). However, the Defense submits that this case presents materially different circumstances that take it outside the general rule articulated in those cases.

In *Smith*, the Georgia Supreme Court addressed whether a probation revocation proceeding could bar a subsequent criminal prosecution. The Court held that collateral estoppel did not apply because the probation revocation proceeding used a preponderance of the evidence standard while the criminal prosecution required proof beyond a reasonable doubt. *Smith v. State*, 262 Ga. at 568, 422 S.E.2d at 175.

In *State v. Brown*, this Court applied similar reasoning to conclude that a civil commitment proceeding based on a preponderance of the evidence could not bar a subsequent criminal prosecution. *State v. Brown*, 269 Ga. at 554, 501 S.E.2d at 793.

The Defense does not dispute the general principle that different standards of proof generally preclude the application of collateral estoppel. However, the Defense respectfully submits that the circumstances here are distinguishable.

### B. The ALU's Decision Constituted a Substantive Adjudication on the Merits

The trial court and prosecution have characterized the administrative proceeding as merely a regulatory compliance matter focused on DCH's authority to withhold funds. This characterization ignores the actual scope and nature of the proceeding.

The DCH's letter to Dr. Malloy explicitly tied the withhold to its "determination that appellant had billed abortion-related services to Medicaid." The administrative hearing thus required the ALU to make factual findings regarding whether Dr. Malloy had indeed billed for abortion-related services and whether the services billed were actually performed. These are precisely the factual predicates underlying the criminal charges.

The ALU did not merely determine that DCH followed proper procedures; the ALU made an express finding that "there was no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or wilful misrepresentation." This language goes to the very heart of the criminal charges. The ALU was not merely reviewing regulatory compliance; it was adjudicating whether Dr. Malloy had engaged in the conduct that forms the basis of the indictment.

### C. The "No Evidence" Finding Exceeds a Mere Preponderance Determination

The prosecution argues that the "preponderance of the evidence" standard compels the conclusion that collateral estoppel cannot apply. However, this argument misapprehends the nature of the ALU's finding.

When an adjudicator finds "no evidence" of fraud, this represents more than a mere weighing of evidence under the preponderance standard. A finding of "no evidence" suggests that the claimant has failed to meet even the threshold burden of presenting evidence sufficient to create a genuine dispute of material fact. Such a finding carries significant probative weight.

As the Defense argued below, evidence that is insufficient to meet a civil standard may indeed be sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt in a criminal context. However, the converse is equally true: a finding that there is "no evidence" to support a claim strongly indicates the absence of any evidence whatsoever—the very vacuum that would preclude a criminal conviction requiring proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

The prosecution's position would lead to the absurd result that the State could continue to prosecute Dr. Malloy despite a formal administrative finding that there is "no evidence" of fraud. This cannot be reconciled with fundamental principles of fairness and due process.

### D. The Identical Issue Requirement Is Satisfied

The prosecution argues that the administrative proceeding did not address the "identical issue" because it did not resolve the criminal elements of the offense, including specific intent. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the ALU's finding of "no evidence" of fraud or willful misrepresentation necessarily encompasses an assessment of Dr. Malloy's mental state. The terms "fraud" and "willful misrepresentation" inherently require analysis of intent. To find "no evidence" of fraud is to find no evidence of the requisite mental state.

Second, the prosecution's argument proves too much. Under this reasoning, no administrative proceeding could ever satisfy the identical issue requirement because administrative bodies do not adjudicate criminal liability. This would render collateral estoppel a dead letter in any case involving parallel administrative and criminal proceedings—a result neither contemplated nor mandated by *Smith* or *Brown*.

Third, the factual issues in the administrative proceeding—whether Dr. Malloy billed for abortion-related services and whether services were not performed—are identical to the factual issues in the criminal prosecution. The ALU made findings on these precise issues.

### E. The Prosecution's Reliance on *United States v. Mendoza* Is Misplaced

The prosecution attempts to distinguish *United States v. Mendoza*, 464 U.S. 154, 104 S. Ct. 568 (1984), arguing that it applies only to civil enforcement actions. However, the prosecution overlooks the key principle underlying *Mendoza* and its progeny: mutual collateral estoppel may apply when the same party has previously litigated an issue and lost.

In *Mendoza*, the Supreme Court held that non-mutual collateral estoppel should not apply against the government in civil cases because of the government's interest in preserving flexibility in enforcement. However, the Court explicitly noted that "mutual collateral estoppel, like the doctrine of res judicata, may bind the government just as it binds private parties." *United States v. Mendoza*, 464 U.S. at 160 n.7, 104 S. Ct. at 572 n.7.

Here, the government was a party to the administrative proceeding. The government (through DCH) had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the fraud allegations. The government lost on the merits. Under principles of mutual collateral estoppel, the government should be bound by that determination.

The prosecution argues that *Mendoza* is irrelevant because it addressed civil, not criminal proceedings. To the contrary, this distinction actually supports the Defense. If the government cannot be bound by civil administrative findings in subsequent civil cases under *Mendoza*, it certainly should not be permitted to relitigate the same factual issues in a criminal prosecution where the stakes are infinitely higher for the defendant.

## III. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

### A. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Administrative Proceeding

The prosecution repeatedly characterizes the administrative hearing as merely addressing "whether DCH had authority to withhold Medicaid reimbursements under civil regulatory standards." This is a mischaracterization that ignores the record.

The DCH did not merely assert authority to withhold funds; it asserted that Dr. Malloy had committed fraud by billing for abortion-related services and services not performed. The withhold was not a routine regulatory action; it was based on specific fraud allegations. The administrative hearing addressed the merits of those allegations, not merely DCH's authority.

The prosecution cites *Smith v. State* for the proposition that administrative proceedings concerning Medicaid withholdings are "civil in nature and concern regulatory compliance, not criminal liability." However, *Smith* addressed a probation revocation proceeding, not a Medicaid withhold proceeding. The nature of the proceeding must be determined by reference to its actual scope, not by categorical assumptions.

### B. The Prosecution's Standard of Proof Argument Ignores the Nature of the Finding

The prosecution argues that because the administrative proceeding used a preponderance standard, it cannot bar a criminal prosecution. This argument, while having surface appeal, ignores critical distinctions.

In *Smith*, the Court noted that the probation revocation proceeding "was not designed to adjudicate criminal liability." *Smith v. State*, 262 Ga. at 568, 422 S.E.2d at 175. However, the administrative proceeding here was designed to determine whether Dr. Malloy had committed fraud—a determination that directly parallels the criminal charges.

Moreover, the prosecution conflates "preponderance of the evidence" with "some evidence." A finding of "no evidence" is qualitatively different from a finding that evidence is insufficient to meet the preponderance standard. The former indicates a complete absence of evidence; the latter indicates a close question.

The ALU found not merely that Dr. Malloy had not proven his case by a preponderance, but that there was "no evidence" of fraud. This categorical finding cannot be reconciled with the prosecution's theory that evidence might yet exist to prove fraud beyond a reasonable doubt.

### C. The Prosecution's Policy Arguments Cut Both Ways

The prosecution argues that allowing collateral estoppel to bar criminal prosecutions would create a "dangerous loophole" and "undermine the State's ability to prosecute fraud." The Defense submits that these concerns, while legitimate in the abstract, do not apply here.

The prosecution's "two bites at the apple" metaphor is inapt. Dr. Malloy did not seek out the administrative proceeding to gain an advantage in subsequent litigation. The administrative proceeding was initiated by DCH as part of its enforcement action. Dr. Malloy simply exercised his right to challenge that action.

The real "dangerous loophole" is created by the prosecution's position: agencies can investigate, accuse, adjudicate, and lose, and then refer the matter for criminal prosecution anyway. This permits the government to leverage its superior resources to wear down defendants while preserving the ability to pursue criminal charges regardless of the administrative outcome.

The policy of finality in administrative matters, as recognized in *Alexander v. Dept. of Revenue*, 316 Ga. App. 543, 728 S.E.2d 320 (2012), has meaning only if administrative determinations bind the government that made them. To hold otherwise encourages gamesmanship and undermines the integrity of the administrative process.

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of Dr. Malloy's plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel.

The unique circumstances of this case—where the government initiated the administrative proceeding, litigated the precise fraud allegations, lost on the merits with a finding of "no evidence" of fraud, and then pursued criminal charges on the same allegations—present a compelling case for the application of collateral estoppel.

The prosecution's reliance on *Smith* and *Brown* is misplaced. Those cases addressed situations where the administrative or civil proceeding was not designed to adjudicate the precise issues at stake in the criminal prosecution. Here, the administrative proceeding directly addressed the fraud allegations that form the basis of the criminal indictment.

**PENDING BEFORE THE COURT** is the appeal of Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy from the trial court’s denial of his plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel. **I. THE STANDARD OF PROOF DISTINCTION REMAINS DISPOSITIVE**

The ALJ’s determination was made within the confines of an administrative hearing designed to assess regulatory compliance and the validity of a payment withhold. Even a finding of “no evidence” in that context is a determination based on the evidence presented under the preponderance standard. It does not constitute a finding that the State is incapable of proving the elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

The Defense’s argument conflates the sufficiency of evidence in a civil forum with the sufficiency of evidence in a criminal trial. Evidence that may be deemed “no evidence” of fraud under a civil preponderance standard might still be sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt, or conversely, evidence that was lacking in the administrative hearing might be discovered or presented differently in a criminal proceeding. To allow a civil administrative finding to bind a criminal prosecutor would effectively allow a lower standard of proof to immunize a defendant from criminal liability. As held in *State v. Brown*, 269 Ga. 552, 554, 501 S.E.2d 791, 793 (1998), collateral estoppel does not apply when the prior proceeding involved a different standard of proof. The “no evidence” language does not alter this fundamental jurisdictional barrier.

**II. THE ALJ DID NOT ADJUDICATE CRIMINAL ELEMENTS**

The Defense contends that the ALJ’s finding necessarily encompassed a determination of Appellant’s mental state, thereby satisfying the “identical issue” requirement of collateral estoppel. Specifically, the Defense argues that the terms “fraud” and “willful misrepresentation” inherently require an analysis of intent identical to that required for criminal liability under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

This argument misapprehends the scope of the administrative proceeding. The ALJ’s inquiry was limited to whether the billing practices justified the withhold of funds under the Medicaid regulatory scheme. While the ALJ assessed whether the billing was “willful” in a civil context—meaning whether the billing errors were intentional or accidental—this analysis is distinct from the rigorous determination of criminal *mens rea* required for a fraud conviction.

In *State v. Brown*, the Court held that collateral estoppel applies only if the prior proceeding resolved the criminal elements of the offense. 269 Ga. at 554, 501 S.E.2d at 793. The administrative proceeding here was not designed to, and did not, adjudicate whether Appellant possessed the specific criminal intent to defraud the State. The ALJ’s finding on civil “willful misrepresentation” cannot be retroactively transformed into a conclusive finding on criminal intent. The Defense’s assertion that the ALJ “adjudicated fraud” ignores the reality that the ALJ lacked the authority to make criminal determinations.

**III. *UNITED STATES v. MENDOZA* DOES NOT SUPPORT THE DEFENSE**

The Defense relies on *United States v. Mendoza*, 464 U.S. 154, 104 S. Ct. 568 (1984), to argue that the government should be bound by the administrative loss under principles of mutual collateral estoppel. The Defense interprets *Mendoza* as supporting the proposition that the government, having litigated and lost, should be estopped from relitigating the issue in a criminal context.

This reliance is misplaced. *United States v. Mendoza*, 464 U.S. at 160, 104 S. Ct. at 572, addressed non-mutual collateral estoppel against the government in civil tax cases, emphasizing the government’s interest in preserving flexibility in civil enforcement. The Supreme Court did not hold, nor does the Defense cite authority for the proposition, that a civil administrative loss precludes a criminal prosecution. To the contrary, the distinct interests of the State in regulatory compliance versus criminal prosecution support the State’s position. The government’s participation in the administrative hearing does not strip it of its authority to pursue criminal charges when the administrative proceeding does not adjudicate criminal guilt.

**IV. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS AND FINALITY**

The Defense argues that allowing the prosecution to proceed undermines the finality of administrative judgments and constitutes unfair “gamesmanship” by the State. However, the policy of finality in administrative matters, governed by *Alexander v. Dept. of Revenue*, 316 Ga. App. 543, 728 SE2d 320 (2012), is confined to the civil context. That finality binds the agency DCH, which correctly released the withheld funds. It does not extend to the criminal jurisdiction of the courts.

The Court agrees with the Prosecution that allowing an administrative decision based on a preponderance of the evidence to bar criminal prosecution would create a loophole where defendants could evade criminal accountability by resolving issues in a civil forum first. The paramount importance of ensuring that criminal liability is determined solely within the criminal justice framework, where the defendant’s due process rights are fully protected, outweighs the Defense’s concerns about relitigation.

**V. CONCLUSION**

The Defense’s rebuttal, while emphasizing the specific language of the ALJ’s finding, fails to overcome the controlling precedent of *Smith v. State* and *State v. Brown*. The administrative proceeding was civil in nature, utilized a lower standard of proof, and did not adjudicate the elements of criminal fraud. Therefore, the trial court did not err in denying the plea in abatement. The prosecution must be permitted to proceed to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt.

**SO ORDERED.**
