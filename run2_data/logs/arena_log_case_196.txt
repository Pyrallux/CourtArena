To begin, the Compassionate Use Act is a narrow criminal defense statute. This distinction is dispositive. Federal law unequivocally classifies marijuana as a Schedule I controlled substance, making its possession illegal regardless of state medical authorizations. The United States Supreme Court has squarely held that federal law preempts state medical marijuana laws in contexts involving interstate commerce. *Gonzales v. Raich*, 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 (2005). Because marijuana remains illegal under federal law, an employer cannot be compelled to accommodate its use without violating federal statutes.  

Under California law, employers have long possessed the right to enforce drug-free workplace policies, including preemployment drug testing. As this Court recognized in *Loder v. City of Glendale*, 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 (1997), "California law permits employers to require drug testing and to make employment decisions based on the results of such tests." The *Loder* Court expressly rejected the notion that medical marijuana use could override an employer's lawful right to maintain drug-free policies. The plaintiff here was hired as a systems administrator—a position requiring reliability, safety, and compliance with workplace standards—and defendant lawfully required a drug test as a condition of employment. The plaintiff’s positive test for THC, a federally prohibited substance, provided a legitimate, nondiscriminatory basis for termination.

This Court reviews the complaint de novo to determine whether the plaintiff has stated a cause of action. ## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights or Obligations

The plaintiff contends that his termination was unlawful because his marijuana use was authorized by the Compassionate Use Act (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5). This argument fails as a matter of law because the Compassionate Use Act does not address employment relationships.

The Compassionate Use Act of 1996, codified at Health and Safety Code section 11362.5, was added by initiative (Proposition 215) and approved by California voters. The Act provides that a person who uses marijuana for medical purposes on a physician's recommendation shall not be subject to certain state criminal charges involving the drug, including possession under Health and Safety Code section 11357. The statute is explicitly a criminal defense mechanism—it provides a shield against state criminal prosecution but creates no rights or obligations in the employment context.

Nothing in the text or history of the Compassionate Use Act suggests that California voters intended the measure to address the respective rights and duties of employers and employees. The voters were concerned with protecting seriously ill patients from criminal prosecution for using medical marijuana, not with regulating private employment relationships. The plaintiff cannot transform a criminal defense statute into a sword to enforce private employment rights.

This interpretation is consistent with the California Supreme Court's recognition in Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], that California law permits employers to require preemployment drug tests and to take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions. The defendant's drug-free workplace policy falls squarely within this recognized employer right.

### B. The FEHA Does Not Require Accommodation of Federally Illegal Conduct

The plaintiff alleges that the defendant violated the FEHA by discharging him because of, and by failing to make reasonable accommodation for, his disability. Gov. Code § 12940, subd. (a). This claim fails as a matter of law because the FEHA's reasonable accommodation requirement does not extend to conduct that violates federal law.

The FEHA prohibits employment discrimination against qualified individuals with disabilities and requires employers to make reasonable accommodation for known disabilities unless doing so would impose an undue hardship on the operation of the employer's business. However, the accommodation obligation applies only to lawful conduct. The FEHA does not require employers to accommodate the use of illegal drugs or substances.

Marijuana remains classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under federal law, and its possession and use are categorically prohibited. 21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a). The United States Supreme Court has unequivocally held that federal law prohibiting marijuana preempts state medical marijuana laws. In Gonzales v. Raich (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195], the Court confirmed that Congress has the power to prohibit the local cultivation and use of marijuana, even when authorized by state law for medical purposes. The Court emphasized that federal policy is "designed to prohibit entirely the possession and use of specified substances," including marijuana.

Similarly, in United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers' Cooperative (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711], the Court held that there is no medical necessity exception to the federal Controlled Substances Act's prohibition on marijuana.

Because the use of marijuana is prohibited by federal law, an employer cannot be required to accommodate such use. To require accommodation would be unreasonable as a matter of law and would force the employer to tolerate conduct that federal law is "designed to prohibit entirely." Gonzales v. Raich, supra, 545 U.S. at 26. The defendant's decision to terminate the plaintiff based on his positive drug test was not discriminatory; rather, it was a lawful response to the plaintiff's use of a federally prohibited substance.

The defendant required preemployment drug testing as a condition of employment, a policy that was disclosed to the plaintiff before he accepted the job offer. This policy is entirely lawful under California law.

In Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], the California Supreme Court held that employers may lawfully require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions. The defendant's policy was neutral and applied uniformly to all prospective employees. It was not directed at the plaintiff's disability but rather at the use of illegal drugs.

The plaintiff's positive test for THC provided a legitimate and lawful basis for his termination. The plaintiff himself alleged that his disability did not affect his ability to perform the essential functions of his job. This admission underscores that the termination was based on drug use, not on any disability-related reason. The FEHA does not protect the use of federally prohibited substances, even when such use is linked to a disability.

### D. No Public Policy Mandate Supports the Wrongful Termination Claim

To state a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy, a plaintiff must identify a clear public policy mandate rooted in constitutional, statutory, or regulatory authority. Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157]; Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330].

The Act is silent on employment issues and creates no such mandate. Moreover, any state policy favoring medical marijuana use is preempted by federal law, as confirmed in Gonzales v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883, California law unequivocally permits employers to require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions. Therefore, the plaintiff cannot rely on the Act to establish a right to use marijuana that overrides an employer's lawful policies.

**II. The FEHA Does Not Require Accommodation of Federally Illegal Conduct**

The plaintiff claims that the respondent violated the FEHA (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (a)) by failing to accommodate his disability. This argument fails as a matter of law because the FEHA’s requirement of "reasonable accommodation" does not extend to conduct that violates federal law.

Marijuana remains classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under federal law, and its possession and use are prohibited. (21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a)). The United States Supreme Court has unequivocally held that federal law prohibiting the possession and use of marijuana preempts state medical marijuana laws. In *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29, the Court confirmed that Congress has the power to prohibit the local cultivation and use of marijuana, even when authorized by state law for medical purposes. The Court emphasized that federal policy is "designed to prohibit entirely the possession and use of specified substances," including marijuana. Similarly, in *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495, the Court held that there is no medical necessity exception to the federal Controlled Substances Act.

Because the use of marijuana is prohibited by federal law, an employer cannot be required to accommodate such use. To require an employer to accommodate the use of a federally illegal substance would be unreasonable as a matter of law. The requested accommodation—permitting off-duty marijuana use—would force the employer to tolerate conduct that federal law is "designed to prohibit entirely." *Gonzales v. Raich*, supra, 545 U.S. at 26. Consequently, the respondent had no duty under the FEHA to accommodate the plaintiff's marijuana use, and the termination based on the positive drug test was not discriminatory.

**III. No Public Policy Mandate Supports the Plaintiff’s Wrongful Termination Claim**

To state a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy, a plaintiff must identify a clear public policy mandate rooted in constitutional, statutory, or regulatory authority. (*Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887; *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170, 176-178.) The plaintiff has not identified any such mandate. The Compassionate Use Act does not establish a public policy protecting employees from termination for medical marijuana use, nor does any other statute or constitutional provision. As the use of marijuana remains illegal under federal law, there is no fundamental public policy prohibiting an employer from terminating an employee for its use. To the extent any state policy could be inferred, it is preempted by federal law as established in *Gonzales v. Raich*, supra, 545 U.S. 1.

**CONCLUSION**

The plaintiff’s complaint fails to state a cause of action because the Compassionate Use Act does not apply to employment, the FEHA does not require accommodation of federally illegal conduct, and established public policy does not protect medical marijuana users from termination. The arguments presented in the closing arguments confirm that the lower courts properly sustained the demurrer without leave to amend. The judgment is affirmed.
