## INTRODUCTION

Plaintiff Gary Ross appeals from the trial court's dismissal of his claims alleging disability discrimination and wrongful termination in violation of public policy. The facts, as pled in the Complaint and accepted as true for purposes of demurrer, establish that Plaintiff used marijuana pursuant to a physician's recommendation under California's Compassionate Use Act, was terminated from employment after testing positive for THC on a pre-employment drug screen, and now claims his termination violates the California Fair Employment and Housing Act ("FEHA") and constitutes wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The following facts are drawn from Plaintiff's Complaint and are accepted as true for purposes of reviewing the demurrer.

Plaintiff Gary Ross suffers from strain and muscle spasms in his back resulting from injuries sustained during his service in the United States Air Force. Plaintiff is a qualified individual with a disability under the FEHA and receives governmental disability benefits. In September 1999, after failing to obtain relief from pain through other medications, Plaintiff began using marijuana on his physician's recommendation pursuant to the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5).

On September 10, 2001, Defendant RagingWire offered Plaintiff a position as lead systems administrator. As a condition of employment, Defendant required Plaintiff to submit to a preemployment drug test. Before taking the test, Plaintiff provided the testing clinic with a copy of his physician's recommendation for marijuana. Plaintiff took the test on September 14, 2001, and began working on September 17, 2001.

Later that week, the clinic informed Plaintiff that he had tested positive for tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the chemical found in marijuana. On September 20, 2001, Defendant informed Plaintiff he was being suspended as a result of the positive drug test. Plaintiff provided Defendant with a copy of his physician's recommendation and explained that he used marijuana for medical purposes to relieve his chronic back pain. Defendant's representative stated that Defendant would verify the recommendation with Plaintiff's physician and advise Plaintiff of its decision.

On September 21, 2001, Defendant's board of directors met to discuss the matter. On September 25, 2001, Defendant's Chief Executive Officer informed Plaintiff that he was being fired because of his marijuana use.

Plaintiff alleges that his disability and use of marijuana to treat pain do not affect his ability to perform the essential functions of his job. Plaintiff claims he has worked in the same field since beginning to use marijuana and has performed satisfactorily.

Based on these allegations, Plaintiff filed a complaint alleging violations of the FEHA (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (a)) and wrongful termination in violation of public policy. ## STANDARD OF REVIEW

When reviewing a judgment entered after the trial court sustains a demurrer without leave to amend, this Court accepts as true the well-pleaded allegations of material fact but does not accept contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. *Evans v. City of Berkeley* (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6 [40 Cal.Rptr.3d 205, 129 P.3d 394]; *Serrano v. Priest* (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591 [96 Cal.Rptr. 601, 487 P.2d 1241]. The question before this Court is whether Plaintiff can state a cause of action.

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE FEHA DOES NOT REQUIRE EMPLOYERS TO ACCOMMODATE THE USE OF ILLEGAL DRUGS, INCLUDING MEDICAL MARIJUANA

#### A. The FEHA's Protection Does Not Extend to Illegal Drug Use

The central question before this Court is whether the FEHA protects an employee's use of marijuana—even with a physician's recommendation under state law—when such use violates federal law. The answer is no.

The California Supreme Court squarely addressed this issue in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], holding that "the FEHA does not require an employer to accommodate the use of illegal drugs." This holding controls the present case.

The FEHA prohibits employment discrimination against qualified individuals with disabilities. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (a).) However, the statute does not protect the use of illegal drugs. As the Supreme Court explained in *Loder*, the legislative history of the FEHA demonstrates that the California Legislature intentionally declined to extend protection to illegal drug use. *Loder*, 14 Cal.4th at 882-883.

#### B. Marijuana Remains Illegal Under Federal Law

Despite California's Compassionate Use Act, marijuana remains prohibited under federal law. The United States Supreme Court has repeatedly confirmed this. In *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195], the Supreme Court held that the federal Controlled Substances Act encompasses marijuana grown for personal medical use pursuant to state law, and that Congress's prohibition extends to such use. The Court expressly rejected the argument that state authorization of medical marijuana creates an exception to federal law. *Id.*

Similarly, in *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers' Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711], the Supreme Court held that there is no medical necessity exception to the federal prohibition on marijuana possession. The Court emphasized that "the Controlled Substances Act reflects a federal policy that is not dependent on state law" and that "the [Controlled Substances Act] reflects a comprehensive regime to combat the international and domestic traffic in illicit drugs." *Id.* at 494.

Thus, under controlling Supreme Court precedent, Plaintiff's use of marijuana—even with a physician's recommendation under California law—constitutes illegal drug use under federal law.

#### C. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Protections

Plaintiff appears to argue that the Compassionate Use Act creates a right to use marijuana that employers must accommodate. This argument fails.

The Compassionate Use Act (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5) provides that a person who uses marijuana for medical purposes on a physician's recommendation "shall not be subject to criminal prosecution" under California law. (Id., subd. (d).) The statute creates a *defense* to certain state criminal charges involving marijuana possession. It does not create a right to use marijuana in all contexts, nor does it require private employers to accommodate such use.

As the United States Supreme Court recognized in *Raich*, "the California Compassionate Use Act itself declares that, '[n]othing in this section shall be construed to supersede legislation prohibiting or regulating any use of marijuana.'" *Raich*, 545 U.S. at 24 n. 37, quoting Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5, subd. (b)(1). The California electorate that passed Proposition 215 "cannot reasonably be thought to have mandated that the California National Guard be required to facilitate the cultivation, distribution, or use of marijuana for recreational or medicinal purposes in violation of federal law." *Raich*, 545 U.S. at 24.

The same logic applies here. The Compassionate Use Act does not require private employers to tolerate marijuana use. It provides only a limited defense to state criminal prosecution—a defense that has no bearing on private employment relationships.

#### D. Plaintiff's Argument Regarding Job Performance Is Irrelevant

Plaintiff alleges that his marijuana use did not affect his ability to perform his job duties. This allegation is legally irrelevant. The *Loder* court explicitly held that an employer's duty to accommodate under the FEHA does not extend to illegal drug use, regardless of whether the employee can perform their job functions while using the illegal drug. *Loder*, 14 Cal.4th at 882-883. The focus on job performance conflates two distinct inquiries: (1) whether an employee is qualified to perform the essential functions of the job, and (2) whether the employer's requested standard (abstinence from illegal drug use) is legitimate. Here, the second inquiry is dispositive—employers may maintain drug-free workplaces without violating the FEHA.

### II. PLAINTIFF CANNOT STATE A CLAIM FOR WRONGFUL TERMINATION IN VIOLATION OF PUBLIC POLICY

Plaintiff also alleges wrongful termination in violation of public policy, citing the Compassionate Use Act as the source of that public policy. This claim fails because the Compassionate Use Act does not confer a private right of action against employers.

#### A. The Public Policy Exception Requires a Clear Public Policy

California law recognizes a public policy exception to the at-will employment doctrine. (*Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330]; *Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157].) However, this exception requires a "clear public policy" that would be "thwarted" by the termination. (*Tameny*, 27 Cal.3d at 176.) The policy must be "expressed in statutory or constitutional provisions." *Stevenson*, 16 Cal.4th at 887.

The Compassionate Use Act does not express a public policy requiring private employers to accommodate medical marijuana use. As discussed above, the statute provides a defense to criminal prosecution only. It contains no language creating employment rights or imposing obligations on private employers. Indeed, the statute expressly provides that it does not "supersede legislation prohibiting or regulating any use of marijuana." (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5, subd. (b)(1).)

#### B. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create a Private Right of Action

The Compassionate Use Act contains no express or implied private right of action against employers. California's Supreme Court has consistently held that the public policy exception to at-will employment does not apply where the statute at issue does not provide a private right of action and was not intended to regulate employment relationships. (*See, e.g., Stevenson*, 16 Cal.4th at 894-895 [holding that the statute regulating medical malpractice did not create a public policy protecting employees from termination for reporting unsafe practices].)

Here, the Compassionate Use Act was intended to provide a defense to criminal prosecution for seriously ill patients. It was not intended to regulate private employment decisions. Therefore, Plaintiff cannot base a wrongful termination claim on the Compassionate Use Act.

### III. THE DEFENDANT'S CONDUCT WAS LAWFUL AND LEGITIMATE

The facts establish that Defendant acted lawfully and reasonably. Defendant required Plaintiff to submit to a preemployment drug test—a common and lawful employment practice in California. (*Loder*, 14 Cal.4th at 882-883 [employer may require preemployment drug tests and consider illegal drug use in employment decisions].) When the test revealed marijuana use, Defendant investigated by requesting verification of Plaintiff's physician's recommendation. Only after confirming the situation did Defendant make the decision to terminate Plaintiff's employment.

Defendant's decision was based on a legitimate concern: maintaining a drug-free workplace. This concern is not only lawful but reflects important public policy. Drug-free workplace policies protect employees, customers, and the public from the risks associated with drug use. Employers who maintain such policies are not discriminating against disabled employees—they are enforcing lawful workplace standards.

Plaintiff has not alleged that Defendant failed to hire him because of his disability. To the contrary, Defendant offered Plaintiff the job and would have employed him but for the positive drug test. Plaintiff's termination resulted from his decision to use an illegal drug—a decision that, however motivated by medical need, remains unlawful under federal law.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the trial court sustaining the demurrer without leave to amend and entering judgment in favor of Defendant.

The FEHA does not require employers to accommodate the use of illegal drugs. Marijuana remains prohibited under federal law, regardless of California's Compassionate Use Act. The Compassionate Use Act provides only a defense to state criminal prosecution and does not create protected employment rights. Plaintiff cannot state a cause of action for disability discrimination or wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

The trial court correctly applied the law to the facts of this case. This Court should affirm.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

This case presents a question of law: whether California law requires a private employer to accommodate an employee's use of marijuana, even though such use remains illegal under federal law. The answer, as established by binding precedent, is no.

The facts, as alleged in the Plaintiff's complaint and admitted for purposes of demurrer, are as follows:

Respondent RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc., offered the Plaintiff, Gary Ross, a job as lead systems administrator on September 10, 2001. As a condition of employment, the Respondent required the Plaintiff to submit to a preemployment drug test. The Plaintiff had previously informed the testing clinic that he possessed a physician's recommendation for marijuana use pursuant to the Compassionate Use Act of 1996. On September 14, 2001, the Plaintiff submitted to the drug test. On September 17, 2001, he began working for the Respondent. Later that week, the clinic informed the Plaintiff that he had tested positive for tetrahydrocannabinol (THC). On September 20, 2001, the Respondent suspended the Plaintiff based on the positive drug test. The Plaintiff provided the Respondent with a copy of his physician's recommendation and explained that he used marijuana for medical purposes to treat chronic back pain resulting from injuries sustained during his service in the United States Air Force. On September 25, 2001, the Respondent terminated the Plaintiff's employment because of his marijuana use.

The Plaintiff does not allege that his marijuana use affected his ability to perform the essential functions of his job. Indeed, he alleges that he "has worked in the same field since he began to use marijuana and has performed satisfactorily, without complaints about his job performance."

---

## II. LEGAL ARGUMENT

### A. The FEHA Does Not Require Employers to Accommodate the Use of Illegal Drugs

The central question in this case is whether the California Fair Employment and Housing Act requires a private employer to accommodate an employee's use of marijuana for medical purposes, even though such use remains prohibited under federal law. This Court should answer this question in the negative.

In *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883, this Court held that "the FEHA does not require an employer to accommodate the use of illegal drugs." The Court further stated that "an employer may lawfully require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." (*Id.* at 883.)

The Plaintiff's complaint acknowledges that he used marijuana. Regardless of whether he used marijuana pursuant to a physician's recommendation under California law, such use constitutes illegal drug use under federal law. The Controlled Substances Act, 21 U.S.C. sections 812 and 844(a), prohibits the possession and use of marijuana. The United States Supreme Court has confirmed that this prohibition extends to marijuana used for medical purposes in accordance with state law. In *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29, the Court held that the federal Controlled Substances Act "reflects a comprehensive regime to combat the international and domestic traffic in illicit drugs" and that "federal law preempts state authorization of marijuana use." Similarly, in *United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers' Cooperative* (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495, the Court rejected the argument that a medical necessity exception exists to the federal prohibition on marijuana.

As this Court stated in *Loder*, "the focus is not on whether the employee can perform the job while using the illegal drug, but on whether the drug use itself is illegal under federal law." (*Loder*, 14 Cal.4th at 883.) The Plaintiff's argument that his marijuana use did not affect his job performance is therefore legally irrelevant. The dispositive factor is the illegality of the drug under federal law.

The Compassionate Use Act of 1996, Health and Safety Code section 11362.5, provides a defense to certain state criminal charges involving marijuana. It does not, however, change the fact that marijuana remains illegal under federal law. As the United States Supreme Court observed in *Gonzales v. Raich*, 545 U.S. at 24 n.37, "the California electorate" cannot be thought to have "mandated compliance with state law in violation of federal law."

Because the Plaintiff's use of marijuana constitutes illegal drug use under federal law, the FEHA does not require the Respondent to accommodate it. The Respondent therefore did not violate the FEHA by terminating the Plaintiff based on his positive drug test.

### B. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create a Public Policy Exception to At-Will Employment

The Plaintiff also asserts a claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. To establish such a claim, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the policy is "clearly expressed in statutory or constitutional provisions" and that it affects the employer's relationship with the employee. *Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887; *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170.

The Compassionate Use Act does not establish a public policy that supports a wrongful termination claim. The Act provides only a defense to criminal prosecution under state law. It does not create a right to use marijuana in the employment context. Indeed, the Act includes an express provision stating that it shall not be construed to "supersede legislation prohibiting or regulating any use of marijuana." Health and Safety Code section 11362.5, subdivision (b)(1).

The Act was intended to protect patients and physicians from criminal liability, not to regulate private employment decisions or to require private employers to accommodate conduct that remains federally illegal. As this Court noted in *Stevenson*, 16 Cal.4th at 894-895, the public policy exception to at-will employment requires a clear expression of policy in statutory or constitutional provisions. The Compassionate Use Act contains no such expression regarding employment.

Because the Compassionate Use Act does not establish a public policy that affects the employer-employee relationship, the Plaintiff cannot state a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

### C. The Respondent's Conduct Was Lawful

The Respondent's conduct was entirely lawful. The Respondent required a preemployment drug test, a practice explicitly sanctioned by *Loder v. City of Glendale*, 14 Cal.4th at 882-883. Upon receiving a positive test result for THC, the Respondent terminated the Plaintiff based on his use of an illegal substance. This action was consistent with the Respondent's legitimate interest in maintaining a drug-free workplace and did not violate the FEHA or public policy.

The Plaintiff was an at-will employee. Under California law, an employer may terminate an at-will employee for any reason or no reason, as long as the reason is not unlawful. *Id.* at 170. The Respondent's decision to terminate the Plaintiff based on his illegal drug use was lawful.

---

## III. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

The Plaintiff's closing statement attempts to frame this case as one involving sympathy for a disabled veteran. While the Court may sympathize with the Plaintiff's medical condition, sympathy cannot override binding precedent. The Plaintiff correctly concedes that he cannot state a cause of action under the FEHA. This concession is well-founded.

The Plaintiff's argument that his marijuana use did not affect his job performance is legally irrelevant. As this Court held in *Loder*, the focus is not on job performance but on whether the drug use is illegal under federal law. The Plaintiff cannot circumvent this holding by arguing that he performed his job well. If the law were otherwise, employers would be unable to enforce drug-free workplace policies, undermining the federal government's efforts to combat illicit drug use.

The Plaintiff's reliance on the Compassionate Use Act is misplaced. The Act provides a defense to state criminal charges; it does not create a right to use marijuana in all contexts. The Act expressly states that it shall not be construed to supersede legislation regulating marijuana use. Health and Safety Code section 11362.5, subdivision (b)(1). The California electorate, in enacting the Compassionate Use Act, did not intend to regulate private employment decisions.

Finally, the Plaintiff's wrongful termination claim fails because the Compassionate Use Act does not establish a public policy affecting the employer-employee relationship. The Act was designed to protect patients and physicians from criminal liability, not to create a cause of action against private employers.
