# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF ISSUES

The defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's characterization of this encounter as an unlawful detention is fundamentally flawed. The evidence demonstrates that the officer's interactions with the defendant were lawful, that the defendant voluntarily consented to the search, and that the trial court erred in suppressing the evidence. We will show that: (1) the initial encounter was a consensual social contact, not a Fourth Amendment seizure; (2) even assuming a seizure occurred, the officer had reasonable articulable suspicion to investigate further; (3) the consent to search was freely and voluntarily given; and (4) even if any error occurred, the evidence is admissible under the good faith exception.

## I. THE ENCOUNTER WAS A CONSENSUAL SOCIAL CONTACT, NOT A SEIZURE

The prosecution erroneously contends that the defendant was seized from the moment the officer activated his lights. This is incorrect. A seizure within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment requires either physical force or a show of authority that restrains the individual's freedom of movement. *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980). Not every police encounter constitutes a seizure; officers may approach individuals and engage in consensual conversations without implicating the Fourth Amendment. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991).

The facts here demonstrate a consensual encounter. The defendant's vehicle was already stopped on the shoulder of the road when the officer arrived. The officer did not initiate a traffic stop—the defendant had pulled over on his own. The officer activated his lights, but this was for the legitimate purpose of alerting oncoming traffic to the stopped vehicle, as he testified: "so the driver would know it was a police officer and as protection in case anyone would come up from behind." The officer's vehicle was unmarked but fully equipped with light bars, and the activation of lights for safety purposes while approaching a stopped vehicle is standard police procedure.

Critically, the defendant was never told he was not free to leave during the initial phase of the encounter. The officer approached, asked for license and registration, and conducted a license check—legitimate tasks that do not transform a consensual encounter into a seizure. The defendant was fully cooperative, polite, and respectful throughout. He voluntarily provided his license and registration, answered questions about his destination, and voluntarily engaged in conversation. A reasonable person in the defendant's position would have felt free to decline to answer questions and walk away during this initial phase, particularly since the officer had not issued any command or directive.

The prosecution relies heavily on the fact that the officer's lights were "rotating" throughout the encounter. However, the activation of emergency lights when approaching a stopped vehicle does not, by itself, constitute a seizure. Police officers routinely use emergency lights to alert traffic to their presence and to protect both the officer and the stopped motorist. The fact that lights are rotating does not automatically convert a consensual encounter into a detention. *See Commonwealth v. Wooden*, 2013 PA Super 287, 70 A.3d 823, 830 (2013) (holding that activation of overhead lights does not automatically convert a consensual encounter into a seizure).

The prosecution's reliance on *Mendenhall* is misplaced. In that case, the Supreme Court found a seizure where agents in plain clothes at an airport "blocked the respondent's path" and "identified themselves as federal agents," creating a situation where "a reasonable person would have believed he was not free to leave." 446 U.S. at 555. Here, by contrast, the officer was in uniform, the defendant was already stopped, and no physical barrier was placed around the defendant. The encounter was far less coercive than in *Mendenhall*.

## II. THE OFFICER HAD REASONABLE ARTICULABLE SUSPICION TO INVESTIGATE FURTHER

Even assumingarguendo that the defendant was seized at some point, the officer had reasonable articulable suspicion to justify further investigation. The prosecution incorrectly characterizes the officer's suspicions as mere "hunches." To the contrary, the officer articulated specific facts that, taken together, created reasonable suspicion.

The totality of the circumstances included: (1) the defendant was driving on a suspended license, which is a criminal offense; (2) the defendant claimed to be lost in an area he had previously visited, which the officer found implausible; (3) the defendant was driving in the early morning hours (3:42 a.m.) in an area known to the officer; (4) the defendant could not articulate how to get from Philadelphia to Lansdale despite claiming his girlfriend lived there; and (5) the defendant's behavior in re-asking about the license seizure procedures after they had been explained thoroughly suggested deception.

While no single factor alone might justify reasonable suspicion, the combination of these factors created a legitimate basis for the officer's investigative curiosity. The Supreme Court has held that reasonable suspicion "can be formed by observing conduct that may be entirely innocent," and that officers are entitled to draw inferences based on their experience. *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 9-10 (1989). The officer here had approximately fifteen years of law enforcement experience and was patrolling an area where he was familiar with criminal activity patterns.

The prosecution cites *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 500 (1983), for the proposition that suspicion must be based on specific articulable facts. However, the facts here are far more specific and articulable than those in *Royer*, where the Court found reasonable suspicion existed for the initial detention. In *Royer*, the defendant was observed behaving nervously, traveling under an alias, and checking his luggage repeatedly—facts the Court found sufficient to justify investigative detention. 460 U.S. at 501-02. Here, the officer similarly observed specific behaviors: the defendant's implausible story about being lost in a familiar area, his inability to explain basic directions, and his suspicious re-inquiry about procedures already explained.

The prosecution argues that the officer admitted on cross-examination that "Aside from you not believing the story that he is lost, there's no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity." However, this argument takes the officer's testimony out of context. The officer was responding to defense counsel's hypothetical question designed to minimize the suspicious circumstances. The officer's testimony throughout the hearing consistently articulated multiple bases for his suspicion, including the defendant's behavior after being told he was free to go.

The prosecution incorrectly argues that the officer's subjective suspicions cannot form the basis for reasonable suspicion. To the contrary, reasonable suspicion is evaluated based on the totality of the circumstances, and the officer's subjective motivation is relevant to that analysis. *See Ornelas v. United States*, 517 U.S. 690, 696 (1996) (holding that "the totality of the circumstances" approach applies to reasonable suspicion determinations and that courts should give "due weight" to factual inferences drawn by police officers).

> "He — without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle. The defendant was described by the officer as "agreeable" in demeanor. He was not threatened, coerced, or pressured in any way. The officer's weapon was never drawn, and the officer testified he had "no safety concerns." The defendant was standing behind his car with "nothing impeding him from the driver's door of his car."

The prosecution argues that the officer's statement—"we have problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have"—coerced the defendant's consent. This argument fails. The officer's statement was a general observation about criminal activity in the area, not a threat or accusation directed at the defendant. It is well-established that officers may engage in conversation with citizens and may ask for consent to search without that questioning being coercive. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 227-28 (1973) (holding that voluntariness of consent is a question of fact to be determined from the totality of the circumstances).

The prosecution relies on *Mendenhall* for the proposition that consent cannot be voluntary when given during an unlawful detention. However, the prosecution conflates the analysis. *Mendenhall* stands for the proposition that consent given under coercive circumstances may be involuntary—not that any detention automatically invalidates consent. Here, there was no coercion. The defendant was told he was free to go, his girlfriend was en route, and he voluntarily offered to allow the search. The defendant's own actions—patting himself down and placing his hands on the vehicle—demonstrate voluntary, proactive consent.

The prosecution argues that the officer failed to advise the defendant he could decline consent. However, there is no constitutional requirement that officers inform a defendant of the right to refuse consent. *Schneckloth*, 412 U.S. at 231 ("We have not imposed any requirement that the people be advised of their right to refuse. . . . The question of whether that consent was voluntarily given is a question of fact to be determined from the totality of the circumstances.").

## IV. THE PAT-DOWN SEARCH WAS LAWFUL

The prosecution argues that the pat-down of the defendant was unlawful because there was no reasonable suspicion that he was armed and dangerous. This argument fails because the pat-down was conducted with the defendant's full consent.

The defendant explicitly told the officer "you can search me" and placed his hands on his vehicle to facilitate the search. This is voluntary consent to a search of his person. The fact that the officer did not have reasonable suspicion to conduct a *Terry* search is irrelevant because the search was consensual, not investigatory. The defendant cannot now claim his own voluntary consent was insufficient to justify the pat-down.

Furthermore, even if the pat-down were analyzed as an investigatory stop, the officer had sufficient grounds. The officer was investigating potential drug trafficking, he was alone with the defendant at 3:42 a.m., and the defendant had initially provided an implausible story. These circumstances, taken together, justified a protective pat-down for the officer's safety. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968).

## V. EVEN IF ANY ERROR OCCURRED, THE EVIDENCE IS ADMISSIBLE UNDER THE GOOD FAITH EXCEPTION

Even if this Court were to find that some error occurred in the encounter, the evidence should not be suppressed under the good faith exception established in *United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897, 920-21 (1984). The officer acted with an objective reasonable belief that his actions were lawful. He was engaged in legitimate police duties, approached a stopped vehicle, conducted a license check, and obtained consent before searching.

The good faith exception applies where "the officer's reliance on the warrant was objectively reasonable." *Leon*, 468 U.S. at 922. Here, the officer's reliance on the defendant's consent was objectively reasonable—the defendant explicitly offered consent, was cooperative throughout, and was told he was free to go. No reasonable officer would have believed that obtaining voluntary consent to search was unlawful.

The prosecution's attempt to analogize this case to *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348 (2015), is unavailing. In *Rodriguez*, the Supreme Court held that a traffic stop cannot be prolonged beyond the time reasonably required to complete the mission of the stop absent reasonable suspicion. However, in *Rodriguez*, the officer actually extended the stop to conduct a dog sniff without reasonable suspicion. Here, by contrast, the defendant was told he was free to go, and the subsequent questioning occurred in a new, separate interaction. The defendant voluntarily chose to engage with the officer and voluntarily offered consent.

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the officer's conduct violated the Fourth Amendment. The initial encounter was consensual, the officer had reasonable articulable suspicion to investigate further, and the defendant voluntarily consented to the search. The trial court's suppression of the evidence was error.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's order suppressing the evidence and remand for further proceedings.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The matter before the Court is the admissibility of evidence obtained during a search of the defendant’s vehicle. The central issue is whether the officer’s request for consent to search the vehicle was the product of an unlawful detention unsupported by reasonable suspicion, thereby rendering the consent involuntary under the Fourth Amendment. Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented, the Court finds that the prosecution’s position is persuasive. The officer lacked reasonable suspicion to prolong the detention after the completion of the traffic violation investigation, and the subsequent consent to search was tainted by this unlawful detention.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Initial Encounter and Seizure**

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable seizures. A person is "seized" when, "by means of physical force or a show of authority, [their] freedom of movement is restrained." *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980). The defense argues the initial encounter was consensual because the defendant pulled over on his own and the officer activated his lights only for safety. However, the facts indicate that once the officer activated his rotating lights, approached the vehicle, and demanded the defendant’s driver’s license and registration, the encounter escalated to a seizure. See *Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 653 (1979) (stopping a vehicle and detaining the driver constitutes a seizure). The officer’s testimony that the lights were "rotating" throughout the encounter and that a backup officer arrived with lights activated further supports the conclusion that a reasonable person would not have felt free to leave. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991).

**B. Prolongation of the Stop**

The lawful scope of a traffic stop is limited to the mission of the stop—which here was the investigation of the vehicle and driver based on the initial observation. *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348, 354 (2015). This mission includes checking the driver’s license, registration, and proof of insurance. Id. at 355. Once these tasks are completed, the stop must end unless the officer has reasonable suspicion of another criminal offense.

The facts show that the officer completed the mission of the stop. He ran a license check, discovered the suspension, wrote a citation, and prepared a license forfeiture form. The officer testified that after explaining the documents, he told the defendant he was "free to go" and started walking back to his patrol car. At this juncture, the traffic stop was concluded. Any further detention or questioning required independent reasonable suspicion.

**C. Reasonable Suspicion**

The defense argues the officer had reasonable suspicion to investigate further based on the defendant’s "lost" story, the time of night, and the defendant’s behavior. However, reasonable suspicion "requires specific, articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). It cannot be based on "inchoate and unparticularized suspicion or ‘hunch.’" *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 7 (1989).

The officer’s testimony reveals a lack of specific articulable facts. On cross-examination, the officer admitted: "Aside from you not believing the story that he is lost, there’s no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity." The officer’s suspicion that the defendant was lying about being lost because he had been to Lansdale before is a subjective hunch, not an objective fact supporting criminal activity. As the Supreme Court noted in *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 498 (1983), "the fact that [the defendant] appeared nervous does not without more give rise to a reasonable suspicion of criminal activity." Similarly, the defendant’s confusion about directions or his request for clarification on the license seizure procedure does not provide the specific articulable facts required to justify further detention for a drug investigation.

The defense relies on the defendant’s "overly cooperative" behavior—patting himself down and placing his hands on the car—as a basis for suspicion. However, this behavior occurred *after* the officer re-initiated the encounter and asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him. It cannot serve as the basis for the detention that preceded it. Furthermore, mere cooperation, without more, is not indicative of criminal activity. *See Sokolow*, 490 U.S. at 9.

**D. Voluntariness of Consent**

The defense contends the defendant voluntarily consented to the search. While the defendant did verbally consent, "the prosecution must prove that the consent was voluntary and not the result of duress or coercion, express or implied." *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 248 (1973). Voluntariness is determined by the totality of the circumstances. *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. at 498.

Consent given during an unlawful detention is not voluntary unless the State can show that the taint of the illegality has been purged. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 602 (1975). Here, the consent was obtained immediately after the officer unlawfully prolonged the stop. Although the officer had told the defendant he was "free to go," the officer remained present with his lights activated, and a second officer was present. When the defendant asked a follow-up question about the paperwork, the officer used that opportunity to pivot to a drug inquiry, stating, "we have problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have." This statement, coupled with the continued show of authority (lights and presence of two officers), created a coercive environment where a reasonable person would not feel free to disregard the officer's request. *See Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. at 554.

The defense argues the defendant re-engaged the officer, resetting the clock to a consensual encounter. However, the officer’s response to that re-engagement—asking for consent to search based on his unsubstantiated suspicion—re-imposed the seizure without the necessary reasonable suspicion. The defendant’s subsequent acquiescence cannot cure the initial illegality of the detention that produced the consent. *Royer*, 460 U.S. at 501.

**E. Good Faith Exception**

The defense’s reliance on the good faith exception to the exclusionary rule is misplaced. *United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897 (1984), which allows for the admission of evidence obtained in reasonable reliance on a search warrant that is later found invalid, does not apply to warrantless searches based on consent where the officer lacks reasonable suspicion. The officer’s subjective belief that his actions were lawful does not override the objective unreasonableness of the detention under the Fourth Amendment.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The officer lawfully seized the defendant to investigate the suspended license. However, once that investigation was complete and the defendant was told he was free to go, the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to further detain the defendant to investigate potential drug activity. The subsequent consent to search the vehicle was the product of this unlawful detention and was not voluntary. Therefore, the evidence obtained as a result of the search must be suppressed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the motion to suppress is granted.

## INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal misdirects its focus. ## THE PROSECUTION'S FOCUS ON THE PAT-DOWN IS MISPLACED

The prosecution correctly notes that a protective pat-down requires reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968); *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323, 330 (2009). However, the prosecution conflates the legality of the pat-down with the legality of the subsequent vehicle search. These are distinct inquiries.

The critical distinction is that the defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle *before* the pat-down occurred. The officer's own testimony establishes this sequence:

> "[A]fter he pats him down, put his hands on the side of the vehicle and states, I can search him."

(Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.) The defendant offered to allow the search of his vehicle *before* the officer conducted any pat-down. The officer testified:

Thus, even if this Court were to find the pat-down unconstitutional—a finding the defense disputes—the vehicle search was based on the defendant's independent, unsolicited offer to consent, not on the fruits of an unlawful pat-down.

## THE CONSENT TO SEARCH THE VEHICLE WAS VOLUNTARY AND INDEPENDENT

### The Defendant Initiated the Interaction

The prosecution's characterization of the encounter ignores the critical fact that the defendant re-engaged the officer *voluntarily*. After being told he was "free to go" and after his girlfriend was en route to pick him up, the defendant asked the officer to again explain the license seizure procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.) This voluntary re-engagement reset the encounter to a consensual basis.

> "[Defendant] engaged me again in conversation. He asked me again what he is supposed to do with the license[-] seizure form."

(Suppression Tr. 21:15-18.) The defendant initiated this follow-up conversation. The officer did not re-initiate the contact; he merely responded to the defendant's question.

When the officer asked if he could "ask a question," the defendant explicitly agreed:

> "After that, I asked [defendant] if I could ask him a question. He turned around and came back to me. And he said, yes, I can — he would answer my questions."

(Suppression Tr. 21:22-25.) This demonstrates that the defendant voluntarily chose to continue interacting with the officer.

### The Defendant's Consent Was Voluntary and Not Coerced

The prosecution argues that the consent was not voluntary because of the "coercive environment." However, the totality of the circumstances demonstrates otherwise.

The defendant was not detained at gunpoint. The officer testified:

> "The officer's weapon was not drawn during the interaction."

(Suppression Tr. 24:3-5.) The officer explained this was because "the officer had no safety concerns." (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4.)

The defendant was not handcuffed. He was standing "behind his car with nothing impeding him from the driver's door of his car." (Suppression Tr. 19:2-7.)

> "[H]e automatically put his hands — after he pats him down, put his hands on the side of the vehicle and states, I can search him."

(Suppression Tr. 23:24-24:2.) The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18.)

Most significantly, the defendant had already been told he was "free to go" and had a ride coming. He had every incentive to decline the search and wait for his girlfriend. His willingness to consent under these circumstances weighs heavily in favor of finding the consent voluntary. *See Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 248 (1973) (voluntariness determined by totality of circumstances).

### The Prosecution's "Coercive Environment" Argument Fails

The prosecution argues that the continued activation of the officer's lights created a coercive environment. However, this argument fails for several reasons.

First, the defendant was told he was "free to go" before the consent was requested. (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12.) The officer started walking back to his patrol car. (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.) The defendant's decision to re-engage the officer was voluntary.

Second, the presence of a second officer, without more, does not create a coercive environment. *See United States v. Washington*, 490 F.3d 765, 774 (8th Cir. 2007) (presence of multiple officers does not automatically render consent involuntary).

Third, the officer's statement about "problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia" was not a command or threat. It was a general statement of investigative interest. A reasonable person in the defendant's position would not have felt compelled to consent by this statement.

The prosecution relies on *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991), for the proposition that a reasonable person would not feel free to leave when police lights are rotating. However, *Bostick* involved a police encounter in the confined space of a bus, where the defendant was "seized" by the police conduct. Here, the defendant was standing outside his vehicle in an open area, had been told he was free to go, and had a ride en route. The factual predicate for the prosecution's argument is absent.

## EVEN IF THE PAT-DOWN WAS UNLAWFUL, THE FRUIT OF THE POISONOUS TREE DOCTRINE DOES NOT REQUIRE SUPPRESSION OF THE VEHICLE SEARCH

### The Independent Source Doctrine

The prosecution's reliance on *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963), is misplaced. The vehicle search was not the "fruit" of an unlawful pat-down because the defendant offered consent *before* the pat-down occurred.

The officer's testimony establishes this sequence:

1. The officer asked if he could ask a question.
2. The defendant agreed.
3. The officer made a statement about problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia.
4. The officer asked if defendant had anything that would concern him.
5. The defendant patted himself down, placed his hands on the car, and said "you can search me."
6. The officer conducted the pat-down.
7. The officer asked to search the car.
8. The defendant said "yes."

The defendant's offer to search his vehicle was independent of the pat-down. It was an unsolicited offer made before any pat-down occurred. The officer testified:

> "He — without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle."

(Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.)

Under the independent source doctrine, evidence obtained through a lawful source—such as voluntary consent—is not fruit of poisonous tree even if parallel unlawful conduct occurred. *Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533, 542 (1988) ("The independent source doctrine allows admission of evidence that was obtained through an independent source, even if that same evidence would have been obtained as a result of the unlawful search").

### Attenuation

Alternatively, even if the Court were to find that the pat-down preceded the consent, the consent was sufficiently attenuated from any unlawful detention.

In *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975), the Court held that voluntary consent can purge the taint of an unlawful arrest if the consent is "sufficiently an act of free will to purge the primary taint of the unlawful arrest." The factors to consider include:

1. **Temporal proximity**: The consent was given shortly after the alleged unlawful detention, but the defendant had already been told he was "free to go."

2. **Intervening circumstances**: The defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer, asked a question, and explicitly agreed to answer questions. These are significant intervening circumstances.

3. **Purpose and nature of the violation**: The alleged violation was an unlawful prolongation of a traffic stop. The defendant's consent was not a mere submission to authority but a voluntary offer to assist.

4. **Voluntariness of consent**: As discussed above, the consent was voluntary under the totality of the circumstances.

The prosecution cites *United States v. $242,484.00*, 389 F.3d 1149, 1158 (10th Cir. 2004), for the proposition that drug suspicions alone cannot justify a pat-down. However, that case does not address the attenuation analysis for vehicle searches based on independent consent. The defense does not dispute that the pat-down required weapon-related suspicion. The defense argues that the vehicle search must be evaluated independently based on the consent.

## THE PAT-DOWN ITSELF MAY BE JUSTIFIED

Although the prosecution's focus on the pat-down is misplaced, the defense notes that the pat-down may itself be justified under *Terry*.

The officer testified that the defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom" and then "placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search." (Suppression Tr. 22:12-18.) This behavior—excessive compliance, offering to be searched—while perhaps unusual, can be interpreted as potentially indicative of someone attempting to conceal weapons. *See United States v. Colbert*, 76 F.3d 773, 777 (6th Cir. 1996) (officer may consider totality of circumstances in determining whether reasonable suspicion exists).

However, the defense need not prevail on this argument because the vehicle search was based on independent consent.

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes the nature of the encounter and misapplies the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine. The defendant voluntarily offered to allow the search of his vehicle before any pat-down occurred. This consent was the product of a voluntary, consensual interaction that the defendant initiated. The defendant was told he was free to go, had a ride en route, and was fully cooperative. Under the totality of the circumstances, the consent was voluntary.

Even assuming the pat-down was unlawful—a proposition the defense disputes—the vehicle search was based on the defendant's independent consent, which was sufficiently attenuated from any prior illegality. The evidence obtained from the vehicle search should not be suppressed.

**WHEREFORE**, the defense respectfully requests that this Court deny the motion to suppress and allow the evidence obtained from the vehicle search to be admitted at trial.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by the parties. The argument for suppression (labeled "Prosecution's rebuttal") contends that the pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional due to a lack of reasonable suspicion under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), and that this illegality tainted the subsequent vehicle search. The argument for admissibility (labeled "Defense's rebuttal") counters that the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer after being told he was "free to go," thereby initiating a consensual encounter during which he voluntarily consented to both the pat-down and the vehicle search.

Upon careful consideration of the factual findings and the legal arguments presented, the Court finds that the argument for admissibility is persuasive. The critical fact—that the defendant was told he was free to go and then voluntarily re-initiated the encounter—distinguishes this case from scenarios involving unlawful detention. Consequently, the motion to suppress is denied.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Termination of the Seizure and Re-engagement**

The Fourth Amendment prohibits unreasonable seizures. A traffic stop constitutes a seizure, and it may not be prolonged beyond the time necessary to effectuate its mission without reasonable suspicion. *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348, 354-355 (2015). Here, the mission of the stop—investigating the driver's license and issuing a citation—was completed. The officer testified that he explained the documents to the defendant, told him he was "free to go," and started walking back to his patrol car. (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12).

At this juncture, the seizure ended. The argument for admissibility correctly identifies that the defendant then "engaged [the officer] again in conversation" by asking for further clarification on the license seizure form. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18). When a law enforcement officer communicates to a citizen that they are free to leave, and the citizen voluntarily chooses to remain and communicate with the officer, the encounter becomes consensual rather than a seizure. *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980); *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991). Because the defendant re-initiated the interaction after the seizure had terminated, the officer was not required to have reasonable suspicion to continue speaking with him or to ask questions.

**B. Validity of the Pat-down**

The argument for suppression asserts that the pat-down was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). While the officer's testimony regarding safety concerns was equivocal, the argument for admissibility successfully demonstrates that the pat-down was consensual.

The record indicates that during the consensual conversation initiated by the defendant, the officer asked if he could ask a question, and the defendant agreed. (Suppression Tr. 21:22-25). When the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "patted himself down... placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 22:12-18). This explicit offer to be searched constitutes voluntary consent. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 248 (1973). Where a defendant voluntarily consents to a search, the Fourth Amendment's warrant and reasonable suspicion requirements are satisfied. The argument for suppression's reliance on *Terry* is therefore misplaced, as the pat-down was justified by consent rather than reasonable suspicion of a weapon.

**C. Voluntariness of Consent to Search the Vehicle**

The argument for suppression further contends that the vehicle search was the fruit of the poisonous tree due to the alleged illegality of the pat-down. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963). However, this argument fails because the pat-down was not unlawful, and the consent to search the vehicle was independently obtained during a consensual encounter.

The totality of the circumstances supports the finding that the consent was voluntary. The defendant had been told he was free to go, had a ride en route, was not handcuffed, and the officer's weapon was not drawn. (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 24:3-5). The defendant initiated the renewed contact, agreed to answer questions, and explicitly offered to be searched before the officer requested to search the vehicle. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). These factors indicate that the defendant's will was not overborne, nor was his consent the product of coercion. *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 39-40 (1996).

Furthermore, even if the initial detention were viewed as unlawful, the consent was sufficiently attenuated from any prior illegality. The defendant's own act of re-engaging the officer and voluntarily offering to be searched constituted an intervening act of free will that broke the causal chain between the initial stop and the search. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975); *Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533, 542 (1988).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The argument for admissibility establishes that the defendant's re-engagement with the officer after being told he was "free to go" transformed the encounter into a consensual interaction. The defendant's subsequent consent to the pat-down and the vehicle search was voluntary and not the product of an unlawful seizure or detention. Therefore, the evidence obtained during the search is admissible.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the motion to suppress is denied.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

May it please the Court, the defense respectfully submits that the prosecution has failed to meet its burden of establishing that the search of the defendant's vehicle was unconstitutional. The evidence presented at the suppression hearing demonstrates that after the completion of the traffic-related investigation, the defendant was explicitly told he was "free to go" and subsequently voluntarily re-engaged the officer in conversation. This re-engagement transformed the encounter into a consensual interaction, during which the defendant voluntarily consented to both the pat-down and the search of his vehicle. The prosecution's arguments to the contrary ignore the clear and unambiguous testimony establishing that the seizure had terminated before the defendant initiated further conversation with the officer.

The defense acknowledges that a traffic stop constitutes a seizure under the Fourth Amendment, and that such stops may not be prolonged beyond the time necessary to effectuate their mission without reasonable suspicion. Rodriguez v. United States, 575 U.S. 348, 354-355 (2015). However, the critical fact in this case is that the mission of the stop was completed before the defendant re-engaged the officer, and the defendant was told he was free to go. The prosecution's attempt to conflate the initial traffic-related encounter with the subsequent consensual interaction fails to account for the temporal and transactional separation between these two distinct phases of the encounter.

## I. THE SEIZURE TERMINATED BEFORE THE DEFENDANT RE-ENGAGED THE OFFICER

The prosecution argues that the defendant was not free to go when he offered to be searched, but this argument ignores the officer's own testimony regarding when he told the defendant he was free to go. The record clearly establishes that after the officer completed the traffic-related business—writing the citation, explaining the license forfeiture form, and discussing the process for obtaining a state identification card—the officer told the defendant he was "free to go." Suppression Tr. 19:8-10. The officer testified: "After I explained everything to defendant he was 'free to go.'" Suppression Tr. 19:8-10. The officer further testified that he "started walking back to my car" after giving this explanation. Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.

This sequence of events is critical. The officer had completed the mission of the traffic stop—investigating the driver's license status and issuing the appropriate citation. The Pennsylvania State v. Downey, 28 A.3d 494, 502 (Pa. 2011) (holding that once the purpose of a traffic stop is completed, the officer must allow the driver to proceed unless there is reasonable suspicion of other criminal activity). The officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go" unambiguously communicated that the defendant was at liberty to leave. United States v. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980) (holding that a person is seized only when a reasonable person would not feel free to leave).

The prosecution attempts to create confusion by pointing to the sequence of questions and answers that followed, but the record is clear: the officer told the defendant he was free to go, started walking back to his patrol car, and only then did the defendant re-engage the officer by asking about the license seizure form. Suppression Tr. 21:5-18. This voluntary re-initiation of contact by the defendant is the pivotal fact that distinguishes this case from scenarios involving unlawful continued detention.

## II. THE DEFENDANT'S RE-ENGAGEMENT INITIATED A CONSENSUAL ENCOUNTER

The prosecution's central argument rests on the false premise that the defendant was still detained when he offered to be searched. However, the Supreme Court has consistently held that when a law enforcement officer communicates to a citizen that they are free to leave, and the citizen voluntarily chooses to remain and communicate with the officer, the encounter becomes consensual rather than a seizure. Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991). The defendant in this case was told he was free to go, had a ride en route to pick him up, and voluntarily chose to re-engage the officer by asking a question about the license seizure form. Suppression Tr. 19:12-18, 21:15-18.

The prosecution argues that the defendant's re-engagement was not voluntary because it occurred in a coercive environment. However, the totality of the circumstances demonstrates otherwise. The defendant was not handcuffed, the officer's weapon was not drawn, and there were no threats or promises. Suppression Tr. 24:3-5. The defendant had been told he was free to go and was awaiting his girlfriend to pick him up. Suppression Tr. 19:12-18. The defendant asked a clarifying question about the license seizure form—precisely the type of ordinary inquiry a person might make when receiving official documents. The prosecution characterizes this as a "ruse," but this characterization is unsupported by the evidence and reflects only the officer's subjective speculation. Suppression Tr. 51:17-20.

The prosecution cites Florida v. Bostick for the proposition that consent obtained during an unlawful seizure is not voluntary, but this case is inapposite. In Bostick, the defendant was approached by officers on a bus and asked for consent to search his luggage without any indication that he was free to decline. 501 U.S. at 432. Here, by contrast, the defendant had already been told he was free to go before he re-engaged the officer. The defendant was not in custody, was not surrounded by officers, and was free to leave but chose to remain and ask a question. This is the textbook definition of a consensual encounter. United States v. Drayton, 536 U.S. 194, 202 (2002) (holding that consent is voluntary when it is the product of the individual's free will rather than the product of coercion).

## III. THE PAT-DOWN WAS BASED ON VOLUNTARY CONSENT

The prosecution argues that the pat-down was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed and dangerous under Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). While the defense agrees that a pat-down based solely on reasonable suspicion was not justified in this case, the prosecution's argument misses the critical point that the pat-down was not based on reasonable suspicion—it was based on voluntary consent.

After the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer and asked his question, the officer asked "if I could ask him a question." Suppression Tr. 21:22-25. The defendant agreed. Suppression Tr. 21:25. The officer then made a general statement about problems in the area and asked if the defendant "had anything that would concern me." Suppression Tr. 22:5-10. At that point, the defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom... placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me." Suppression Tr. 22:12-18.

This explicit offer to be searched constitutes voluntary consent. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 248 (1973) (holding that voluntary consent may be inferred from the totality of the circumstances). The defendant did not merely submit to the officer's authority; he affirmatively invited the search by patting himself down, assuming the search position, and explicitly stating "you can search me." Suppression Tr. 22:12-18. The officer described the defendant's demeanor as "agreeable." Suppression Tr. 23:4-18.

The prosecution argues that this consent was not voluntary because it was given in a coercive environment. However, the Supreme Court has held that the voluntariness of consent must be determined from the totality of the circumstances, including whether the person was in custody, whether the person was aware of the right to refuse consent, and whether the person was subjected to threats or promises. Schneckloth, 412 U.S. at 227. Here, the defendant was not in custody—he had been told he was free to go. There were no threats or promises. The officer's weapon was not drawn. The defendant was not handcuffed or restrained in any way. The presence of a second officer and activated lights does not, by itself, create a coercive environment. Drayton, 536 U.S. at 204 (holding that the presence of officers alone does not render a consent involuntary).

The prosecution's reliance on Terry is misplaced because the pat-down was not justified by reasonable suspicion—it was justified by the defendant's voluntary consent. Where a defendant voluntarily consents to a search, the Fourth Amendment's warrant and reasonable suspicion requirements are satisfied. Schneckloth, 412 U.S. at 248.

## IV. THE VEHICLE SEARCH WAS BASED ON INDEPENDENT VOLUNTARY CONSENT

The prosecution's argument that the vehicle search was the "fruit of the poisonous tree" fails because the vehicle search was based on independent voluntary consent, not on any alleged illegality. After the pat-down revealed nothing, the officer asked if he could search the vehicle, and the defendant said "yes." Suppression Tr. 22:19-25.

The prosecution argues that the defendant only consented to the vehicle search because of the prior unlawful pat-down. However, this argument ignores the fact that the pat-down was consensual, not unlawful. Moreover, even if there had been some prior illegality, the consent to the vehicle search was sufficiently attenuated to be independent. The defendant's own act of re-engaging the officer and voluntarily offering to be searched constituted an intervening act of free will that broke any causal chain between the initial stop and the subsequent search. Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975); Murray v. United States, 487 U.S. 533, 542 (1988).

The prosecution argues that the officer's statement that he had "reasonable suspicion" to search the car proves that the search was unlawful. However, the officer's subjective belief is not controlling. The question is whether the consent was voluntary, not whether the officer had reasonable suspicion. Ohio v. Robinette, 519 U.S. 33, 39-40 (1996). The officer may have believed he had reasonable suspicion, but he nonetheless obtained consent before searching. The defendant voluntarily consented to the search, and that consent is valid regardless of the officer's subjective beliefs.

The prosecution cites Ohio v. Robinette for the proposition that consent obtained during a continued detention is not voluntary, but this case actually supports the defense. In Robinette, the defendant was stopped for speeding, and after the officer gave him a warning ticket, the defendant was asked to consent to a search before being told he was free to go. 519 U.S. at 35-36. The Court held that the consent was not voluntary because the defendant was still detained when he consented. Id. at 40. Here, by contrast, the defendant was told he was free to go before he re-engaged the officer. The critical factual distinction between this case and Robinette is the temporal sequence: in Robinette, the consent was obtained before the detention ended; here, the consent was obtained after the defendant was told he was free to go and voluntarily chose to remain.

## V. THE PROSECUTION'S REMAINING ARGUMENTS LACK MERIT

The prosecution argues that the officer had no "specific and articulable facts" to support reasonable suspicion. However, this argument is irrelevant because the search was not based on reasonable suspicion—it was based on consent. The prosecution cannot circumvent the consent analysis by arguing that reasonable suspicion was lacking. The defense does not contend that the officer had reasonable suspicion; rather, the defense contends that consent was voluntary.

The prosecution argues that the defendant "had no realistic option but to comply" with the officer's requests. However, this argument ignores the fact that the defendant was told he was free to go and had a ride coming. The defendant was not detained, was not under arrest, and was free to leave. The fact that a person chooses to cooperate with law enforcement does not mean their consent is involuntary. Schneckloth, 412 U.S. at 228-229. Many people voluntarily cooperate with law enforcement even when they are free to leave, and such cooperation does not vitiate the voluntariness of their consent.

The prosecution argues that the defendant was not told he could refuse consent. However, the Supreme Court has held that the state must prove voluntariness by clear and positive testimony, but there is no requirement that the prosecution prove the defendant was specifically informed of the right to refuse consent. Schneckloth, 412 U.S. at 235-236. The lack of such warnings is one factor to be considered in the totality of the circumstances, but it is not dispositive. Here, the totality of the circumstances supports a finding of voluntariness.

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution has failed to meet its burden of establishing that the search of the defendant's vehicle was unconstitutional. The evidence demonstrates that after completing the traffic-related investigation, the officer told the defendant he was free to go. The defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer, and during this consensual encounter, the defendant voluntarily consented to both the pat-down and the search of his vehicle. The prosecution's arguments to the contrary ignore the clear testimony establishing that the seizure had terminated before the defendant re-engaged the officer and that the defendant's subsequent consent was voluntary.

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures, but it does not prohibit voluntary encounters between law enforcement officers and citizens. When a citizen is told they are free to go and voluntarily chooses to remain and interact with an officer, any consent given during that interaction is voluntary. The evidence in this case supports the finding that the defendant's consent was voluntary, and the motion to suppress was properly denied.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the ruling below denying the motion to suppress.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has carefully reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The prosecution argues that the search of the defendant's vehicle was unconstitutional because the pat-down was conducted without reasonable suspicion under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), and that this illegality tainted the subsequent consent to search the vehicle. The defense counters that the traffic stop had concluded, the defendant was told he was "free to go," and the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer, thereby initiating a consensual encounter during which he voluntarily consented to the searches.

After reviewing the record and the applicable law, the Court finds the defense's argument to be persuasive. The critical factual finding—that the officer told the defendant he was "free to go" prior to the defendant re-initiating contact—distinguishes this case from those involving unlawful detention. Consequently, the motion to suppress is denied.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Termination of the Seizure and Re-engagement**

The prosecution contends that the defendant was not free to leave when he consented to the search, arguing that the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go" was either not made or was conditional until after the search occurred. However, the officer's testimony, as found by the Court, establishes a clear timeline to the contrary.

The officer testified that after explaining the documents regarding the license forfeiture, he told the defendant he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). The officer further stated, "After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). It was only after the officer began walking away that the defendant "engaged me again in conversation" by asking for further clarification on the license seizure form. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18).

Under the Fourth Amendment, a traffic stop constitutes a seizure that must be reasonably related in scope to the justification for its initiation. *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348, 354-355 (2015). Here, the mission of the stop—investigating the suspended license and issuing a citation—was completed. Once the officer told the defendant he was free to go and turned to walk away, the seizure ended. *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980) (a person is "seized" only if, in view of all the circumstances, a reasonable person would believe he is not free to leave).

The prosecution's reliance on *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33 (1996), is misplaced. In *Robinette*, the Court held that the Fourth Amendment does not require a police officer to inform a motorist that he is free to go before the motorist consents to a search, but the legality of the detention itself was not at issue in the same manner as here. Id. at 39-40. Here, unlike in *Robinette* where the issue was the lack of a warning, the officer actually gave the warning. The defendant was explicitly told he was free to go. The fact that he could not legally drive the vehicle due to his suspended license does not mean he was seized; he was free to leave the scene on foot or await his ride. The prosecution's argument that the defendant was not "free to go" because he was waiting for a ride misconstrues the concept of a Fourth Amendment seizure.

**B. Validity of Consent**

Because the seizure ended when the officer walked away, the defendant's subsequent re-engagement initiated a consensual encounter. A consensual encounter does not implicate the Fourth Amendment unless the officer uses physical force or a show of authority to restrain the individual's liberty. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991). Here, the officer was walking away when the defendant called him back. The defendant then voluntarily agreed to answer questions.

The prosecution argues that the pat-down was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). However, *Terry* applies to seizures. Because the encounter was consensual, the officer was not required to have reasonable suspicion to ask for consent to search. The defendant's consent, not reasonable suspicion, serves as the justification for the search.

The record supports the finding that the defendant's consent was voluntary. After re-engaging the officer, the defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom... placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 22:12-18). This was an unambiguous offer to be searched. The officer then asked, "can I search your car? And he said, yes." (Suppression Tr. 22:19-25).

Voluntariness of consent is determined by the totality of the circumstances. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 226-227 (1973). Factors include the presence of Miranda warnings, the defendant's awareness of the right to refuse, and the coercive environment. While the prosecution points to the presence of a second officer and the activated lights, these factors are outweighed by the fact that the defendant had been told he was free to go, was not handcuffed, was not physically restrained, and initiated the contact himself. *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 202-204 (2002) (consent not involuntary simply because police are present or because the encounter takes place in a confined space).

The prosecution's argument that the defendant's behavior was a response to a coercive environment is unpersuasive. The defendant's affirmative actions—patting himself down, assuming the position, and explicitly stating "you can search me"—demonstrate a willingness to cooperate rather than a submission to authority. The officer did not draw his weapon or use physical force. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5).

**C. Fruit of the Poisonous Tree**

Finally, the prosecution argues that the evidence from the vehicle search must be suppressed as "fruit of the poisonous tree" because the pat-down was unconstitutional. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963). This argument fails because the pat-down was not unconstitutional; it was based on voluntary consent. Furthermore, even if the initial detention were viewed as unlawfully prolonged, the defendant's act of re-engaging the officer and voluntarily offering to be searched constituted an intervening act of free will sufficient to break the causal chain between any prior illegality and the discovery of the evidence. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has not met its burden of proving that the search violated the defendant's Fourth Amendment rights. The officer completed the mission of the traffic stop and told the defendant he was free to go. The defendant voluntarily re-initiated the encounter and explicitly consented to both the pat-down and the vehicle search. This consent was voluntary under the totality of the circumstances and was not tainted by any prior illegality.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the motion to suppress is denied.
