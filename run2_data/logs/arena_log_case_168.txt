**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, the central question before this Court is whether *Flor v. Holguin* governs the determination of proximate causation for the aggravation of Mr. Van Ness’s pre-existing asthma condition. The answer is unequivocally **no**. The Director’s decision misapplied the law by applying the *Flor* test to this case, when the correct legal framework—the unitary test—must govern. For the reasons set forth, the Director’s ruling must be reversed because it failed to apply the proper standard for evaluating whether the aggravation arose out of and in the course of employment.  

### I. *Flor v. Holguin* Does Not Apply to Aggravations of Pre-Existing Conditions  
*Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Haw. 203, 58 P.3d 1272 (2002), explicitly limits its "nature of employment" test to cases involving **occupational diseases**—that is, diseases *resulting from* the nature of the employment itself. As the Hawaii Supreme Court held in *Flor*, the test applies only when the disease is "inherent to the job" or "caused by the specific conditions of employment." Id. at 211, 58 P.3d at 1280. This is a narrow rule reserved for cases where employment *creates* the disease, such as silicosis in miners or asbestos-related illnesses in shipyard workers.  

Here, however, Mr. Van Ness suffered from a **pre-existing condition**—mild persistent asthma—that was *aggravated* by external environmental factors. His asthma was not caused by his employment; it existed long before he began working at Lahainaluna High School, as documented in his medical history dating to 1989. Dr. Sweet and Dr. Tom treated his asthma for years prior to his assignment to Maui, and he controlled it effectively with medication during his time in Idaho and on Oahu. The Director correctly acknowledged that "Van Ness’s exposure to vog aggravated his long-standing asthma condition." Yet the Director then improperly applied the *Flor* test—a test designed for diseases *resulting from* employment—to an *aggravation* of a pre-existing condition.  

This error is fatal. Hawaii law is clear: **aggravations of pre-existing conditions are governed by the unitary test**, not the *Flor* test. The unitary test requires only that the injury "arise out of and in the course of employment," meaning employment must be a proximate cause of the aggravation. *Flor* does not apply here because Mr. Van Ness’s condition was not "resulting from" his employment—it was a pre-existing condition worsened by external factors during work.  

### II. The Director Applied the Wrong Standard and Erred in Denying Compensation  
The Director’s decision rests on the flawed premise that because vog was present island-wide, there was "insufficient evidence" of a nexus between employment and injury. But this reasoning conflates the *Flor* test with the unitary test. Under *Flor*, the "nature of employment" test would require proof that vog exposure was "peculiar to the workplace." But *Flor* is inapplicable here. The unitary test—properly applied—does **not** require uniqueness of exposure. It requires only that employment was a **substantial contributing factor** in the aggravation.  

The record is replete with evidence meeting this standard:  
- Mr. Van Ness’s job duties required him to be exposed to vog during work hours while performing physically demanding tasks. He testified he was "forced to stop to catch his breath" while climbing the steep, uneven stairs between campus buildings—a task that "took about five minutes to go down but like 20 minutes to come back." This exertion worsened his breathing difficulties during vog events.  
- Medical evidence directly links his work exposure to the exacerbation. Dr. Tom’s clinical notes from December 23, 2005, referenced "vog" and documented "difficulty breathing and coughing." Dr. Mathison’s consultation report explicitly stated that Mr. Van Ness experienced "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" during "a 40-day spell of rainy weather with vog (moisture, pollutants including volcanic smoke/ash) exposure from about November 2005 and continuing into the spring of 2006." Dr. Mathison further diagnosed "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and warned of "serious and potentially fatal asthma exacerbation with exposure to volcanic products."  
- The timing and location of symptoms confirm employment as a proximate cause. Mr. Van Ness’s symptoms worsened *only* while working on Maui during vog season, improved after he was transferred to Oahu in November 2006, and Dr. Tom’s March 2006 note confirmed symptoms "worsened since moving to Maui due to higher vog exposure."  

The Director’s focus on vog’s island-wide presence ignores the unitary test’s core inquiry: whether employment was a proximate cause. Hawaii law does not demand that the exposure be unique to the workplace. It demands only that employment contributed substantially to the aggravation. *See, e.g., Kekauoha v. Hawaii Dep’t of Educ.*, 108 Haw. 157, 118 P.3d 398 (2005) (holding that "proximate cause" under the unitary test is satisfied when employment is a "substantial contributing factor," even if other factors exist). Here, Mr. Van Ness’s job required him to be in the vog-exposed environment during work hours while performing tasks that intensified his respiratory distress—a clear proximate cause.  

### III. Conclusion  
The Director erred by applying *Flor v. Holguin* to an aggravation of a pre-existing condition. *Flor* governs only diseases *resulting from* employment, not exacerbations of pre-existing conditions. For the latter, the unitary test applies, and the record indisputably shows that employment was a proximate cause of Mr. Van Ness’s aggravated asthma. The Director’s reliance on the island-wide presence of vog—without considering whether employment was a substantial contributing factor—violates well-established Hawaii workers’ compensation law.  

Your Honor, we ask this Court to reverse the Director’s decision and remand for a determination under the correct legal standard. The evidence compels the conclusion that Mr. Van Ness’s injury arose out of and in the course of employment.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Matter of the Workers' Compensation Claim of VAN NESS, Claimant-Appellant

### Introduction

The Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations properly denied Mr. Van Ness's workers' compensation claim. The plaintiff's opening argument mischaracterizes both the applicable legal standard and the record evidence. Contrary to the plaintiff's assertions, the Director did not err in evaluating this claim, and the evidence fully supports the denial of benefits. The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the Director's decision.

### I. The Director Properly Applied the Legal Framework

The plaintiff argues that *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Haw. 203, 58 P.3d 1272 (2002), applies only to occupational diseases "resulting from" employment and that the "unitary test" governs aggravations of pre-existing conditions. This argument, while creative, misreads Hawaii law and ignores the practical reality of workers' compensation causation analysis.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, the Hawaii Supreme Court addressed the causation standard for occupational diseases, holding that when a disease is inherent to or caused by the specific conditions of employment, the claimant must demonstrate that the employment was the proximate cause of the disease. The court recognized that some conditions affect workers generally regardless of where they work, and in such cases, the "nature of employment" test ensures that employment is truly the cause rather than a neutral circumstance.

The Director's decision here did not misapply *Flor*; rather, the Director correctly applied fundamental principles of proximate causation. Even accepting arguendo that the unitary test applies to aggravations of pre-existing conditions, the Director's analysis remains sound because the core requirement—demonstrating that employment was a proximate cause—applies equally under both frameworks.

### II. The Evidence Does Not Establish That Employment Was a Proximate Cause

The plaintiff contends that the evidence overwhelmingly demonstrates that employment was a proximate cause of his asthma aggravation. This assertion fails upon examination of the record.

The Director found, and the evidence establishes, that vog was present throughout the entire island of Maui during the relevant period. The plaintiff himself testified that he lived in Kihei, where Vice Principal Dennis noted "the vog was even worse." The record contains no evidence that the vog concentration at Lahainaluna High School was materially different from vog levels elsewhere on Maui. Dr. Arora specifically noted in his report that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely," accurately observing that vog is an island-wide environmental condition.

Under both the unitary test and the *Flor* framework, employment must be a proximate cause of the injury. Proximate cause requires that the employment be a substantial contributing factor—not merely a background circumstance. Here, the evidence shows that Mr. Van Ness would have been exposed to vog regardless of his employment location. He lived on Maui, worked on Maui, and experienced vog exposure both at work and at home. The plaintiff has not established that his employment at Lahainaluna specifically caused his asthma to worsen any more than his residence on Maui generally.

The plaintiff's reliance on Dr. Mathison's report is misplaced. While Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness experienced asthma exacerbations "with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution," he did not opine that the exposure at Lahainaluna was distinct from or more severe than the exposure Van Ness experienced elsewhere on Maui. The timing of symptoms—worsening during vog season—is consistent with general island-wide exposure, not workplace-specific causation.

### III. The Physical Exertion Theory Does Not Establish Employment Causation

The plaintiff argues that his job duties required significant physical exertion—climbing fifty to sixty stairs, walking between buildings—which worsened his breathing difficulties during vog events. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the physical exertion of climbing stairs is not unique to Lahainaluna High School. Employees in numerous occupations must climb stairs as part of their job duties. The question under workers' compensation law is whether the employment created a risk particular to that employment, not whether the employment required physical activity.

Second, the record shows that Van Ness's breathing difficulties were caused primarily by vog exposure, not by physical exertion. The plaintiff testified that when the vog was severe, he experienced "a lot of coughing, wheezing" and "real shortness of breath." He stated that vog "basically reduced the amount of air I was able to breathe." Dr. Tom's clinical notes from December 23, 2005, documented "difficulty breathing and coughing," and Dr. Mathison's report attributed the exacerbation to "volcanic smoke-ash-pollution." The physical exertion may have compounded his difficulties, but it did not cause the underlying respiratory distress.

Third, even if physical exertion were a contributing factor, the plaintiff has not established that the exertion requirements of his job were unusual or excessive compared to other technology coordinator positions. He acknowledged that he was "the only technology coordinator at the school" responsible for maintaining equipment, but he did not establish that this role differed materially from other school technology positions in ways that would create a unique employment-related risk.

### IV. The Timing of Symptoms Does Not Compel a Finding of Employment Causation

The plaintiff points out that his symptoms worsened while working on Maui during vog season and improved after his transfer to Oahu in November 2006. This temporal correlation, however, does not establish employment causation.

The plaintiff lived on Maui from July 2005 through November 2006. He worked on Maui during this period, but he also resided on Maui. The vog was an island-wide phenomenon affecting all residents, not merely those employed at Lahainaluna. The improvement in symptoms after his transfer to Oahu is equally consistent with the conclusion that residence on Maui generally—and not employment specifically—caused his exacerbations.

Dr. Tom's March 2006 note stated that Van Ness's symptoms "worsened since moving to Maui due to the higher vog exposure there." Notably, Dr. Tom did not state that the symptoms worsened due to employment at Lahainaluna specifically. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant-Appellant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The central issue on appeal is whether the Director erred as a matter of law by applying the causation standard established in *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Haw. 203, 58 P.3d 1272 (2002), to Claimant’s claim for the aggravation of a pre-existing asthma condition, rather than the “unitary test” for injuries arising out of and in the course of employment. Based on the opening arguments submitted by both parties and the record provided, the Court finds that the Director’s decision must be reversed.

**II. APPLICABLE LEGAL STANDARDS**

The Hawaii Supreme Court has established distinct legal frameworks for evaluating workers’ compensation claims depending on whether the injury is an occupational disease or an aggravation of a pre-existing condition.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Haw. 203, 58 P.3d 1272 (2002), the court articulated the “nature of employment” test for occupational diseases. Under *Flor*, a claimant must demonstrate that the disease is inherent to the employment or caused by conditions peculiar to the work environment. This test requires a showing that the employment, rather than general environmental exposure, is the proximate cause of the disease.

Conversely, for injuries involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition, Hawaii law applies the “unitary test.” This test requires only that the injury “arise out of and in the course of employment,” meaning that the employment must be a substantial contributing factor to the injury. *See Kekauoha v. Hawaii Dep’t of Educ.*, 108 Haw. 157, 118 P.3d 398 (2005). The unitary test does not require that the risk be unique to the workplace, nor does it require that the employment be the sole cause of the aggravation.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director Misapplied the *Flor* Standard**

The Director’s decision denying the claim rests on the finding that “vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment.” The Director concluded that because the vog was island-wide, there was “insufficient evidence” to establish that the exposure at Lahainaluna School was the cause of the injury. This reasoning explicitly relies on the premise that the environmental trigger must be unique to the workplace to establish compensability.

As argued by the prosecution, this legal framework is directly at odds with the nature of Claimant’s injury. Claimant does not allege that his asthma was *caused* by his employment; rather, he alleges that his pre-existing mild persistent asthma was *aggravated* by the conditions of his employment. The Director’s focus on the island-wide prevalence of vog mirrors the “nature of employment” inquiry reserved for occupational diseases under *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Haw. 203, 58 P.3d 1272 (2002). By requiring Claimant to show that his exposure at work was so overwhelming it differed from general island exposure, the Director improperly imposed the “peculiar to employment” burden of *Flor* onto an aggravation claim.

**B. The Evidence Supports Compensability Under the Unitary Test**

Under the correct legal standard—the unitary test—the relevant inquiry is whether Claimant’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma. The record provides substantial evidence to support this finding.

Claimant’s employment at Lahainaluna High School required him to perform significant physical exertion that was not required of him in his home environment. The record establishes that Claimant was the sole technology coordinator for a campus situated on a steep mountain slope. He was required to repeatedly climb “fifty to sixty stairs” at the base of the library, another “eighty stairs” to the next level, and hike down to lower buildings multiple times a day. Claimant testified that this exertion caused him to stop and catch his breath and that the uphill climb took significantly longer and caused more distress than the descent.

While the defense correctly notes that vog was present island-wide, the defense fails to account for the interaction between the environmental hazard and the physical demands of the employment. The medical evidence, particularly the testimony of Dr. Tom and the report of Dr. Mathison, links Claimant’s exacerbated symptoms to the period of vog exposure while he was working on Maui. Dr. Mathison’s report explicitly notes that Claimant experienced “serious flare of asthmatic symptoms” during the period of vog exposure and that his condition improved coincident with his residence on O‘ahu.

The combination of the vog exposure and the mandatory physical exertion of climbing stairs in that environment distinguishes the workplace exposure from the general exposure of a resident. Under the unitary test, the employment need not be the sole cause; it need only be a substantial contributing factor. *Kekauoha v. Hawaii Dep’t of Educ.*, 108 Haw. 157, 118 P.3d 398 (2005). The evidence suggests that the physical requirements of the job, performed in a vog-polluted environment, substantially contributed to the worsening of Claimant’s respiratory condition beyond what he would have experienced merely by residing on Maui.

**C. The Defense’s Reliance on Proximate Cause is Unpersuasive**

The defense argues that even under the unitary test, the evidence fails to establish proximate cause because the vog was a neutral condition. However, this argument conflates the presence of a neutral condition with the effect of employment duties on that condition. The defense does not refute that the physical exertion required by Claimant’s job was a significant stressor on his respiratory system. The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s statement that vog is “not a factor that is associated with school uniquely” is misplaced in the context of an aggravation claim, as it ignores the specific physical demands placed on Claimant *at* the school.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Director erred as a matter of law by applying the “nature of employment” test from *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Haw. 203, 58 P.3d 1272 (2002), to Claimant’s claim for the aggravation of a pre-existing condition. The Director’s finding that the vog was island-wide does not negate the possibility that employment was a substantial contributing factor to the injury under the unitary test. The record contains sufficient evidence to suggest that the physical demands of Claimant’s employment, combined with environmental vog exposure, substantially aggravated his asthma.

Therefore, the Director’s decision denying benefits is **REVERSED**, and the case is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with the unitary test for aggravation of pre-existing conditions.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant-Appellant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. For the reasons stated below, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling that the Director’s decision must be reversed.

**II. APPLICABLE LEGAL STANDARDS**

As established in the preliminary ruling and acknowledged by the parties in their rebuttals, the correct legal standard for determining compensability of a pre-existing condition aggravated by employment is the "unitary test." Under *Kekauoha v. Hawaii Dep't of Educ.*, 108 Haw. 157, 118 P.3d 398 (2005), a claimant need only demonstrate that the employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. This standard does not require that the risk be unique to the workplace, nor does it require that the employment be the sole cause of the aggravation. *Id.* at 163, 118 P.3d at 404. The "nature of employment" test articulated in *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Haw. 203, 58 P.3d 1272 (2002), which requires a showing that the disease is inherent to or caused by conditions peculiar to the work environment, applies only to occupational diseases, not to aggravations of pre-existing conditions.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director’s Application of the Wrong Legal Standard Was Not Harmless Error**

The defense argues that even if the Director erred by citing *Flor*, the decision should be affirmed because the Director’s ultimate finding—that employment was not a substantial contributing factor—is supported by substantial evidence. The Court disagrees.

The Director’s decision explicitly states: "The Director found that ‘[Van Ness’s] exposure to vog aggravated his long standing asthma condition.’ However, ‘[t]he vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment.’" The Director concluded there was "insufficient evidence" to support the claim because the exposure at work was not "so overwhelming" that it occurred only at the place of employment.

This reasoning demonstrates that the Director’s legal error infected the factual findings. By focusing exclusively on whether the *vog* was unique to the workplace, the Director failed to consider whether the *conditions of employment*—specifically the physical exertion required to perform the job—were a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation. The defense’s assertion that the Director "properly weighed the evidence" is unconvincing because the Director applied the wrong weighing criteria. Under the unitary test, the inquiry is not whether the environmental hazard (vog) is unique, but whether the employment, as a whole, substantially contributed to the injury. *Kekauoha*, 108 Haw. at 163, 118 P.3d at 404.

**B. The Physical Demands of Employment Were a Substantial Contributing Factor**

The defense contends that the physical exertion required at Lahainaluna did not "cause" the asthma exacerbation, arguing instead that "the stairs merely made it more difficult for him to breathe once his asthma had already been triggered by vog exposure." This argument misconstrues the nature of the injury.

Claimant’s injury is the *aggravation* of his pre-existing asthma. The record establishes that Claimant was required to climb hundreds of stairs daily, often stopping to catch his breath, and that this exertion took significantly more effort and caused more distress than walking on flat ground. The prosecution correctly argues that the combination of this physical exertion with the vog exposure distinguishes the workplace environment from the home environment.

While the defense notes that vog was "even worse" in Kihei where Claimant lived, it ignores that Claimant’s home did not require him to climb steep stairs repeatedly throughout the day. The medical evidence confirms that Claimant’s symptoms flared during the period of vog exposure *while he was working* and that his condition improved when he left both the island and the job. Dr. Mathison’s report links the "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" to "exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" during the relevant time period.

Under the unitary test, if the physical demands of the job exacerbated the respiratory distress caused by vog, then the employment was a substantial contributing factor to the injury. The defense’s attempt to sever the physical exertion from the vog exposure fails because the injury—the aggravation—was the result of the interaction between the two. The Director’s failure to consider this interaction constitutes a clear error in applying the law to the facts.

**C. The Medical Evidence Does Not Preclude a Finding of Compensability**

The defense relies on Dr. Arora’s statement that vog is "not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" and Dr. Mathison’s general references to vog exposure on Maui to argue that the medical evidence does not support a finding of employment causation. However, neither physician opined that the physical demands of the job were *not* a contributing factor. Dr. Mathison’s observation that Claimant’s condition improved "coincident to his residence in Oahu" is consistent with the conclusion that removing the combination of vog exposure and strenuous physical exertion alleviated the aggravation.

The Director’s finding that the evidence was insufficient was based on the flawed premise that the vog exposure itself had to be unique to the school. Having rejected that premise, the Court finds that the medical evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Claimant, supports a finding that the employment conditions substantially contributed to the aggravation of his asthma.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The defense has not demonstrated that the Director’s decision was supported by substantial evidence under the correct legal standard. The Director’s focus on the island-wide prevalence of vog, to the exclusion of the physical demands of the employment, constitutes a misapplication of the unitary test. Accordingly, the Director’s decision denying benefits is **REVERSED**, and the case is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant-Appellant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. For the reasons stated below, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling reversing the Director’s decision.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a decision by the Director, this Court applies the clearly erroneous standard to the Director’s findings of fact and *de novo* to the Director’s conclusions of law. *Kekauoha v. Hawaii Dep't of Educ.*, 108 Haw. 157, 162, 118 P.3d 398, 403 (2005). If the Director’s decision is based on an error of law, it must be set aside even if the findings of fact are supported by substantial evidence. *Id.*

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Unitary Test Governs Aggravation Claims, Not the Nature of Employment Test**

The defense argues that the Director’s decision should be affirmed because substantial evidence supports the finding that vog exposure was not unique to the workplace. However, this argument relies on the incorrect legal standard. As established in *Kekauoha*, claims for the aggravation of a pre-existing condition are governed by the "unitary test," not the "nature of employment" test found in *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Haw. 203, 58 P.3d 1272 (2002).

Under the unitary test, a claimant need only show that employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. *Kekauoha*, 108 Haw. at 163, 118 P.3d at 404. The defense’s insistence that the exposure must be "unique" to the workplace misstates the law. *Kekauoha* explicitly rejected the requirement that a risk be "peculiar to or inherent in" the employment for aggravation claims. *Id.* at 163, 118 P.3d at 404. Therefore, the Director’s focus on the island-wide prevalence of vog was a legal error that tainted the factual findings.

**B. The Physical Demands of Employment Were a Substantial Contributing Factor**

The defense contends that even under the unitary test, the evidence does not support a finding that employment was a substantial contributing factor. Specifically, the defense argues that the physical exertion required to climb stairs at Lahainaluna did not cause the asthma exacerbation but merely made breathing more difficult once the asthma was triggered by vog. This argument misconstrues the causal inquiry for aggravation claims.

The injury in this case is the *aggravation* of Van Ness’s pre-existing asthma. The record establishes that Van Ness was required to climb hundreds of stairs daily, often stopping to catch his breath, and that this exertion caused significant distress, including "incredibly sharp" pain and tachycardia. The prosecution correctly identifies that the combination of this physical exertion with the vog exposure distinguished the workplace environment from the home environment.

While the defense notes that vog was "even worse" in Kihei where Van Ness lived, it ignores the critical fact that his home did not require him to perform strenuous physical labor in the midst of an asthma attack. The medical evidence from Dr. Mathison confirms that Van Ness experienced a "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" during the period he was working at Lahainaluna. The defense’s attempt to sever the physical exertion from the vog exposure fails because the aggravation was the result of the interaction between the two. The employment conditions—specifically the requirement to exert oneself physically while exposed to respiratory irritants—were a substantial contributing factor to the worsening of his condition.

**C. The Director’s Findings Are Not Supported by Substantial Evidence Under the Correct Standard**

The defense argues that the Director’s findings are supported by substantial evidence because vog was present island-wide. However, this finding is irrelevant to the unitary test. The Director failed to make any findings regarding the physical demands of the job or whether those demands contributed to the aggravation. By focusing exclusively on the environmental hazard (vog), the Director ignored the "conditions of employment" that *Kekauoha* requires be considered.

The defense’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s statement that vog is "not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" is misplaced. Neither Dr. Arora nor Dr. Mathison opined that the physical demands of the job were *not* a contributing factor. To the contrary, Dr. Mathison’s report links the severity of the symptoms to the exposure during the work period and recommends that Van Ness avoid assignments to areas with volcanic exposure.

Because the Director applied the wrong legal standard and failed to consider the physical exertion required by the job, the decision cannot stand. The defense has not demonstrated that the Director’s conclusion would have been the same had the correct standard been applied.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The defense has not shown that the Director’s decision was supported by substantial evidence under the correct legal standard. The Director’s focus on the island-wide prevalence of vog, to the exclusion of the physical demands of the employment, constitutes a misapplication of the unitary test. Accordingly, the Director’s decision denying benefits is **REVERSED**, and the case is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
