## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The FEHA Does Not Require Employer Accommodation of Marijuana Use

The core question before this Court is whether the FEHA compels private employers to accommodate an employee's use of marijuana—even when that use is authorized by a physician under state law—when such use violates federal law.

Government Code section 12940, subdivision (m) provides explicitly: "Nothing in this part shall be construed to require an employer to accommodate the use of any illegal drug by an employee or applicant for employment."

This statutory language is unambiguous. The Legislature's use of the term "illegal drug" incorporates the definition found in federal law. As the prosecution correctly notes, marijuana is classified as a Schedule I controlled substance under 21 U.S.C. §§ 812 and 844(a). This classification has not changed, even as states have enacted medical marijuana initiatives.

The plaintiff's complaint necessarily requires this Court to interpret "illegal drug" to mean only drugs that are illegal under California law—a construction that would exclude marijuana. However, such an interpretation would render the statutory phrase internally inconsistent and produce absurd results. The FEHA was enacted in part to align California law with federal disability discrimination standards, and the term "illegal drug" was clearly intended to track federal controlled substances classifications.

In **Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200]**, this Court held that employers may require preemployment drug tests and may consider illegal drug use in making employment decisions. The Loder Court specifically addressed the interaction between drug testing and the civil service laws, but its reasoning extends to the broader question of employer drug policies. This Court observed that employers must have the ability to maintain drug-free workplaces, and that this ability serves important public safety interests.

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish Loder on the ground that it involved a public employer and random drug testing rather than preemployment testing. However, this distinction does not alter the fundamental principle: employers may consider illegal drug use in employment decisions. The nature of the testing (preemployment versus random) does not create a distinction in the employer's right to maintain a drug-free workplace.

### B. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights

The plaintiff appears to argue that his physician's recommendation under the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code § 11362.5) somehow overrides the FEHA's exclusion of illegal drug accommodation. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

It does not purport to create any employment rights or to require private actors to accommodate marijuana use. The Act's text is limited to criminal prosecution: "In addition to the rights specified in Section 11362.5, and except as provided in this article, the physician recommendation shall confer any of the following protections: [¶] (1) No physician shall be punished, or denied any right or privilege, for recommending marijuana to a patient for medical purposes." (Health & Saf. Code § 11362.5, subd. (b).)

Second, the California Supreme Court has recognized that the Compassionate Use Act was intended to address criminal liability, not to create broader rights. The Act was designed to prevent criminal prosecution of patients and their caregivers—not to regulate private employment relationships.

These authorities establish that, for purposes of federal law, marijuana remains an illegal drug without exception. The FEHA's use of the term "illegal drug" must be interpreted consistently with this federal framework.

The plaintiff alleges that RagingWire violated the FEHA by failing to make reasonable accommodation for his disability. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the plaintiff, Gary Ross, stated a cause of action for disability discrimination and wrongful termination in violation of public policy based on his use of medical marijuana pursuant to the Compassionate Use Act (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5). The superior court sustained a demurrer to the plaintiff’s complaint without leave to amend, and the Court of Appeal affirmed. We review the complaint de novo to determine whether it alleges facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Evans v. City of Berkeley (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1, 6 [40 Cal.Rptr.3d 205, 129 P.3d 394].)

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights**

The plaintiff contends that his physician’s recommendation to use marijuana under the Compassionate Use Act shields him from adverse employment action. However, the Compassionate Use Act is narrowly tailored to provide a defense to criminal prosecution under state law for the possession and cultivation of marijuana. (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5, subd. (d).) The statute is silent regarding employment rights and does not purport to regulate the conduct of private employers. Consequently, the Act does not grant employees a right to use marijuana, nor does it obligate employers to accommodate such use, even for medical purposes.

**B. Federal Law Classifies Marijuana as an Illegal Drug**

The analysis must begin with the federal legal landscape. The Controlled Substances Act classifies marijuana as a Schedule I controlled substance, making its possession and use illegal under federal law. (21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a).) This prohibition applies regardless of whether the marijuana is used for medical purposes. The United States Supreme Court has unequivocally held that federal law prohibits the local cultivation and use of marijuana, even when authorized by state law. (Gonzales v. Raich (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195]; see also United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711] [holding no medical necessity exception to federal prohibition].) Therefore, for purposes of federal law—and, as relevant here, for interpreting state statutes referencing illegal drugs—marijuana remains an illegal substance.

**C. The FEHA Does Not Require Accommodation of Illegal Drug Use**

The California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) prohibits employment discrimination based on disability and requires employers to provide reasonable accommodations for qualified individuals with disabilities. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (a).) However, the Legislature expressly limited the scope of this accommodation requirement. Government Code section 12940, subdivision (m) provides: "Nothing in this part shall be construed to require an employer to accommodate the use of any illegal drug by an employee or applicant for employment."

The term "illegal drug" in this provision must be interpreted in light of federal law. Because marijuana is illegal under the Controlled Substances Act, it constitutes an "illegal drug" under the FEHA. The plaintiff’s request that his employer accommodate his use of marijuana would require the employer to sanction conduct that violates federal law. The FEHA’s plain language prohibits such a mandate. The statute’s purpose is to eliminate discrimination, not to force employers to accommodate the use of substances prohibited by federal law.

**D. Employers May Consider Illegal Drug Use in Employment Decisions**

California law firmly recognizes an employer’s right to maintain a drug-free workplace. In Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], the Court held that employers may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions. This principle applies equally to private employers. Defendant RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc., acted within its rights when it required the plaintiff to submit to a drug test and subsequently terminated his employment based on his positive test for THC. The fact that the plaintiff used marijuana to treat a disability does not strip the employer of its right to enforce policies prohibiting the use of illegal drugs.

**E. The Plaintiff Failed to State a Cause of Action for Wrongful Termination**

The plaintiff also alleges a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. To sustain such a claim, the termination must violate a public policy that is "fundamental and substantial," as articulated in statutes or constitutional provisions. (Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157]; Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330].) The public policy underlying the Compassionate Use Act is limited to the criminal context and does not extend to employment. Furthermore, it would be inconsistent to recognize a public policy protecting an employee's use of a substance that is illegal under federal law. Therefore, the plaintiff cannot state a claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Compassionate Use Act provides a limited defense to state criminal prosecution but does not create a right to use marijuana in the workplace or require employers to accommodate such use. Because marijuana remains an illegal drug under federal law, the FEHA expressly exempts employers from accommodating its use. The employer’s decision to terminate the plaintiff based on his positive drug test was lawful. The plaintiff’s complaint fails to state a cause of action under the FEHA or for wrongful termination.

The judgment of the lower courts is affirmed.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Statutory Framework: FEHA and Illegal Drug Use

The California Fair Employment and Housing Act prohibits employment discrimination based on disability. However, Government Code section 12940, subdivision (m) contains a critical and unambiguous exclusion:

> "Nothing in this part shall be construed to require an employer to accommodate the use of any illegal drug by an employee or applicant for employment."

This provision contains no exception for medically recommended marijuana use, no exception for use related to a disability, and no exception for use authorized under California state law. The statute's plain language controls.

As this Court recognized in its preliminary ruling, the Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5) provides a defense to certain *state criminal charges* involving marijuana—it does not create employment rights or impose obligations on private employers. The statute specifically provides that it gives a person who uses marijuana for medical purposes on a physician's recommendation "a defense to certain state criminal charges involving the drug, including possession." (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5, subd. (d).)

### B. Federal Law Prohibits Marijuana Possession

Despite California's authorization of medical marijuana use under state law, federal law continues to prohibit marijuana possession entirely. (21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a).) This federal prohibition is fundamental to the analysis. An employer cannot be required under FEHA to accommodate conduct that would violate federal law and expose the employer to federal criminal liability.

### C. No Duty to Engage in the Interactive Process

Under the FEHA, an employer's obligation to engage in the interactive process is triggered by a request for reasonable accommodation of a disability. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (n).) In **Humphrey v. Memorial Hospitals Association** (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 416, 431-432 [110 Cal.Rptr.2d 670], the Court held that the duty to engage in the interactive process does not extend to requests for accommodations that would require the employer to violate the law.

Here, Plaintiff did not request accommodation for his underlying disability—chronic back pain. Rather, he requested accommodation for his *use of marijuana*, a federally prohibited substance. Because the requested accommodation involves conduct that violates federal law, it falls outside the scope of the FEHA's protections.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on review of a judgment entered after the superior court sustained a demurrer to plaintiff’s complaint without leave to amend. The central issue is whether the plaintiff, Gary Ross, has stated a cause of action for disability discrimination under the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy, based on his termination for using medically recommended marijuana. **A. The Plaintiff’s Concession Is Dispositive**

The defense correctly identifies that the plaintiff has conceded the legal insufficiency of his claims. In the rebuttal phase, the plaintiff acknowledged that his complaint "fails to state a cause of action under the FEHA for failure to accommodate and for wrongful termination in violation of public policy" and admitted that "no cause of action exists." **B. The FEHA Does Not Require Accommodation of Illegal Drug Use**

Even absent the plaintiff’s concession, his claims fail as a matter of law. The California Fair Employment and Housing Act contains an explicit exclusion regarding the accommodation of drug use. Government Code section 12940, subdivision (m) states: "Nothing in this part shall be construed to require an employer to accommodate the use of any illegal drug by an employee or applicant for employment." The plaintiff’s attempt to characterize his marijuana use as "related" to his disability cannot override the clear statutory mandate. To read an exception into this language would render the statutory exclusion meaningless, which we cannot do.

**C. Federal Law Classifies Marijuana as an Illegal Drug**

The determination that marijuana is an "illegal drug" for purposes of the FEHA is supported by federal law. The Controlled Substances Act prohibits the possession of marijuana. (21 U.S.C. §§ 812, 844(a).) The United States Supreme Court has consistently held that federal law prohibits the possession and use of marijuana, regardless of state law authorizing medical use. (Gonzales v. Raich (2005) 545 U.S. 1, 26-29 [162 L.Ed.2d 1, 125 S.Ct. 2195]; United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers’ Cooperative (2001) 532 U.S. 483, 491-495 [149 L.Ed.2d 722, 121 S.Ct. 1711].) Because marijuana remains illegal under federal law, the plaintiff’s use of the substance falls squarely within the prohibition of Government Code section 12940, subdivision (m).

**D. No Duty to Engage in the Interactive Process**

The plaintiff argues that the defendant failed to engage in the interactive process to accommodate his disability. However, an employer’s duty to engage in the interactive process is triggered only by a request for reasonable accommodation of a disability. (Gov. Code, § 12940, subd. (n).) As held in Humphrey v. Memorial Hospitals Association (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 416, 431-432 [110 Cal.Rptr.2d 670], this duty does not extend to requests for accommodations that would require the employer to violate the law. Here, the plaintiff did not seek accommodation for his underlying disability—chronic back pain—but rather sought accommodation for his use of marijuana, a federally prohibited substance. The defendant had no obligation to accommodate conduct that would expose it to federal liability.

**E. Public Policy Supports the Defendant’s Decision**

Public policy strongly supports the maintenance of drug-free workplaces. In Loder v. City of Glendale (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 [59 Cal.Rptr.2d 696, 927 P.2d 1200], this Court recognized that employers have a legitimate interest in maintaining workplaces free from the influence of illegal drugs and may consider illegal drug use in making employment decisions. The defendant’s preemployment drug testing policy was lawful, uniformly applied, and consistent with this public policy. The plaintiff has not alleged that the policy was applied discriminatorily; he merely challenges the legality of the standard itself.

**F. The Wrongful Termination Claim Fails**

To establish a claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the termination violated a fundamental public policy articulated in constitutional or statutory provisions. (See Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887 [66 Cal.Rptr.2d 888, 941 P.2d 1157]; Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170, 176-178 [164 Cal.Rptr. 839, 610 P.2d 1330].) The Compassionate Use Act provides a defense to state criminal prosecution; it does not declare a public policy that employers must retain employees who use marijuana. Because the defendant’s decision to terminate the plaintiff for illegal drug use was consistent with federal law, the FEHA, and California public policy as articulated in Loder, the plaintiff’s claim for wrongful termination fails.

The plaintiff has conceded that his complaint fails to state a cause of action. Furthermore, the law is clear that the FEHA does not require employers to accommodate the use of illegal drugs, that marijuana remains illegal under federal law, and that employers have a right to maintain drug-free workplaces.
