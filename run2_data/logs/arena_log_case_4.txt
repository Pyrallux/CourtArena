## I. THE IMPROPER TESTIMONY BY OFFICER SHUGRUE WAS NOT HARMLESS AND REQUIRES REVERSAL

The State acknowledges that Officer Shugrue improperly commented on the Defendant's refusal to answer questions during cross-examination. This Court has characterized the officer's conduct as something that "cannot in good conscience condone such conduct from officers who know or should know better." The prosecution requests this Court to apply the harmless error doctrine as established in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), and *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), to affirm the conviction despite this improper conduct.

The Defendant respectfully submits that the harmless error analysis applied by the State and the lower court fails to properly account for the constitutional magnitude of the error committed.

### A. The Standard for Harmless Error

In *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), the United States Supreme Court held that before a federal constitutional error can be held harmless, the court must be able to declare a belief that it was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The burden rests squarely upon the State to prove that the error did not contribute to the verdict obtained. *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), further elaborated that the harmless error inquiry is not whether the evidence is sufficient to support the verdict absent the improper element, but rather whether the improper testimony had any influence on the verdict.

### B. The Officer Shurgrue's Comment Violated the Defendant's Fifth Amendment Rights

The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution guarantees that no person shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself. This protection extends to a defendant's right to remain silent and refuse to answer questions. When Officer Shugrue voluntarily commented on the Defendant's refusal to answer questions, he directly implicated this constitutional protection.

The comment by a law enforcement officer regarding a defendant's silence carries tremendous persuasive weight with a jury. Police officers occupy a position of authority and trust in the eyes of the jury. When such an officer testifies that the defendant refused to answer questions, the jury may interpret this silence as consciousness of guilt, effectively punishing the defendant for exercising a fundamental constitutional right.

This Court has recognized that the comment was improper and expressed disapproval of the officer's conduct. However, the Court below concluded that reversal was not required because the Defendant failed the roadside test and the breathalyzer reading was .12. This reasoning improperly applies the harmless error standard.

### C. The Evidence Was Not So Overwhelming As To Render The Error Harmless

The State argues that the evidence of guilt was overwhelming: the breathalyzer reading of .12 and the Defendant's refusal to submit to field sobriety testing. However, this argument conflates the sufficiency of evidence with the harmless error analysis.

In *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), the Florida Supreme Court emphasized that the question is not whether the defendant could have been convicted without the improper testimony, but whether the improper testimony might have influenced the jury. The DiGuilio court stated that the inquiry is whether the error contributed to the verdict, not whether the verdict could have been sustained without that evidence.

The Defendant submits that Officer Shugrue's comment could have influenced the jury in several ways. First, the jury may have viewed the Defendant's silence as indicative of guilt, reasoning that an innocent person would have answered questions. Second, the jury may have drawn negative inferences from the Defendant's exercise of his constitutional right. Third, the officer's improper comment may have undermined the Defendant's credibility generally.

The State has failed to carry its burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that this constitutional violation did not contribute to the verdict. The mere existence of other evidence of intoxication does not satisfy this burden. The Defendant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial.

## II. THE STATE COMMITTED A DISCOVERY VIOLATION BY FAILING TO DISCLOSE THE BREATHALYZER INSPECTION REPORTS

The Defendant maintains that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer's monthly inspection reports prior to trial. The State's position that no violation occurred because the administrative rules do not require disclosure absent a challenge by the Defendant fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the State's discovery obligations and the Defendant's right to prepare a meaningful defense.

### A. The State's Discovery Obligations

The Defendant submits that the State's duty to disclose exculpatory evidence extends beyond the narrow technical requirements of administrative regulations. The Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution guarantees due process of law, which includes the right to a fair trial. The United States Supreme Court in *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), held that the suppression by the prosecution of evidence favorable to an accused upon request violates due process where the evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment.

Furthermore, in *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667 (1985), the Supreme Court clarified that evidence is material if there is a reasonable probability that, had the evidence been disclosed to the defense, the result of the proceeding would have been different.

### B. The Inspection Reports Were Material To The Defense

The breathalyzer reading of .12 constitutes the primary evidence upon which the State relies to establish the Defendant's intoxication. The reliability of this reading depends directly upon the proper functioning and calibration of the breathalyzer machine. The monthly inspection reports would reveal whether the machine was properly maintained and calibrated at the time of the Defendant's test.

These records are precisely the type of evidence that the Defendant needed to evaluate the State's case and to prepare a meaningful defense. Without access to these reports, the Defendant could not determine whether to challenge the reliability of the breathalyzer reading or how to effectively cross-examine the State's witnesses regarding the machine's condition.

### C. The State's Interpretation Of The Administrative Rules Is Erroneous

The State argues that the administrative rules at § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules do not require disclosure of inspection reports unless the Defendant first challenges the equipment's reliability. This interpretation creates an absurd result that defeats the purpose of discovery.

Under the State's reasoning, the Defendant would be required to challenge the reliability of the breathalyzer without having access to the very information necessary to make that challenge. This circular reasoning places an impossible burden on the Defendant and effectively nullifies any right to challenge the equipment's condition.

The administrative rules should be interpreted in a manner consistent with the Defendant's constitutional right to prepare a defense. The State bears the responsibility of maintaining proper records and making them available for the Defendant's review. The purpose of these record-keeping requirements is to ensure the reliability of breathalyzer tests, and that purpose is defeated if the Defendant cannot access these records.

The Defendant submits that the proper interpretation of the rules is that the State must maintain and disclose the inspection reports, and upon a proper challenge by the Defendant, must establish that the equipment was properly maintained. However, the Defendant should not be required to make a blind challenge without access to the underlying documentation.

### D. The Discovery Violation Requires Reversal

The Defendant submits that the failure to disclose the inspection reports constitutes reversible error. The breathalyzer reading was central to the State's case, and the inspection reports could have been used to challenge the reliability of that reading. The Defendant was denied the opportunity to present a complete defense.

Given the constitutional dimensions of this issue, the Defendant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial with appropriate disclosure of the inspection reports.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding a conviction for driving under the influence. The Appellant raises three issues on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in admitting certain testimony by Officer Shugrue regarding the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error on the third issue. Based upon the arguments presented and the facts provided, the Court affirms the conviction as to the first and second issues and remands solely on the issue of court costs.

**II. ANALYSIS OF THE IMPROPER TESTIMONY**

The Appellant argues that the trial court committed reversible error by allowing Officer Shugrue to comment on the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions during cross-examination. The Defense contends this comment violated the Defendant's Fifth Amendment rights and was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The State acknowledges the impropriety of the comment but argues the error was harmless under the standards established in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), and *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986).

The Court agrees with the Appellant that Officer Shugrue’s comment was improper. As noted in the case background, officers "should not have voluntarily commented on the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions," and such conduct "cannot in good conscience [be] condone[d]." However, not every error requires reversal.

Under *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), the State bears the burden of demonstrating that the constitutional error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The Florida Supreme Court elaborated on this standard in *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), stating that the inquiry is whether the error contributed to the verdict, rather than whether the evidence was sufficient to support the verdict without the error.

Here, the evidence of the Defendant's guilt was substantial and uncontradicted. The record reflects that the Defendant failed the roadside sobriety tests and provided a breathalyzer reading of .12. Pursuant to § 316.1934(2)(c), Florida Statutes (1985), a blood alcohol reading exceeding .10 establishes prima facie evidence that the defendant was under the influence of alcohol. The Defense argues that the officer's comment likely influenced the jury by portraying the Defendant's silence as indicative of guilt. However, given the strength of the physical evidence—specifically the statutory prima facie evidence of intoxication—the Court finds that the State has met its burden under *Chapman* and *DiGuilio*. It is unlikely that the single improper comment by Officer Shugrue influenced the jury to return a guilty verdict in light of the .12 breathalyzer result and the failure of the roadside tests. Therefore, the error is deemed harmless, and the conviction stands on this issue.

**III. ANALYSIS OF THE DISCOVERY VIOLATION**

The Appellant next argues that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports prior to trial. The Defense relies on *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), and *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667 (1985), asserting that the State violated due process by withholding material evidence necessary to challenge the reliability of the breathalyzer reading.

The State counters that no discovery violation occurred because the administrative rules, specifically § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules, do not mandate pre-trial disclosure of inspection reports absent a specific challenge by the defense regarding the equipment's reliability.

The Court finds the State’s position persuasive based on the provided facts. The administrative rules cited in the record establish the procedures for administering breathalyzer tests. As stated in the case background, these rules "do not require the State to prove that the machinery was fit for proper testing in order to prove a prima facia case." Rather, the documentation required by the rules serves as the predicate for the admissibility of the reading "should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment."

The Defense argues that requiring a challenge before disclosure creates an impossible burden, violating the principles of *Brady*. However, the facts indicate that the duty to produce the documentation arises "upon such challenge." The Defense has not demonstrated that the State suppressed evidence that was material under *Brady* or *Bagley* in the context of these specific administrative procedures. The State is not required to produce maintenance records to establish a prima facie case; that burden shifts to the State only if the Defendant challenges the equipment's condition. Because the Defense's obligation to challenge the equipment precedes the State's duty to produce the specific inspection reports under the governing administrative rules, there was no discovery violation. The State complied with the statutory and administrative framework governing the admissibility of the breathalyzer results.

**IV. ANALYSIS OF COURT COSTS**

Regarding the third issue, the State has conceded that the trial court erred in imposing court costs upon the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. The Appellant agrees that remand is appropriate. Accordingly, the Court accepts the State's concession. The cause is remanded to the Trial Court to vacate the order imposing court costs and for further proceedings should the State seek to tax such costs against the Defendant.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the conviction on the underlying DUI charges. The judgment is affirmed in all respects except for the imposition of court costs. The case is remanded to the Trial Court for proceedings consistent with this opinion regarding the vacatur of the court costs order.

**SO ORDERED.**

Under *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), the prosecution bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the constitutional error did not contribute to the verdict. This is not a burden that can be lightly discharged by simply pointing to other evidence of guilt. The prosecution must demonstrate that the improper comment was "harmless beyond a reasonable doubt," a standard that reflects the gravity of allowing constitutional violations to stand.

The prosecution's argument essentially asks this Court to find that because strong evidence of guilt existed, the constitutional error must have been harmless. This reasoning is precisely what *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), cautioned against. The DiGuilio Court explicitly stated that the inquiry is whether the error contributed to the verdict, not whether the evidence was sufficient to support the verdict absent the error. The prosecution conflates these two distinct inquiries, and the Court should reject this misapplication of the harmless error standard.

### B. The Character of the Improper Comment Warrants Greater Scrutiny

Officer Shugrue's comment was not merely an incidental remark; it directly implicated the Defendant's Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination. When Officer Shugrue voluntarily commented on the Defendant's refusal to answer questions, he effectively told the jury that the Defendant's silence was indicative of guilt. This is precisely the inference that the Fifth Amendment was designed to prevent.

The prosecution argues that the breathalyzer reading of .12 and the failed roadside tests created overwhelming evidence of guilt. However, the Defendant had the constitutional right to remain silent, and the jury should never have been permitted to draw negative inferences from the exercise of that right. The prosecution cannot cure a constitutional violation by pointing to other evidence of guilt; otherwise, the Fifth Amendment would become meaningless in criminal trials.

### C. The Prosecution Cannot Prove the Comment Did Not Contribute to the Verdict

The prosecution argues that no duty to disclose arose because the administrative rules require the defendant to first challenge the equipment's condition before the State must produce documentation. The Defense submits that this argument creates an impossible Catch-22 that violates due process.

The inspection reports are precisely the evidence a defendant needs to determine whether a challenge is warranted. By requiring the defendant to make a challenge before obtaining access to the very evidence needed to support that challenge, the State's interpretation of the administrative rules denies defendants any meaningful opportunity to contest the reliability of breathalyzer readings.

This Court should reject the prosecution's attempt to impose an impossible burden on the Defendant and instead hold that the State's failure to disclose the breathalyzer's monthly inspection reports constituted a violation of Brady and its progeny.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding a conviction for driving under the influence. The Appellant raises three issues on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in admitting certain testimony by Officer Shugrue regarding the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error on the third issue. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense. Based upon the arguments presented and the facts provided, the Court affirms the conviction as to the first and second issues and remands solely on the issue of court costs.

**II. ANALYSIS OF THE IMPROPER TESTIMONY**

The Appellant argues that the trial court committed reversible error by allowing Officer Shugrue to comment on the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions. The Defense contends this comment violated the Defendant's Fifth Amendment rights and was not harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The Defense relies on *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), asserting that the State must show there is no reasonable possibility the error influenced the jury, and argues that the State cannot meet this burden merely by pointing to other evidence of guilt.

The Prosecution counters that the error was harmless under the standards established in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), and *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986). The State argues that the Defendant’s breathalyzer reading of .12 and failure of roadside sobriety tests created overwhelming physical evidence of guilt, making it unlikely that the single improper comment affected the verdict.

The Court agrees with the Appellant that Officer Shugrue’s comment was improper. As noted in the case background, officers "should not have voluntarily commented on the Defendant’s refusal to answer questions," and such conduct "cannot in good conscience [be] condone[d]." However, not every error requires reversal.

Under *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), the State bears the burden of demonstrating that the constitutional error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The Florida Supreme Court elaborated on this standard in *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So. 2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), stating that the inquiry is whether the error contributed to the verdict, rather than whether the evidence was sufficient to support the verdict without the error.

Here, the evidence of the Defendant's guilt was substantial and uncontradicted. The record reflects that the Defendant failed the roadside sobriety tests and provided a breathalyzer reading of .12. Pursuant to § 316.1934(2)(c), Florida Statutes (1985), a blood alcohol reading exceeding .10 establishes prima facie evidence that the defendant was under the influence of alcohol. The Defense argues that the officer's comment likely influenced the jury by portraying the Defendant's silence as indicative of guilt. However, given the strength of the physical evidence—specifically the statutory prima facie evidence of intoxication—the Court finds that the State has met its burden under *Chapman* and *DiGuilio*. It is unlikely that the single improper comment by Officer Shugrue influenced the jury to return a guilty verdict in light of the .12 breathalyzer result and the failure of the roadside tests. Therefore, the error is deemed harmless, and the conviction stands on this issue.

**III. ANALYSIS OF THE DISCOVERY VIOLATION**

The Appellant next argues that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports prior to trial. In its rebuttal, the Defense relies on *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), and *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667 (1985), asserting that the State violated due process by withholding material evidence necessary to challenge the reliability of the breathalyzer reading. The Defense contends that the administrative rule requiring a challenge by the defendant before the State must produce documentation places the defendant in an impossible position, effectively denying them the opportunity to challenge the equipment's accuracy.

The State counters that no discovery violation occurred because the administrative rules, specifically § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) of the H.R.S. Administrative Rules, do not mandate pre-trial disclosure of inspection reports absent a specific challenge by the defense regarding the equipment's reliability.

The Court finds the State’s position persuasive based on the provided facts. The administrative rules cited in the record establish the procedures for administering breathalyzer tests. As stated in the case background, these rules "do not require the State to prove that the machinery was fit for proper testing in order to prove a prima facia case." Rather, the documentation required by the rules serves as the predicate for the admissibility of the reading "should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment."

The Defense argues that requiring a challenge before disclosure creates an impossible burden, violating the principles of *Brady*. However, the facts indicate that the duty to produce the documentation arises "upon such challenge." The Defense has not demonstrated that the State suppressed evidence that was material under *Brady* or *Bagley* in the context of these specific administrative procedures. The State is not required to produce maintenance records to establish a prima facie case; that burden shifts to the State only if the Defendant challenges the equipment's condition. Because the Defense's obligation to challenge the equipment precedes the State's duty to produce the specific inspection reports under the governing administrative rules, there was no discovery violation. The State complied with the statutory and administrative framework governing the admissibility of the breathalyzer results.

**IV. ANALYSIS OF COURT COSTS**

Regarding the third issue, the State has conceded that the trial court erred in imposing court costs upon the indigent Defendant without the requisite notice and hearing. The Appellant agrees that remand is appropriate. Accordingly, the Court accepts the State's concession. The cause is remanded to the Trial Court to vacate the order imposing court costs and for further proceedings should the State seek to tax such costs against the Defendant.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the conviction on the underlying DUI charges. The judgment is affirmed in all respects except for the imposition of court costs. The case is remanded to the Trial Court for proceedings consistent with this opinion regarding the vacatur of the court costs order.

**SO ORDERED.**
