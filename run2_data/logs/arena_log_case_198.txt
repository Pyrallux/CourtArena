**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honors, this case presents a clear legal error by the Director in misapplying *Flor v. Holguin* to a claim that falls squarely outside its scope. The Director’s denial of Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of Hawaii law: *Flor* does not govern whether an aggravation of a pre-existing condition was proximately caused by employment. Instead, *Flor* applies only to injuries "resulting from the nature of the employment"—meaning conditions inherently caused by the job duties themselves. Here, the aggravation of Van Ness’s asthma was not "resulting from the nature of his employment" as a technology coordinator; it was proximately caused by environmental exposure at work. Thus, the unitary test for proximate cause, not the *Flor* test, applies. The Director’s error requires reversal.  

### I. *Flor v. Holguin* Is Limited to Injuries "Resulting from the Nature of the Employment"  
In *Flor v. Holguin*, this court explicitly confined its test to injuries "resulting from the nature of the employment." *Flor v. Holguin*, 111 Haw. 356, 366, 141 P.3d 1139, 1149 (2006). There, the claim involved a disease that arose directly from the inherent risks of the job—specifically, a firefighter’s exposure to toxic fumes during firefighting duties. *Id.* at 366, 141 P.3d at 1149. The court held that for such injuries, the "resulting from the nature of the employment" test applies: the disease must be "peculiar to the employment and not common to the general public." *Id.*  

But this case involves no such scenario. Van Ness’s job as a technology coordinator did not inherently involve volcanic vog exposure. His duties centered on maintaining computer systems—tasks unrelated to environmental hazards. Vog is not a "peculiar" risk of being a tech coordinator; it is an external environmental factor present island-wide. As Dr. Arora correctly noted, "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." The Director’s attempt to apply *Flor* to this situation misreads the law. *Flor* does not govern proximate cause for injuries caused by external environmental factors; it applies only when the job duties themselves create the injury.  

### II. Proximate Cause for Aggravation of Pre-Existing Conditions Requires the Unitary Test  
When an injury is proximately caused by employment—rather than "resulting from the nature of the employment"—Hawaii law applies the unitary test. As this court held in *Sato v. City & County of Honolulu*, the unitary test asks whether "the employment was a substantial contributing factor to the injury, even if not the sole cause." *Sato v. City & County of Honolulu*, 59 Haw. 552, 558, 584 P.2d 119, 123 (1978). This standard is well-established for aggravated pre-existing conditions. In *Matsuura v. State*, this court reaffirmed that "when a pre-existing condition is aggravated by work, the unitary test applies if the employment was a substantial contributing factor." *Matsuura v. State*, 122 Haw. 459, 470, 228 P.3d 423, 434 (2010).  

Here, the Director erred by failing to apply this test. Van Ness had a pre-existing mild asthma condition, but the evidence shows his employment at Lahainaluna substantially contributed to its aggravation. Specifically:  
- Van Ness was required to work in non-air-conditioned buildings with louvered windows and open ventilation, leading to dust accumulation that exacerbated his respiratory symptoms.  
- During severe vog episodes, he could not see halfway down the campus from his office due to air quality, and he experienced worsened coughing, wheezing, and shortness of breath while moving between buildings.  
- His inhalers were exhausted months before his prescription expired—unlike his experience on Oahu—because medication failed to control symptoms at work.  
- Dr. Mathison confirmed that volcanic pollution is a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients" and that Van Ness’s symptoms "improved coincident to his residence in Oahu," underscoring the causal link between work exposure and aggravation.  

Crucially, the Director’s focus on vog being "present on the entire island" ignores the unitary test’s core principle: proximate cause does not require the hazard to be unique to the workplace. *Sato*, 59 Haw. at 558, 584 P.2d at 123. If employment increases exposure or severity—such as through workplace conditions that heighten risk compared to home—then it is a substantial contributing factor. Van Ness’s home had HEPA filters and was air-conditioned, while his work environment lacked these protections. Dr. Mathison’s testimony and the DOE’s own approval of a hardship transfer for "medical reasons" due to vog exposure confirm that employment intensified his risk.  

### III. The Director’s Legal Error Requires Reversal  
By applying *Flor* to a claim about proximate cause, the Director committed reversible error. *Flor* has no application here because vog exposure is not "resulting from the nature of the employment." As a matter of law, the unitary test must govern. Under that test, the evidence overwhelmingly shows Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma. The Director’s conclusion—that "insufficient evidence" linked the injury to employment—ignores this standard and the record.  

Your Honors, this case is not about whether vog is "unique" to Lahainaluna. It is about whether work conditions made Van Ness’s exposure worse. Under *Sato* and *Matsuura*, that is sufficient. We respectfully request this court reverse the Director’s denial and remand for application of the correct legal standard.  

Thank you, Your Honors.

### I. THE DIRECTOR CORRECTLY APPLIED *FLOR V. HOLGUIN* TO DETERMINE WHETHER VAN NESS'S INJURY AROSE OUT OF EMPLOYMENT

The plaintiff erroneously contends that *Flor v. Holguin* applies only to injuries "resulting from the nature of the employment" and that a different "unitary test" should govern proximate cause analysis. This argument misreads Hawaii workers' compensation law and ignores the fundamental issue before the Director: whether Van Ness's alleged injury arose out of and in the course of his employment.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, the Hawaii Supreme Court articulated the standard for determining whether an injury is compensable under the Hawaii Workers' Compensation Act. The court held that an injury "arises out of" employment when there is a causal connection between the employment and the injury. *Flor v. Holguin*, 111 Haw. 356, 366, 141 P.3d 1139, 1149 (2006). The court recognized that the "arising out of" requirement "is met when a reasonable connection is shown to exist between the employment and the injury." *Id.*

The plaintiff attempts to create an artificial distinction between injuries "resulting from the nature of employment" and injuries "proximately caused by employment," arguing that different legal tests apply to each category. This distinction finds no support in Hawaii law. The *Flor* court explicitly addressed the "arising out of" requirement—the very issue presented here—and its framework governs whether any injury, including aggravations of pre-existing conditions, is compensable.

The plaintiff's reliance on *Sato v. City & County of Honolulu* and *Matsuura v. State* is misplaced. In *Sato*, the Hawaii Supreme Court addressed whether employment was a "substantial factor" in causing an injury—a causation analysis that falls within the broader "arising out of" inquiry. *Sato v. City & County of Honolulu*, 59 Haw. 552, 558, 584 P.2d 119, 123 (1978). The court did not create a separate "unitary test" that supersedes *Flor*; rather, it applied the "substantial factor" analysis within the *Flor* framework. Similarly, *Matsuura v. State* applied the substantial factor analysis to determine whether employment contributed to an aggravation of a pre-existing condition. *Matsuura v. State*, 122 Haw. 459, 470, 228 P.3d 423, 434 (2010). Neither case supports the plaintiff's argument that a different legal standard applies here.

### II. THE DIRECTOR CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT VOG WAS NOT A SPECIAL HAZARD UNIQUE TO THE WORKPLACE

The central factual finding supporting the Director's decision is that vog was present throughout the entire island of Maui and was not unique to Lahainaluna High School. This finding is supported by substantial evidence in the record and is fatal to the plaintiff's claim.

The Director found that "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment" and that "it was claimant's exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition." This finding is supported by Van Ness's own testimony that he lived in Kihei, where "the vog was even worse" according to Vice Principal Dennis, and that he spent minimal time outdoors other than at work. Van Ness himself testified that there was a significant amount of vog on Maui from late October 2005 through April 2006—encompassing the entire island, not merely the school campus.

Dr. Arora, the independent medical examiner, explicitly noted that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely," confirming that the environmental condition affecting Van Ness was island-wide rather than workplace-specific. This uncontroverted evidence supports the Director's conclusion that Van Ness's exposure to vog was not a special hazard of his employment.

Under Hawaii workers' compensation law, an injury arises out of employment only when the employment creates a risk or hazard to which the general public is not equally exposed. *See* Hawaii Rev. Stat. § 386-3 (defining compensable injury as one "arising out of and in the course of employment"). When a hazard is present throughout the community and not peculiar to the workplace, the injury does not "arise out of" employment. The Director correctly applied this principle.

---

### III. THE PLAINTIFF FAILS TO ESTABLISH THAT EMPLOYMENT WAS A SUBSTANTIAL CONTRIBUTING FACTOR

Even under the "substantial factor" analysis employed in *Sato* and *Matsuura*, the plaintiff cannot demonstrate that his employment was a substantial contributing factor to his respiratory condition.

The plaintiff argues that his workplace conditions—non-air-conditioned buildings, open windows, dust accumulation, and the need to climb stairs—substantially contributed to his condition. However, the evidence shows that these factors did not create a risk beyond what Van Ness faced in his daily life on Maui.

Van Ness testified that his home in Kihei was air-conditioned with HEPA filters, and he had additional Bionic Breeze filter units throughout his home. He also had a HEPA filter in his car. While his workplace lacked air conditioning, the plaintiff has not demonstrated that the workplace conditions meaningfully increased his exposure to vog or dust beyond what he would have experienced in the general environment. Indeed, Vice Principal Dennis testified that the vog was "even worse" in Kihei where Van Ness lived, suggesting his home environment presented equal or greater exposure.

The plaintiff's argument that his inhalers were exhausted faster while working at Lahainaluna is insufficient to establish causation. Van Ness testified that while on Oahu, he "usually did not need to use" his inhaler and only refilled when the prescription expired. The increased use during late 2005 and early 2006 could be attributable to the severe vog conditions affecting the entire island during that period, not specifically his employment. The timing of the severe vog—late October 2005 through April 2006—corresponds with the period when Van Ness experienced worsened symptoms, but this correlation does not establish that his workplace was the cause.

Dr. Mathison's report, while noting that Van Ness's symptoms improved after moving back to Oahu, does not establish that employment was a substantial contributing factor. Dr. Mathison stated that Van Ness "has been relatively free of respiratory symptoms" after moving to Oahu, but this improvement is equally consistent with reduced island-wide vog exposure rather than reduced workplace exposure. Dr. Mathison did not opine that Van Ness's employment at Lahainaluna specifically caused his condition; he merely noted that volcanic pollution is a risk factor for asthmatic patients generally.

The approval of Van Ness's hardship transfer does not support the plaintiff's claims. The DOE approved the transfer "due to medical reasons" based on Dr. Tom's recommendation, but this administrative action does not establish that the plaintiff's employment caused his injury. The transfer was approved in March 2006, but Van Ness was not actually transferred until November 2006—after his hospitalization and surgery. The timing and circumstances of the transfer suggest it was a reasonable accommodation for an employee with a medical condition rather than an admission that employment caused the condition.

---

### IV. THE DIRECTOR PROPERLY WEIGHED THE EVIDENCE

The Director credited the report of Dr. Arora and the employer's position, finding insufficient evidence to establish a nexus between Van Ness's employment and his injury. This credibility determination is within the Director's discretion and should not be disturbed on appeal.

Dr. Arora noted the complexity of the issues and correctly observed that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." While Dr. Arora did not have access to all medical records at the time of his examination, his observation about the general nature of vog exposure is consistent with the other evidence in the record.

The plaintiff argues that Dr. Mathison's report supports his claim, but the Director was not required to credit this evidence. Dr. Mathison's conclusions—that Van Ness can be "considered to be disabled" and that his employer should not "place him at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution"—do not establish that Van Ness's employment at Lahainaluna actually caused his injury. The opinion is speculative and addresses general risk factors rather than the specific causation question presented here.

---

### CONCLUSION

The Director correctly applied Hawaii workers' compensation law to this case. The evidence supports the finding that vog was present throughout the island of Maui and was not a special hazard of Van Ness's employment at Lahainaluna High School. Because Van Ness failed to establish that his respiratory condition arose out of and in the course of his employment, the Director properly denied his workers' compensation claim.

The plaintiff has not demonstrated that the Director committed legal error or that the decision was unsupported by substantial evidence. Accordingly, this Court should affirm the Director's decision denying Van Ness's claim.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the court on appeal from the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations’ decision denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The issue on appeal is whether the Director erred as a matter of law in determining that Van Ness’s injury did not arise out of and in the course of his employment. Specifically, the court must determine whether the Director correctly applied the legal standard for compensability regarding the aggravation of a pre-existing condition caused by environmental exposure.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Director's conclusions of law are reviewed *de novo*. See *Fujimoto v. State*, 70 Haw. 566, 570, 779 P.2d 828, 831 (1989). Where the Director has applied an incorrect legal standard, the decision must be vacated and the case remanded for application of the correct standard.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director Erred by Applying the "Nature of Employment" Test Instead of the Unitary Test**

The Director denied the claim based on the finding that vog was present island-wide and not unique to the workplace. This reasoning implies that for an injury to be compensable, the hazard must be "peculiar" to the employment. The Defense relies on *Flor v. Holguin*, 111 Haw. 356, 141 P.3d 1139 (2006), to support this position. However, the Prosecution correctly argues that *Flor* is limited to injuries "resulting from the nature of the employment." *Id.* at 366, 141 P.3d at 1149. The *Flor* test applies to occupational diseases that are inherent to the specific trade or profession, such as a firefighter’s exposure to smoke. *Id.*

Van Ness’s employment as a technology coordinator did not inherently involve exposure to volcanic vog. Vog is an environmental hazard affecting the general public, not a risk peculiar to the duties of repairing computers. Therefore, the "nature of employment" test articulated in *Flor* is inapposite to the facts of this case. The Director’s reliance on the absence of a "unique" workplace hazard constitutes legal error.

**B. The Unitary Test Governs Aggravations of Pre-Existing Conditions**

Where, as here, a claimant alleges that a pre-existing condition was aggravated by environmental exposure encountered during employment, the appropriate inquiry is whether the employment was a substantial contributing factor to the injury. This is the "unitary test" established in *Sato v. City & County of Honolulu*, 59 Haw. 552, 584 P.2d 119 (1978). Under *Sato*, an injury arises out of employment if the employment is a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury, even if it is not the sole cause. *Id.* at 558, 584 P.2d at 123. This standard was reaffirmed in *Matsuura v. State*, 122 Haw. 459, 228 P.3d 423 (2010), which held that the unitary test applies to aggravations of pre-existing conditions. *Matsuura*, 122 Haw. at 470, 228 P.3d at 434.

**C. Substantial Evidence Supports a Finding that Employment Was a Substantial Contributing Factor**

Applying the unitary test to the record, the evidence supports a finding that Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma. The record establishes a significant disparity between the environmental conditions at Van Ness’s home and his workplace.

Van Ness’s home in Kihei was climate-controlled with air conditioning and equipped with high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters and "Bionic Breeze" units. In contrast, Van Ness’s work environment at Lahainaluna High School consisted of non-air-conditioned buildings with louvered windows and fans, which allowed dust and volcanic ash to accumulate. Van Ness testified that he spent less than five percent of his time in his air-conditioned office and the remainder of his time in these non-air-conditioned environments, frequently climbing stairs and hiking up steep terrain, which exerted physical stress on his respiratory system.

Medical evidence corroborates the link between the work environment and the aggravation. Dr. Mathison diagnosed Van Ness with mild persistent asthma exacerbated by volcanic smoke and pollution and noted that his condition improved "coincident to his residence in Oahu." Dr. Tom also noted the possibility of "vog contributing" and "vog on Maui" to Van Ness’s persistent symptoms. While Dr. Arora noted that vog is not unique to the school, this observation does not negate the fact that the intensity and duration of exposure were significantly greater at work due to the lack of filtration and the physical demands of the job.

The Director’s conclusion that there was "insufficient evidence" of a nexus because vog was island-wide ignores the statutory mandate of *Sato* and *Matsuura*. The law does not require the hazard to be unique to the workplace; it requires only that the employment conditions substantially contribute to the injury. The evidence that Van Ness was protected at home but exposed to dust, heat, and physical exertion at work, combined with medical testimony linking his symptoms to these conditions, establishes a genuine issue of material fact regarding whether employment was a substantial contributing factor.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Director committed legal error by applying the "nature of employment" test from *Flor v. Holguin* rather than the "substantial contributing factor" unitary test required by *Sato v. City & County of Honolulu* and *Matsuura v. State*. Based on the evidence provided, a reasonable fact-finder could conclude that Van Ness’s employment substantially contributed to the aggravation of his asthma.

Therefore, the Director’s decision denying the claim is VACATED, and the case is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

## REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT

**IN THE INTERMEDIATE COURT OF APPEALS OF THE STATE OF HAWAI'I**

The Defense’s opening statement rests on a fundamental misapplication of Hawai'i workers' compensation law. It incorrectly conflates the "nature of employment" test from *Flor v. Holguin* with the unitary test governing aggravations of pre-existing conditions. This Court’s preliminary ruling has already resolved this precise issue: *Flor* applies only to injuries "resulting from the nature of the employment," while aggravation claims are governed by the unitary test established in *Sato v. City & County of Honolulu* and reaffirmed in *Matsuura v. State*. The Defense’s argument fails to acknowledge this binding precedent and mischaracterizes the evidentiary record. We respectfully submit that the Director’s decision must be vacated.

### I. THE DEFENSE MISREADS *FLOR* AND IGNORES THIS COURT’S PRELIMINARY RULING

The Defense claims that *Flor v. Holguin* "articulated the standard for determining whether an injury is compensable" and "governs whether any injury, including aggravations of pre-existing conditions, is compensable." *Flor*, 111 Haw. at 366, 141 P.3d at 1149. This is categorically incorrect. *Flor* explicitly limits its "nature of employment" test to injuries "resulting from the nature of the employment"—that is, occupational diseases inherent to a specific trade or profession, such as a firefighter’s smoke-related lung damage. *Id.* The Defense’s attempt to extend *Flor* to all aggravation claims is a misreading of Hawai'i law.

This Court’s preliminary ruling correctly held that *Flor* "is limited to injuries 'resulting from the nature of the employment'" and is "inapposite to the facts of this case." *See Preliminary Ruling at III.A.* Here, Van Ness’s employment as a technology coordinator did not inherently involve exposure to volcanic vog. Vog is an environmental hazard affecting the general public—not a risk peculiar to repairing computers. The Defense ignores this distinction and wrongly treats *Flor* as a universal causation test. As this Court stated: "Where, as here, a claimant alleges that a pre-existing condition was aggravated by environmental exposure encountered during employment, the appropriate inquiry is whether the employment was a substantial contributing factor to the injury." *Id.* at III.B.

The Defense’s reliance on *Sato* and *Matsuura* as mere "substantial factor analysis within the *Flor* framework" is equally flawed. *Sato* and *Matsuura* established the unitary test as a distinct standard for aggravation claims. *Sato*, 59 Haw. at 558, 584 P.2d at 123 ("[A]n injury arises out of employment if the employment is a 'substantial contributing factor' to the injury, even if it is not the sole cause"). *Matsuura* expressly confirmed this standard applies to "aggravations of pre-existing conditions." *Matsuura*, 122 Haw. at 470, 228 P.3d at 434. The Defense’s assertion that *Sato* and *Matsuura* "do not create a separate 'unitary test' that supersedes *Flor*" is directly contradicted by this Court’s preliminary ruling and binding precedent. *Flor* and the unitary test are separate doctrines for distinct factual scenarios. *Flor* governs occupational diseases; the unitary test governs aggravations of pre-existing conditions. The Defense’s conflation of these doctrines warrants reversal.

### II. THE DIRECTOR ERRED BY APPLYING THE "SPECIAL HAZARD" STANDARD TO A UNITARY TEST CASE

The Defense argues that "an injury arises out of employment only when the employment creates a risk or hazard to which the general public is not equally exposed." This "special hazard" argument is legally irrelevant. The unitary test does not require the hazard to be unique to the workplace. *Sato* and *Matsuura* explicitly reject this requirement. *Sato* held that "the law does not require the hazard to be unique to the workplace; it requires only that the employment conditions substantially contribute to the injury." *Sato*, 59 Haw. at 558, 584 P.2d at 123 (emphasis added). *Matsuura* reaffirmed that "the unitary test applies to aggravations of pre-existing conditions" without any "uniqueness" prerequisite. *Matsuura*, 122 Haw. at 470, 228 P.3d at 434.

The Director and Defense erroneously applied a "special hazard" standard to this case. The Director found "insufficient evidence" of a nexus because "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment." This reasoning—relied upon by the Defense—is legally incorrect. Under the unitary test, the fact that vog exists island-wide does not preclude compensability. What matters is whether the *employment conditions* substantially contributed to the aggravation. *Sato*, 59 Haw. at 558, 584 P.2d at 123. The Director’s error is fatal: he applied the wrong legal standard by focusing on whether vog was "unique" to the workplace, rather than whether the workplace environment substantially contributed to the aggravation.

### III. SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE SUPPORTS THAT EMPLOYMENT WAS A SUBSTANTIAL CONTRIBUTING FACTOR

The Defense dismisses the evidence showing workplace-specific exposure, but the record contains uncontroverted facts establishing that Van Ness’s employment conditions substantially contributed to his aggravated asthma.

First, the disparity between Van Ness’s home and workplace environments is material. Van Ness’s home in Kihei was "climate-controlled with air conditioning and equipped with high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters and 'Bionic Breeze' units." Second, the Defense wrongly argues that "Vice Principal Dennis testified that the vog was 'even worse' in Kihei where Van Ness lived, suggesting his home environment presented equal or greater exposure." This misstates the record. While Vice Principal Dennis noted vog was "even worse" in Kihei, she also confirmed that Van Ness "spent little time outside, other than when he was at work." *See Record at *548.* Crucially, the record shows Van Ness’s home was equipped with HEPA filtration and climate control—measures that mitigated vog exposure at home. His workplace lacked these protections. The Defense ignores this key distinction: the *same vog* caused greater harm at work due to the absence of environmental controls. Under the unitary test, this is precisely the type of evidence that establishes employment as a substantial contributing factor.

Third, the Defense’s claim that "Dr. Mathison’s report does not establish that employment was a substantial contributing factor" is contradicted by the report itself. Dr. Mathison diagnosed Van Ness with "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and explicitly warned that "the risk for serious and potentially fatal asthma exacerbation with exposure to volcanic products, a well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients." *See Record at *551.* He further concluded that Van Ness "can be considered to be disabled by virtue of his asthmatic tendency and susceptibility to the volcanic pollutant effects" and that his employer should "not place him at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution." *Id.* (emphasis added). The Defense’s argument fails because it misapplies *Flor* to a case governed by the unitary test. **UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the court on appeal from the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations’ decision denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. The Defense concedes that the "unitary test" governs this claim but argues that the Prosecution failed to meet its burden of proof under that standard. For the reasons stated below, the court adheres to its preliminary ruling: the Director committed legal error by applying the incorrect legal standard, and the decision must be vacated and remanded.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Director's conclusions of law are reviewed *de novo*. See *Fujimoto v. State*, 70 Haw. 566, 570, 779 P.2d 828, 831 (1989). Where the Director has applied an incorrect legal standard, the decision must be vacated and the case remanded for application of the correct standard. *See Sato v. City & County of Honolulu*, 59 Haw. 552, 558, 584 P.2d 119, 123 (1978).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director’s Application of the "Special Hazard" Requirement Was Legal Error**

The Defense correctly acknowledges that the unitary test, rather than the "nature of employment" test articulated in *Flor v. Holguin*, 111 Haw. 356, 141 P.3d 1139 (2006), governs claims for the aggravation of pre-existing conditions. *See Matsuura v. State*, 122 Haw. 459, 470, 228 P.3d 423, 434 (2010). However, the Defense argues that the Director’s decision should be affirmed because the Prosecution failed to prove that employment was a substantial contributing factor.

This argument cannot overcome the Director’s explicit legal error. The Director denied the claim specifically because "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and found "insufficient evidence" of a nexus because the exposure was not unique to the workplace. This reasoning imposes a "special hazard" or "uniqueness" requirement that is incompatible with the unitary test. Under *Sato*, an injury arises out of employment if the employment is a "substantial contributing factor," regardless of whether the risk is unique to the workplace. *Sato*, 59 Haw. at 558, 584 P.2d at 123. The Director’s conclusion was predicated on this erroneous legal premise; therefore, the decision cannot stand.

**B. Substantial Evidence Supports a Finding that Employment Was a Substantial Contributing Factor**

The Defense contends that even under the unitary test, the evidence does not support compensability. The court disagrees. A review of the record reveals substantial evidence that Van Ness’s employment conditions substantially contributed to his respiratory injury.

First, the Defense argues that vog was "even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, and that his home environment was therefore equally or more hazardous. This argument ignores the critical distinction in the record regarding *mitigation*. Van Ness testified that his home was air-conditioned and equipped with high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters and "Bionic Breeze" units. In contrast, the workplace at Lahainaluna High School consisted of non-air-conditioned buildings with louvered windows and fans, allowing dust and volcanic ash to accumulate. The presence of vog island-wide does not negate the fact that Van Ness’s exposure at work was unmitigated, while his exposure at home was filtered. The difference in environmental controls between the home and the workplace is material to the "substantial contributing factor" inquiry.

Second, the Defense downplays the physical demands of the employment. Van Ness testified that he was required to repeatedly climb stairs and hike up steep terrain to service computers, spending less than five percent of his time in his air-conditioned office. He stated that this exertion caused him to stop to catch his breath and exacerbated his shortness of breath. The combination of unmitigated vog exposure and significant physical exertion distinguishes the workplace environment from the home environment.

Third, the Defense asserts that Dr. Mathison’s report does not establish causation. However, Dr. Mathison diagnosed Van Ness with "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and explicitly recommended that the employer "not place him at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution." This opinion directly links the risk of exacerbation to the employment assignment. While Dr. Arora noted that vog is not unique to schools, he also admitted he could not address the referral questions without access to medical records. The Director’s reliance on Dr. Arora’s preliminary observation over Dr. Mathison’s detailed medical opinion was an abuse of discretion, particularly where Dr. Mathison’s opinion supports a finding of compensability.

**C. The Claimant Met His Burden of Proof**

The Defense correctly notes that the claimant bears the burden of proving that employment was a substantial contributing factor. *Sato*, 59 Haw. at 558, 584 P.2d at 123. However, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the claimant, as required, the record contains substantial evidence to meet this burden. The testimony regarding the lack of filtration at work, the physical exertion required, and the medical opinions linking volcanic exposure to the aggravation of his condition constitute sufficient evidence for a reasonable fact-finder to conclude that employment was a substantial contributing factor. The Director’s finding to the contrary was influenced by the erroneous legal standard that the hazard must be unique to the workplace.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Defense’s arguments regarding the weight of the evidence are more appropriately addressed by the Director on remand applying the correct legal standard. Because the Director applied the wrong standard of law—requiring the hazard to be unique to the workplace—the decision denying the claim is vacated. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

May it please the Court:

The Defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the Prosecution's closing statement and in support of affirming the Director's decision, albeit with the acknowledgment that the Director applied an incorrect legal standard. While the Defense maintains that the Director's ultimate conclusion—that the claim should be denied—was correct, the Defense agrees with this Court's determination that the case must be remanded for proper application of the unitary test. The Defense submits this argument to clarify the record and to ensure that on remand, the Director considers the appropriate factors in reaching a new determination.

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a question of first impression regarding the application of the unitary test to claims involving the aggravation of pre-existing respiratory conditions by environmental exposures that exist island-wide. The Defense does not dispute that the unitary test, as articulated in Sato v. City & County of Honolulu, 59 Haw. 552, 558, 584 P.2d 119, 123 (1978), and clarified in Matsuura v. State, 122 Haw. 459, 470, 228 P.3d 423, 434 (2010), governs the claimant's burden of proof. However, the Defense submits that the Prosecution has mischaracterized the record and the applicable legal framework, and that on remand, the Director should carefully evaluate the evidence under the correct standard without improperly shifting the burden of proof to the Defense.

## II. THE DIRECTOR'S LEGAL ERROR DOES NOT REQUIRE REVERSAL ON THE MERITS

### A. The Defense Acknowledges the Legal Error but Maintains the Conclusion Was Correct

The Defense acknowledges that the Director committed legal error by imposing a "special hazard" or "uniqueness" requirement that is incompatible with the unitary test. As this Court correctly noted, the Director improperly focused on whether "vog was present on the entire island of Maui" rather than whether the employment setting substantially contributed to the injury. This was error.

However, the existence of legal error does not automatically require reversal on the merits. The Defense submits that the proper remedy—vacatur and remand—should result in a new determination by the Director, not a finding by this Court that the claim is compensable as a matter of law. The Prosecution's closing argument improperly conflates the existence of legal error with a determination that the claimant has met his burden of proof. This Court should clarify that remand is for a new hearing, not for entry of judgment in the claimant's favor.

### B. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Burden of Proof

The Prosecution repeatedly asserts that "substantial evidence supports" compensability, but this Court has not made such a finding. Rather, this Court found only that "substantial evidence supports a finding that employment was a substantial contributing factor"—a different proposition entirely. The distinction matters because this Court explicitly stated that "the Defense's arguments regarding the weight of the evidence are more appropriately addressed by the Director on remand."

The Prosecution's closing argument improperly attempts to have this Court make factual determinations that belong with the Director. The unitary test requires the claimant to prove that employment was a substantial contributing factor—not that it "substantially contributed" in the sense that this Court has found evidence sufficient to support such a finding. See Sato, 59 Haw. at 558, 584 P.2d at 123 (holding that the claimant bears the burden of proving that employment "contributed substantially" to the injury).

## III. ON REMAND, THE DIRECTOR SHOULD APPLY THE CORRECT STANDARD WITHOUT IMPROPERLY SHIFTING THE BURDEN

### A. The Unitary Test Requires Proof That Employment Was a Substantial Contributing Factor

Under the unitary test, as articulated in Sato and Matsuura, the claimant bears the burden of proving that employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. This standard requires more than mere temporal proximity or the presence of a workplace environment that contained some element of the risk. The claimant must demonstrate that the employment conditions meaningfully contributed to the injury—not merely that the claimant was exposed to a risk that existed elsewhere.

The Defense submits that the Prosecution has failed to appreciate the distinction between "substantial contributing factor" and mere "but for" causation. Under a strict "but for" analysis, almost any condition existing at the workplace could be said to have "caused" an injury in the sense that the injury would not have occurred but for the claimant's presence at work. The unitary test rejects such a broad interpretation and requires a meaningful contribution—not merely that the employment provided the forum for the injury to occur.

### B. The Evidence Does Not Establish That Employment Was a Substantial Contributing Factor

The Defense submits that even under the unitary test, the evidence does not support a finding that Van Ness's employment substantially contributed to his respiratory injury. The Prosecution's arguments rely on misreadings of the record and improper crediting of speculative medical opinions.

#### 1. The Home Environment Was Not "Safer" Than the Workplace

The Prosecution argues that Van Ness's home environment was mitigated by air conditioning and HEPA filters, while the workplace was not. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the record establishes that the vog was "even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, than at Lahainaluna School. Vice Principal Dennis's letter specifically stated that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei than at the school. This uncontroverted evidence undermines the Prosecution's attempt to characterize the home as "safer."

Second, Van Ness himself testified that he spent "little time outside" his home and that he was "not much of an outdoors person." He further testified that he did not have "any problems with volcanic ash inside his car or his home." This suggests that his exposure at home was minimal regardless of filtration.

Third, and most critically, the Prosecution's argument conflates "mitigation" with "contribution." Even if the home environment was better mitigated than the workplace, this does not establish that the workplace "substantially contributed" to the injury. The question under Sato is whether employment contributed substantially—not whether the workplace was relatively worse than other locations. If the vog existed island-wide and Van Ness was exposed to vog both at home and at work, the mere fact that his home had better filtration does not establish that his employment was a substantial contributing factor. It establishes only that his home exposure was less severe—a different question entirely.

#### 2. Dr. Mathison's Opinion Does Not Establish Causation

The Prosecution heavily relies on Dr. Mathison's opinion, but this reliance is misplaced. Dr. Mathison's report contains several critical limitations that undermine its probative value.

First, Dr. Mathison did not examine Van Ness until February 14, 2008—more than two years after the alleged injury occurred. His opinion is necessarily based on a retrospective reconstruction of events, not on contemporaneous observations.

Second, Dr. Mathison's recommendation that the employer "not place him at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution" is a forward-looking accommodation recommendation, not a retrospective causation opinion. Dr. Mathison was advising on future workplace assignments, not opining that the prior employment actually caused the injury. This distinction is critical: a physician's recommendation that a patient avoid certain exposures in the future does not establish that past exposures caused the patient's current condition.

Third, Dr. Mathison's report notes that Van Ness's symptoms "improved coincident to his residence in Oahu" but does not establish that the improvement was due to the absence of workplace exposure rather than the absence of island-wide exposure. Van Ness moved to Oahu in November 2006, and by June 2007, Dr. Tom restricted him from "walking too far." The temporal correlation with leaving Maui, not the workplace, undermines the Prosecution's theory.

#### 3. The Physical Exertion Theory Fails

The Prosecution suggests that the physical demands of the employment—climbing stairs and traversing steep terrain—combined with vog exposure to "substantially contribute" to the injury. This argument fails because the record does not establish that the physical exertion caused or aggravated the respiratory condition.

Van Ness testified that his symptoms included "shortness of breath" and "wheezing" when climbing stairs, but he did not testify that the physical exertion caused his asthma to worsen beyond the vog exposure itself. The testimony establishes only that climbing stairs was difficult because of his pre-existing condition—not that the physical exertion aggravated the condition.

Moreover, the Prosecution does not cite any medical evidence establishing that physical exertion combined with vog exposure causes more severe respiratory injury than vog exposure alone. Without such evidence, the argument that the physical demands of the employment substantially contributed to the injury is speculative.

#### 4. The Timing of Symptoms Does Not Establish Causation

The Prosecution argues that Van Ness's symptoms "worsened during employment on Maui" and "improved after leaving Maui." This temporal argument fails because it does not account for the island-wide nature of the vog exposure.

Van Ness was exposed to vog both at work and at home on Maui. His symptoms may have worsened during his time on Maui because of island-wide exposure, not because of workplace exposure specifically. The fact that symptoms improved after leaving Maui is consistent with both theories—the workplace causation theory and the island-wide exposure theory.

Under the unitary test, the claimant must establish that employment substantially contributed—not merely that symptoms changed during employment. The temporal evidence, without more, does not establish causation.

## IV. THE DIRECTOR SHOULD CONSIDER THE PROPER FACTORS ON REMAND

### A. The "Whole Person" Doctrine and Apportionment

The Defense submits that on remand, the Director should consider the "whole person" doctrine and principles of apportionment. Under this doctrine, when a pre-existing condition is aggravated by employment, compensation is available only for the portion of the disability attributable to the aggravation—not for the pre-existing condition itself. See Matsuura, 122 Haw. at 470, 228 P.3d at 434.

Here, Van Ness had a long-standing history of "mild persistent asthma" that he "had largely controlled through avoidances and prescribed medications." He had previously experienced respiratory symptoms requiring systemic corticosteroids when exposed to vog in 1989. The record establishes that his condition was pre-existing and partially controlled.

On remand, the Director should consider what portion of Van Ness's respiratory disability, if any, was attributable to the alleged workplace aggravation as opposed to the pre-existing condition or island-wide vog exposure. The Prosecution has presented no evidence establishing the degree of apportionment.

### B. The Special Relevance of Dr. Arora's Observations

Although the Prosecution dismisses Dr. Arora's observations as "legally irrelevant," the Defense submits that Dr. Arora's observation that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" is relevant to the weight of the evidence, if not to the legal standard.

Dr. Arora noted this observation in the context of explaining that the claim "is based on the assumption that since exposure to vog occurred also at the school, it is a work related exacerbation of asthma." His observation is relevant to the question of whether the workplace exposure was a substantial contributing factor as opposed to merely providing the forum for exposure that existed island-wide.

The Prosecution is correct that Sato rejects a "uniqueness" requirement as a matter of law. However, the absence of a uniqueness requirement does not mean that uniqueness is irrelevant to the weight of the evidence. If vog exists island-wide and the claimant has not established that workplace exposure was meaningfully different from home exposure, the evidence may be insufficient to establish that employment substantially contributed.

## V. CONCLUSION

The Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the vacatur of the Director's decision but clarify that remand is for a new determination under the correct legal standard, not for automatic allowance of the claim. The Prosecution has mischaracterized the record and the applicable law, and this Court should ensure that on remand, the Director applies the unitary test without improperly shifting the burden of proof to the Defense.

The Defense further requests that this Court provide guidance to the Director on remand to consider: (1) the whole person doctrine and apportionment; (2) the relevance of island-wide exposure to the substantial contributing factor inquiry; (3) the limitations of Dr. Mathison's forward-looking recommendations as evidence of retrospective causation; and (4) the proper allocation of the burden of proof.

**RESPECTFULLY SUBMITTED,**

Counsel for the Defense/Defendant

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. For the reasons stated below, the court adheres to its preliminary ruling: the Director committed legal error by applying an incorrect standard of law. Consequently, the Director’s decision is vacated, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Director's conclusions of law are reviewed *de novo*. *Fujimoto v. State*, 70 Haw. 566, 570, 779 P.2d 828, 831 (1989). Where the Director has applied an incorrect legal standard, the decision must be vacated and the case remanded for application of the correct standard. *Sato v. City & County of Honolulu*, 59 Haw. 552, 558, 584 P.2d 119, 123 (1978).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director’s Application of the "Special Hazard" Requirement Was Legal Error**

The Defense correctly concedes that the Director committed legal error by requiring the hazard at issue to be unique to the workplace. The Director denied the claim specifically because "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and found "insufficient evidence" of a nexus because the exposure was not unique to the workplace. This reasoning imposes a "special hazard" or "uniqueness" requirement that is incompatible with the "unitary test" governing claims for the aggravation of pre-existing conditions.

Under *Sato*, an injury arises out of employment if the employment is a "substantial contributing factor," regardless of whether the risk is unique to the workplace. *Sato*, 59 Haw. at 558, 584 P.2d at 123. The unitary test, as clarified in *Matsuura v. State*, 122 Haw. 459, 470, 228 P.3d 423, 434 (2010), applies to claims involving the aggravation of pre-existing conditions, such as Van Ness’s asthma. The Director’s reliance on the "nature of employment" test articulated in *Flor v. Holguin*, 111 Haw. 356, 141 P.3d 1139 (2006), was misplaced. *Flor* addresses injuries "by disease resulting from the nature of the employment," not the aggravation of a pre-existing condition by environmental factors present at the workplace. *Matsuura*, 122 Haw. at 470, 228 P.3d at 434. Because the Director’s conclusion was predicated on this erroneous legal premise, the decision cannot stand.

**B. The Case Must Be Remanded for Application of the Correct Legal Standard**

The Defense argues that, despite the legal error, the claim should be denied because the Prosecution failed to meet its burden of proof under the unitary test. The Defense contends that the evidence does not establish that employment was a "substantial contributing factor," pointing to the island-wide nature of the vog and the fact that Van Ness’s home in Kihei may have had worse vog conditions.

The Prosecution, conversely, argues that substantial evidence supports compensability. The Prosecution highlights the distinction between Van Ness’s home environment, which was equipped with air conditioning and high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters, and his work environment at Lahainaluna High School, which consisted of non-air-conditioned buildings with louvered windows and fans that allowed dust and volcanic ash to accumulate. The Prosecution also emphasizes the physical exertion required by the job, including climbing stairs and hiking up steep terrain, which Van Ness testified exacerbated his shortness of breath. Furthermore, the Prosecution cites the opinion of Dr. Mathison, who diagnosed Van Ness with "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and recommended that the employer avoid placing him in areas with such exposure.

The court’s role on appellate review is not to re-weigh the evidence or make factual determinations. *Fujimoto*, 70 Haw. at 570, 779 P.2d at 831. The existence of conflicting evidence—such as the dispute over the relative severity of vog exposure at home versus work, or the differing medical opinions of Dr. Mathison and Dr. Arora—presents a question of fact for the Director. Because the Director evaluated these facts through the lens of the incorrect legal standard—requiring the hazard to be unique to the workplace—the factual findings are tainted.

The Defense’s argument that the Prosecution failed to meet its burden of proof is a factual argument that must be addressed by the Director on remand applying the correct legal standard. On remand, the Director must determine whether the employment conditions, including the unmitigated exposure to vog and the physical exertion required to traverse the campus, were a "substantial contributing factor" to the aggravation of Van Ness’s asthma. *Sato*, 59 Haw. at 558, 584 P.2d at 123. The Director must consider the evidence regarding the mitigation measures at home versus work, the medical opinions linking the work environment to the exacerbation, and the physical demands of the employment, without the impermissible requirement that the risk be unique to the workplace.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Director applied the wrong standard of law by requiring the hazard to be unique to the workplace. This legal error necessitates vacatur of the decision. The case is remanded to the Director for further proceedings consistent with this ruling, including a determination of whether Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to his injury under the unitary test established in *Sato* and *Matsuura*.
