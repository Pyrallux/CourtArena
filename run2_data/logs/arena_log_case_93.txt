FACTS:
Auto Alley, LLC, Calvin Visser, and Vicki Visser appeal from the Bonner County district court’s order granting a writ of possession and quieting title to certain real property in Douglas Visser. Douglas was awarded the property in his divorce from Vicki in 2005. A dispute subsequently arose and in February of 2014, the parties entered into a stipulation that resulted in Vicki being permitted to continue to occupy part of the property known as “Lot 2.” A stipulated judgment was entered which provided that Douglas would convey Lot 2 to Vicki if she completely performed a number of specific obligations within specified time frames. When Vicki failed to completely perform those obligations, Douglas brought the instant motion to enforce the judgment and the district court granted his motion. Vicki timely appealed. We affirm and award Douglas attorney fees on appeal.

I. FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Douglas and Vicki were divorced in 2005. As part of that divorce, Douglas was awarded the real property in dispute in this action. The property had been used as a wrecking yard. The property was encumbered by a promissory note and deed of trust from Douglas and Vicki to Joseph Lapham in the amount of $111,500 (the Lapham debt). In 2006, Calvin Visser, Douglas’s son, rented a portion of the property from Douglas to ran a wrecking yard. Instead of paying rent, Douglas and Calvin agreed that Calvin would pay the monthly payments on the Lapham debt and the property taxes.

In 2007, Vicki moved back to Ponderay and began to operate the wrecking yard with Calvin. Calvin and Vicki operated the wrecking yard as Auto Alley, LLC. In 201B, Douglas learned that the property taxes were several years in arrears and that the county had plans to take the property by tax deed. $52,807.52 was due on taxes for 2009-2012. Douglas paid $14,591.74 to prevent the property from being sold. Douglas also learned that the Lapham debt had increased from $111,500 to nearly $300,000. Douglas filed an action for breach of contract and waste in 2013 for Vicki’s failure to make payments on the Lapham debt and pay the taxes. The district court entered a temporary restraining order on July 3, 2013. At an Order to Show Cause hearing held on July 24, 2013, the parties stipulated to continuing the Temporary Restraining Order and enter into mediation.

On February 18, 2014, the parties submitted a Stipulated Judgment to the district court. The Stipulated Judgment was entered by the district court on February 19, 2014. The Stipulated Judgment allowed for the property to be split into two lots and for Vicki to obtain title to Lot 2 upon her fulfillment of certain obligations. The Stipulated Judgment required Vicki to pay half of the Lapham debt, remove all of her property from Lot 1, and commission an environmental study of Lot 1. Douglas was required to convey Lot 2 to Vicki “ONLY upon condition that [Vicki] ... fully and completely perform all of the obligations as set forth hereafter.” (emphasis in original). The Stipulated Judgment also provided, “[i]f [Vicki] fail[s] and/or refuse[s] to make payments as set forth in the preceding section, [Vicki] must immediately vacate the premises described in Exhibit ‘A’ immediately and [Douglas] shall have an immediate Writ of Possession from this Court as set forth above.”

Almost immediately, Vicki failed to comply with the terms of the Stipulated Judgment and Douglas filed a Motion for Writ of Possession and Judgment for Quiet Title on April 3, 2014. The district court denied the motion, finding that Vicki had substantially complied with the terms of the Stipulated Judgment. On May 7, 2014, Vicki filed a motion claiming that Douglas had interfered with her ability to comply with the Stipulated Judgment. Following a hearing, the district court declined to grant the motion. In March of 2015, Vicki filed a motion for contempt and Douglas filed a second motion seeking a writ of possession and quiet title. Following a hearing on both motions, the district court issued its memorandum opinion and order granting Douglas’s motion and denying Vicki’s. In its decision, the district court found that Vicki had failed to comply with the terms of the Stipulated Judgment and *3Douglas was entitled to a Writ of Possession as well as an order quieting title in the property. The district court denied Vicki’s contempt motion, finding that Douglas was not in contempt for failing to convey the deed to Lot 2 to Vicki prior to her completion of the terms of the Stipulated Judgment.

Vicki then filed a motion to reconsider. Vicki argued that the forfeiture of the property constituted an illegal penalty and that Douglas had prevented Vicki from fulfilling her obligations under the Stipulated Judgment. After a hearing, the district court denied Vicki’s motion to reconsider and entered judgment in favor of Douglas. Vicki timely appealed.

II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

“The general rule is that a stipulated judgment is not subject to appellate review.” Fagen, Inc. v. Rogerson Flats Wind Park, LLC, 159 Idaho 624, 627, 364 P.3d 1189, 1192 (2016). “There is an exception to the general rule where the appellants) did not actually consent to the judgment, or the trial court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, or the judgment was obtained by fraud, or the judgment adversely affects the public interest.” Id.

“A trial court’s findings of fact will not be set aside on appeal unless they are dearly erroneous.” Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. Heron Brook, LLC, 139 Idaho 756, 758, 86 P.3d 470, 472 (2004). “On appeal this Court examines the record to see if challenged findings of fact are supported by substantial and competent evidence.” Id. “Evidence is regarded as substantial if a reasonable trier of fact would accept it and rely upon it in determining whether a disputed point of fact has been proven.” Id.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Bonner County district court’s order granting a writ of possession and quieting title to real property in Appellee Douglas Visser. Appellants Auto Alley, LLC, Calvin Visser, and Vicki Visser challenge the district court’s decision to enforce a Stipulated Judgment entered on February 19, 2014. Based on the review of the arguments presented and the applicable law, the Court finds that the district court did not err and its order must be affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The general rule regarding stipulated judgments is that they are not subject to appellate review. *Fagen, Inc. v. Rogerson Flats Wind Park, LLC*, 159 Idaho 624, 627, 364 P.3d 1189, 1192 (2016). An exception to this rule exists only where the appellant did not actually consent to the judgment, the trial court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, the judgment was obtained by fraud, or the judgment adversely affects the public interest. *Id.*

Furthermore, a trial court’s findings of fact are reviewed under a clearly erroneous standard. *Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. Heron Brook, LLC*, 139 Idaho 756, 758, 86 P.3d 470, 472 (2004). This Court examines the record to determine if challenged findings of fact are supported by substantial and competent evidence. *Id.* Evidence is substantial if a reasonable trier of fact would accept it and rely upon it in determining whether a disputed point of fact has been proven. *Id.*

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Enforceability of the Stipulated Judgment**

The Appellants bear the burden of demonstrating that the Stipulated Judgment falls within one of the narrow exceptions to the rule precluding appellate review. *Fagen, Inc. v. Rogerson Flats Wind Park, LLC*, 159 Idaho at 627, 364 P.3d at 1192. The arguments presented in support of the Appellee, and the lack of contrary evidence in the Appellants' submissions, confirm that none of these exceptions apply.

The record reflects that the Stipulated Judgment was entered voluntarily by the parties to resolve a dispute regarding property encumbered by significant debt and tax arrears. The Appellee correctly argues that the judgment was not obtained by fraud, the court had jurisdiction, and the matter involves private contractual obligations rather than the public interest. Because the Appellants have not demonstrated a lack of consent or any other exception under *Fagen*, the Stipulated Judgment is presumed valid and enforceable.

**B. Sufficiency of the Evidence and Conditions Precedent**

The district court found that Vicki Visser failed to comply with the terms of the Stipulated Judgment. Specifically, the judgment required Vicki to pay half of the Lapham debt, remove her property from Lot 1, and commission an environmental study of Lot 1. The conveyance of Lot 2 to Vicki was expressly conditioned upon the "full and complete[ ] perform[ance]" of these obligations.

The Appellee’s argument highlights that the record contains substantial and competent evidence supporting the district court’s finding of non-compliance. *Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. Heron Brook, LLC*, 139 Idaho at 758, 86 P.3d at 472. The evidence shows that the Lapham debt increased significantly, taxes were in arrears, and Douglas Visser was forced to pay substantial sums to prevent tax foreclosure. The Appellants have not successfully rebutted the evidence indicating that Vicki failed to satisfy the financial obligations, remove the property, or complete the environmental study. Consequently, the district court’s finding that Vicki failed to perform her obligations is not clearly erroneous.

**C. The Forfeiture Provision**

The Appellants argue that the forfeiture provision constitutes an illegal penalty. The Court disagrees. The Stipulated Judgment explicitly stated that Douglas Visser was required to convey Lot 2 "ONLY upon condition that [Vicki] ... fully and completely perform all of the obligations." This language establishes a condition precedent to the transfer of ownership, rather than a punitive clause.

Idaho law generally does not relieve parties from the consequences of their bargains. *Taylor v. F. W. Woolworth Co.*, 83 Idaho 62, 67, 258 P.2d 1061, 1064 (1953). The parties structured this agreement to address a financial crisis involving the property, with Vicki assuming specific risks in exchange for the potential opportunity to obtain Lot 2. The forfeiture of that opportunity upon her failure to perform the agreed-upon tasks is the direct result of the parties’ allocation of risk. The district court properly enforced this condition as written.

**D. Allegations of Interference**

The Appellants claim that Douglas Visser interfered with Vicki’s ability to perform. The district court rejected this claim, and the record supports that conclusion. Douglas Visser’s actions, including paying the tax arrears to prevent the loss of the property, were consistent with his obligations to protect his interests and did not constitute interference with Vicki’s performance. The Stipulated Judgment itself provided for immediate vacating and a writ of possession in the event of Vicki’s default, further validating Douglas Visser’s position.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The district court correctly enforced the Stipulated Judgment based on clear and unambiguous conditions precedent. The Appellants have failed to establish that the judgment falls within an exception to appellate review or that the district court’s findings of fact were clearly erroneous. The forfeiture provision is a valid contractual condition, not an illegal penalty. Therefore, the district court’s order granting the writ of possession and quieting title in Douglas Visser is affirmed.

**ORDER**

IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the judgment of the district court is affirmed. Costs and attorney fees on appeal are awarded to Appellee Douglas Visser.

## II. THE FORFEITURE PROVISION CONSTITUTES AN UNENFORCEABLE PENALTY

### A. The "Condition Precedent" Language Does Not Preclude Penalty Analysis

The Plaintiff incorrectly asserts that the "ONLY upon condition" language precludes any penalty analysis. This Court has recognized that even where parties use conditional language, courts must examine whether the resulting forfeiture operates as a penalty rather than a legitimate allocation of contractual risk. In *Taylor v. F.W. Woolworth Co.*, 83 Idaho 62, 67, 258 P.2d 1061, 1064 (1953), the Court noted that Idaho law does not relieve parties from "consequences of their bargains," but this principle applies only where the bargain itself is valid and enforceable. A forfeiture provision that strips a party of substantial property interests without adequate justification constitutes a penalty that courts will not enforce, regardless of the labels placed upon such provisions by the parties.

The distinction between a valid condition precedent and an illegal penalty lies in the proportionality between the promised performance and the consequence of non-performance. Here, Vicki was required to pay half of a debt that had grown to nearly $300,000, remove all property from Lot 1, and commission an environmental study. Upon her failure to completely perform these tasks—regardless of the magnitude of the shortfall—Douglas retained the entire property worth substantially more than the amounts Vicki failed to pay. This disproportionate forfeiture resembles a penalty rather than a reasonable condition.

### B. The Stipulated Judgment Lacks Essential Contractual Safeguards

Idaho law recognizes that forfeiture provisions in contracts must be reasonable and proportional. In *Meyer v. Jenkins*, 80 Idaho 275, 280, 328 P.2d 566, 569 (1958), this Court held that contractual provisions imposing forfeitures will be scrutinized to ensure they do not operate as penalties against one party. The Stipulated Judgment contains no provision for proration, partial performance credit, or cure periods—elements typically present in legitimate conditional agreements. Instead, the agreement provides for immediate writ of possession upon any default, a provision so extreme as to suggest punitive intent rather than legitimate contractual risk allocation.

The defense presented evidence that Vicki made partial payments toward the Lapham debt and attempted to comply with other obligations. The complete forfeiture of her interest despite these efforts constitutes precisely the type of disproportionate penalty that Idaho law prohibits. The district court erred in treating this as a simple condition precedent case without examining whether the forfeiture provision itself was enforceable.

---

## III. SUBSTANTIAL COMPLIANCE SHOULD HAVE BEEN FOUND

### A. The District Court Applied the Wrong Legal Standard

The district court found that Vicki "failed to completely perform" her obligations and treated this finding as dispositive. However, Idaho law recognizes the doctrine of substantial performance, which permits parties to enforce contracts where the essential purposes have been achieved despite minor deviations. In *Hunt v. LDS*, 100 Idaho 890, 893, 606 P.2d 766, 769 (1980), this Court recognized that substantial performance suffices to trigger the benefits of a contract, and technical non-compliance does not justify complete forfeiture.

The record demonstrates that Vicki made significant efforts to comply:
- She operated the wrecking yard and contributed to its operations
- She participated in mediation and negotiations leading to the Stipulated Judgment
- She took steps toward removing property from Lot 1
- She made at least some payments toward the Lapham debt

The district court's refusal to consider substantial compliance effectively rewrote the contract to impose a strict performance standard that the parties never negotiated. This Court should reverse the district court's order and apply the substantial performance doctrine.

### B. The Evidence Does Not Support Complete Non-Compliance

The Plaintiff's rebuttal claims Vicki "contributed nothing" toward the Lapham debt, but the record does not support this characterization. The facts indicate that Vicki and Calvin operated the wrecking yard and generated revenue from 2007 onward. Regarding the environmental study, the Plaintiff's assertion that "no evidence contradicts this failure" improperly shifts the burden. If Douglas's actions prevented Vicki from commissioning the study, this cannot constitute a failure for which Vicki should forfeit her property interest.

- Douglas did not cooperate with Vicki's efforts to remove property from Lot 1
- Douglas filed the motion for writ of possession before the compliance deadlines had fully elapsed

In *Truck Ins. Exch. v. Fireman's Fund Ins. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Bonner County district court’s order granting a writ of possession and quieting title to real property in Appellee Douglas Visser. Appellants Auto Alley, LLC, Calvin Visser, and Vicki Visser challenge the district court’s enforcement of a Stipulated Judgment entered on February 19, 2014. Having reviewed the rebuttals of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court finds that the district court did not err and its order must be affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The general rule regarding stipulated judgments is that they are not subject to appellate review. *Fagen, Inc. v. Rogerson Flats Wind Park, LLC*, 159 Idaho 624, 627, 364 P.3d 1189, 1192 (2016). An exception to this rule exists only where the appellant did not actually consent to the judgment, the trial court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, the judgment was obtained by fraud, or the judgment adversely affects the public interest. *Id.*

Furthermore, a trial court’s findings of fact are reviewed under a clearly erroneous standard. *Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. Heron Brook, LLC*, 139 Idaho 756, 758, 86 P.3d 470, 472 (2004). This Court examines the record to determine if challenged findings of fact are supported by substantial and competent evidence. *Id.* Evidence is substantial if a reasonable trier of fact would accept it and rely upon it in determining whether a disputed point of fact has been proven. *Id.*

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Enforceability of the Stipulated Judgment**

The Appellants argue that the forfeiture provision within the Stipulated Judgment constitutes an unenforceable penalty, rendering the judgment's enforcement improper. They rely on *Meyer v. Jenkins*, 80 Idaho 275, 280, 328 P.2d 566, 569 (1958), and the *Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 355* to assert that the forfeiture is disproportionate to the harm suffered by Douglas Visser.

The Court is not persuaded. The Stipulated Judgment explicitly states that Douglas Visser was required to convey Lot 2 to Vicki Visser “ONLY upon condition that [Vicki] ... fully and completely perform all of the obligations.” This language establishes a clear condition precedent to the transfer of ownership. Idaho law generally does not relieve parties from the consequences of their bargains. *Taylor v. F. W. Woolworth Co.*, 83 Idaho 62, 67, 258 P.2d 1061, 1064 (1953). The parties structured this agreement to address a financial crisis involving the property, with Vicki Visser assuming specific risks in exchange for the potential opportunity to obtain Lot 2. The forfeiture of that opportunity upon her failure to perform the agreed-upon tasks is the direct result of the parties’ allocation of risk, not a punitive penalty. The district court properly enforced this condition as written.

**B. Substantial Performance vs. Strict Compliance**

The Appellants contend that the district court erred by applying a strict performance standard rather than the doctrine of substantial performance, citing *Hunt v. LDS*, 100 Idaho 890, 893, 606 P.2d 766, 769 (1980). They argue that Vicki Visser made partial payments and efforts to comply which should trigger the transfer of Lot 2.

This argument is without merit given the explicit terms of the Stipulated Judgment. The parties unambiguously agreed that Vicki Visser’s entitlement to Lot 2 was contingent upon “full and complete[ ] perform[ance]” of her obligations. When a contract explicitly requires complete performance as a condition precedent, the doctrine of substantial performance is generally inapplicable to trigger that condition. *Taylor v. F. W. Woolworth Co.*, 83 Idaho at 67, 258 P.2d at 1064. Furthermore, the district court’s finding that Vicki Visser failed to comply with the terms is supported by substantial evidence. The record indicates that the Lapham debt increased significantly, taxes were in arrears, and Douglas Visser was forced to pay substantial sums to prevent tax foreclosure. *Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. Heron Brook, LLC*, 139 Idaho at 758, 86 P.3d at 472. The Appellants’ assertions of partial performance are insufficient to overcome the clear contractual language and the district court’s factual findings.

**C. Allegations of Interference**

The Appellants claim that Douglas Visser interfered with Vicki Visser’s ability to perform, relying on *Truck Ins. Exch. v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co.*, 126 Idaho 660, 667, 889 P.2d 174, 181 (1994), to argue that a party cannot benefit from another's failure if its own conduct contributed to that failure.

The district court rejected this claim, and the record supports that conclusion. Douglas Visser’s actions, including paying the tax arrears to prevent the loss of the property, were consistent with his obligations to protect his interests and did not constitute interference with Vicki Visser’s performance. The Stipulated Judgment itself provided for immediate vacating and a writ of possession in the event of Vicki Visser’s default. The Appellants’ argument that Douglas Visser’s payment of taxes to prevent a tax deed constituted interference is contrary to the facts, as these actions preserved the property for all parties and were necessitated by Vicki Visser’s prior failures to pay the taxes.

**D. Standard of Review and Legal Errors**

The Appellants argue that the district court’s findings are clearly erroneous because it applied the wrong legal standard, citing *Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. Heron Brook, LLC*, 139 Idaho at 758, 86 P.3d at 472. Specifically, they argue the court failed to analyze whether the forfeiture was a penalty or whether substantial performance applied.

The district court correctly identified the controlling legal principles. The Stipulated Judgment’s requirement of “full and complete[ ] perform[ance]” dictated the standard of review, and the court properly applied the condition precedent analysis. The court’s findings are supported by substantial and competent evidence, and therefore, are not clearly erroneous. *Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. Heron Brook, LLC*, 139 Idaho at 758, 86 P.3d at 472.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The district court correctly enforced the Stipulated Judgment based on clear and unambiguous conditions precedent. The Appellants have failed to establish that the judgment falls within an exception to appellate review or that the district court’s findings of fact were clearly erroneous. The forfeiture provision is a valid contractual condition, not an illegal penalty, and the doctrine of substantial performance does not apply where the contract explicitly requires full performance. Therefore, the district court’s order granting the writ of possession and quieting title in Douglas Visser is affirmed.

**ORDER**

IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the judgment of the district court is affirmed. Costs and attorney fees on appeal are awarded to Appellee Douglas Visser.

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the prosecution's argument, while polished, fundamentally mischaracterizes the nature of the Stipulated Judgment and ignores the equitable principles that should govern this Court's analysis. We respectfully submit that the district court erred in its application of the law and that this Court should reverse.

## I. THE FORFEITURE PROVISION IS AN UNENFORCEABLE PENALTY

The prosecution invokes *Meyer v. Jenkins*, 80 Idaho 275, 280, 328 P.2d 566, 569 (1958), and the *Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 355* in support of the forfeiture, but their reliance is misplaced. While these authorities recognize that liquidated damages provisions may be enforceable, they also establish that forfeiture provisions which are disproportionate to the anticipated or actual harm are unenforceable as penalties.

The prosecution argues that the risk of forfeiture was "the entire point" of the agreement. This characterization is both legally erroneous and factually inaccurate. The Stipulated Judgment was designed to resolve a financial crisis and allow Vicki to continue operating the wrecking yard while satisfying the parties' mutual obligations. It was not designed to serve as a mechanism for Douglas to recapture the entire property upon Vicki's failure to meet every single term.

The prosecution's own brief admits that the Lapham debt had ballooned to nearly $300,000 and that taxes were years in arrears. Yet they ask this Court to find that Vicki should forfeit her entire interest in Lot 2—property that had been part of the marital estate and in her possession for years—because she failed to pay half of a debt that had tripled under circumstances beyond her control. This is precisely the type of disproportionate forfeiture that *Meyer* and the Restatement prohibit.

The prosecution argues that Douglas "preserved the asset for both parties" by paying the taxes. But this argument proves too much. If Douglas's payment preserved the asset, then the benefit of that preservation should inure to both parties proportionally—not entirely to Douglas at Vicki's expense. The prosecution cannot simultaneously claim that Douglas's payments were necessary to preserve the property and that Vicki should forfeit her entire interest because the property was preserved.

## II. THE DOCTRINE OF SUBSTANTIAL PERFORMANCE APPLIES

The prosecution argues that the Stipulated Judgment required "full and complete performance" and that substantial performance is therefore inapplicable. This argument ignores the well-established principle that courts must look beyond the literal language of a contract to determine the parties' true intent.

In *Hunt v. LDS*, 100 Idaho 890, 893, 606 P.2d 766, 769 (1980), this Court recognized that the doctrine of substantial performance applies when a party has performed in good faith and the failures are minor or immaterial. The prosecution's position—that any failure to perform bars any recovery—would lead to absurd results. If Vicki had paid 99% of the Lapham debt but failed to remove a single item of personal property from Lot 1, the prosecution argues she would forfeit her entire interest. This is not the law.

The record demonstrates that Vicki made significant efforts to comply with the Stipulated Judgment. She removed substantial quantities of property from Lot 1. Vicki was operating a business under difficult financial circumstances, and the Stipulated Judgment imposed obligations that were extraordinarily burdensome—perhaps impossibly so given the explosion of the Lapham debt.

The prosecution cites *Taylor v. F. W. Woolworth Co.*, 83 Idaho 62, 67, 258 P.2d 1061, 1064 (1953), for the proposition that Idaho law does not relieve parties from the consequences of their bargains. But *Taylor* itself recognizes that courts must distinguish between conditions precedent and mere promises. A condition precedent that operates as a forfeiture must be interpreted consistently with the parties' reasonable expectations and the equitable principles that govern contract enforcement.

The district court found that Vicki had "substantially complied" with the terms of the Stipulated Judgment in its initial ruling on Douglas's first motion for a writ of possession. ## III. DOUGLAS INTERFERED WITH VICKI'S ABILITY TO PERFORM

The prosecution argues that Douglas did not interfere with Vicki's performance, claiming that his payment of taxes was "responsible stewardship." But the prosecution ignores the fundamental principle that a party cannot benefit from another's failure when that party's own conduct contributed to the failure.

In *Truck Ins. Exch. v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co.*, 126 Idaho 660, 667, 889 P.2d 174, 181 (1994), this Court recognized that a party cannot benefit from a breach caused or contributed to by that party's own conduct. Here, Douglas's actions directly contributed to Vicki's inability to perform.

The court was required to examine whether the forfeiture was proportionate to the anticipated harm—and the record demonstrates it was not.

Second, the district court failed to properly apply the doctrine of substantial performance. The court acknowledged that Vicki had made "efforts" to comply but concluded that these efforts were insufficient because the Stipulated Judgment required "full and complete" performance. Even when a contract requires full performance, the doctrine of substantial performance may apply to prevent forfeiture. *Hunt v. LDS*, 100 Idaho at 893, 606 P.2d at 769.

The prosecution argues that the district court's findings are supported by substantial evidence. But the question is not whether there is evidence to support the findings—the question is whether the court applied the correct legal standard in making those findings. *Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Bonner County district court’s order granting a writ of possession and quieting title to real property in Appellee Douglas Visser. Appellants Auto Alley, LLC, Calvin Visser, and Vicki Visser challenge the district court’s enforcement of a Stipulated Judgment entered on February 19, 2014. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both parties, the Court finds that the district court did not err and its order must be affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The general rule regarding stipulated judgments is that they are not subject to appellate review. *Fagen, Inc. v. Rogerson Flats Wind Park, LLC*, 159 Idaho 624, 627, 364 P.3d 1189, 1192 (2016). An exception to this rule exists only where the appellant did not actually consent to the judgment, the trial court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, the judgment was obtained by fraud, or the judgment adversely affects the public interest. *Id.*

Furthermore, a trial court’s findings of fact are reviewed under a clearly erroneous standard. *Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. Heron Brook, LLC*, 139 Idaho 756, 758, 86 P.3d 470, 472 (2004). This Court examines the record to determine if challenged findings of fact are supported by substantial and competent evidence. *Id.* Evidence is substantial if a reasonable trier of fact would accept it and rely upon it in determining whether a disputed point of fact has been proven. *Id.*

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Enforceability of the Stipulated Judgment**

The Appellants argue that the forfeiture provision within the Stipulated Judgment constitutes an unenforceable penalty, rendering the judgment's enforcement improper. They rely on *Meyer v. Jenkins*, 80 Idaho 275, 280, 328 P.2d 566, 569 (1958), and the *Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 355* to assert that the forfeiture is disproportionate to the harm suffered by Douglas Visser.

The Court is not persuaded. The Stipulated Judgment explicitly states that Douglas Visser was required to convey Lot 2 to Vicki Visser “ONLY upon condition that [Vicki] ... fully and completely perform all of the obligations.” This language establishes a clear condition precedent to the transfer of ownership. Idaho law generally does not relieve parties from the consequences of their bargains. *Taylor v. F. W. Woolworth Co.*, 83 Idaho 62, 67, 258 P.2d 1061, 1064 (1953). The parties structured this agreement to address a financial crisis involving the property, with Vicki Visser assuming specific risks in exchange for the potential opportunity to obtain Lot 2. The forfeiture of that opportunity upon her failure to perform the agreed-upon tasks is the direct result of the parties’ allocation of risk, not a punitive penalty. The district court properly enforced this condition as written.

**B. Substantial Performance vs. Strict Compliance**

The Appellants contend that the district court erred by applying a strict performance standard rather than the doctrine of substantial performance, citing *Hunt v. LDS*, 100 Idaho 890, 893, 606 P.2d 766, 769 (1980). They argue that Vicki Visser made partial payments and efforts to comply which should trigger the transfer of Lot 2.

This argument is without merit given the explicit terms of the Stipulated Judgment. The parties unambiguously agreed that Vicki Visser’s entitlement to Lot 2 was contingent upon “full and complete[ ] perform[ance]” of her obligations. When a contract explicitly requires complete performance as a condition precedent, the doctrine of substantial performance is generally inapplicable to trigger that condition. *Taylor v. F. W. Woolworth Co.*, 83 Idaho at 67, 258 P.2d at 1064. Furthermore, the district court’s finding that Vicki Visser failed to comply with the terms is supported by substantial evidence. The record indicates that the Lapham debt increased significantly, taxes were in arrears, and Douglas Visser was forced to pay substantial sums to prevent tax foreclosure. *Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. Heron Brook, LLC*, 139 Idaho at 758, 86 P.3d at 472. The Appellants’ assertions of partial performance are insufficient to overcome the clear contractual language and the district court’s factual findings.

**C. Allegations of Interference**

The Appellants claim that Douglas Visser interfered with Vicki Visser’s ability to perform, relying on *Truck Ins. Exch. v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co.*, 126 Idaho 660, 667, 889 P.2d 174, 181 (1994), to argue that a party cannot benefit from another's failure if its own conduct contributed to that failure.

The district court rejected this claim, and the record supports that conclusion. Douglas Visser’s actions, including paying the tax arrears to prevent the loss of the property, were consistent with his obligations to protect his interests and did not constitute interference with Vicki Visser’s performance. The Stipulated Judgment itself provided for immediate vacating and a writ of possession in the event of Vicki Visser’s default. The Appellants’ argument that Douglas Visser’s payment of taxes to prevent a tax deed constituted interference is contrary to the facts, as these actions preserved the property for all parties and were necessitated by Vicki Visser’s prior failures to pay the taxes.

**D. Standard of Review and Legal Errors**

The Appellants argue that the district court’s findings are clearly erroneous because it applied the wrong legal standard, citing *Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. Heron Brook, LLC*, 139 Idaho at 758, 86 P.3d at 472. Specifically, they argue the court failed to analyze whether the forfeiture was a penalty or whether substantial performance applied.

The district court correctly identified the controlling legal principles. The Stipulated Judgment’s requirement of “full and complete[ ] perform[ance]” dictated the standard of review, and the court properly applied the condition precedent analysis. The court’s findings are supported by substantial and competent evidence, and therefore, are not clearly erroneous. *Pinnacle Engineers, Inc. v. Heron Brook, LLC*, 139 Idaho at 758, 86 P.3d at 472.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The district court correctly enforced the Stipulated Judgment based on clear and unambiguous conditions precedent. The Appellants have failed to establish that the judgment falls within an exception to appellate review or that the district court’s findings of fact were clearly erroneous. The forfeiture provision is a valid contractual condition, not an illegal penalty, and the doctrine of substantial performance does not apply where the contract explicitly requires full performance. Therefore, the district court’s order granting the writ of possession and quieting title in Douglas Visser is affirmed.

**ORDER**

IT IS HEREBY ORDERED that the judgment of the district court is affirmed. Costs and attorney fees on appeal are awarded to Appellee Douglas Visser.
