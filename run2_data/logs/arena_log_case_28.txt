Your Honor, the State respectfully submits that 13 V.S.A. § 1062, as it existed in June 2015, was facially constitutional. A facial challenge to a statute is the most difficult to sustain, as the challenger must demonstrate that the statute is unconstitutional in all possible applications. United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987). The statute criminalizes a course of conduct intended to harass, where harassment is defined as actions directed at a specific person that would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death. This definition targets only unprotected true threats, which have long been held outside the scope of First Amendment protection. Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343, 359-360 (2003) ("The First Amendment permits a State to ban true threats. 'True threats' encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.").

The "reasonable person" standard is a well-established objective test used to determine whether speech constitutes a true threat and does not render the statute overbroad. In Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), the Supreme Court held that political hyperbole, such as a statement made at a rally that "if they ever make me carry a rifle the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J.," does not rise to the level of a true threat because it was conditional and contextually understood as non-serious. The statute, properly applied, would not criminalize such protected speech because it requires that the conduct would cause a reasonable person to fear specific violent acts—not mere annoyance, emotional distress, or abstract political commentary. The statute's focus on objective fear of concrete harms like bodily injury or death ensures it captures only unprotected expression.

Moreover, the statute is content-neutral. It does not regulate speech based on its subject matter or viewpoint but instead targets the effect of causing fear of specific unlawful acts. As the Court explained in R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, 505 U.S. 377, 386 (1992), content-based restrictions are those that "singl[e] out particular subject matter for differential treatment," but this statute applies equally to all threatening conduct regardless of the topic. Whether the threat relates to politics, personal grievances, or any other subject, the statute only prohibits conduct that would cause a reasonable person to fear unlawful violence. This is a content-neutral regulation of conduct, not speech, as it hinges on the objective impact of the actions, not the message conveyed.

Defendant's reliance on Elonis v. United States, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015), is misplaced. Elonis addressed the mens rea required for a federal statute but did not hold the statute facially unconstitutional; instead, it remanded for proper jury instructions on intent. The Vermont statute was properly interpreted by the trial court to require that the defendant acted "intentionally" with the purpose to harass, which satisfies the mens rea requirement for true threats. The Court in Elonis emphasized that the First Amendment does not permit criminal liability based solely on a reasonable person's perception of threat without intent, but the statute here explicitly requires intentional conduct aimed at causing harassment, aligning with constitutional standards.

Thus, there are numerous constitutional applications of the statute, such as when a defendant intentionally engages in conduct that would cause a reasonable person to fear bodily injury or death. Because the statute can be applied constitutionally in all relevant circumstances, it is not facially unconstitutional. Salerno, 481 U.S. at 745. The conviction should be affirmed.

## DEFENSE RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF'S OPENING ARGUMENT

---

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits that the Plaintiff's opening argument fails to address the fundamental constitutional deficiencies in the criminal stalking statute as applied to this case. The Defense requests that this Court reverse the conviction and dismiss the charges for the reasons set forth below.

---

## I. THE CRIMINAL STALKING STATUTE IS FACIALLY UNCONSTITUTIONAL

The Plaintiff correctly cites *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987), for the proposition that facial challenges are difficult to sustain. However, the Plaintiff misapplies this standard by failing to recognize that the statute at issue, 13 V.S.A. § 1062 (2015), suffers from a constitutional infirmity that renders it unconstitutional in all its applications: it fails to require adequate mens rea for the threat element.

The Plaintiff argues that the statute targets only "true threats" as defined in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359-360 (2003). However, the statute as written does not require that the defendant actually intend to threaten violence. Instead, it defines harassment based solely on how a "reasonable person" would interpret the conduct, without requiring the defendant to subjectively intend the threatened harm. This renders the statute unconstitutional under *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015).

In *Elonis*, the United States Supreme Court held that liability under a threat statute cannot attach based solely on how a reasonable person would perceive communications without requiring some form of criminal intent. The Court emphasized that "the mental state required to conviction under the [federal threat statute] depends upon the proper construction of the statute," and that "negligence is not a sufficient mental state" for crimes that carry serious penalties. *Elonis*, 135 S. Ct. at 2011-2012.

The Plaintiff argues that the trial court "read the state-of-mind requirement that a defendant act 'intentionally' to apply to all elements of the statute." However, judicial interpretation cannot salvage a statute that is facially deficient. As the Supreme Court explained in *Elonis*, when a statute is written without adequate mens rea requirements, courts cannot simply rewrite the statute through interpretation. The statute must be struck down as facially unconstitutional.

The Plaintiff's reliance on *Virginia v. Black* is misplaced. In *Virginia v. Black*, the Court upheld a Virginia statute that specifically required "an intent to communicate a threat," which satisfied constitutional requirements. The Vermont statute lacks this essential element. The Vermont statute defines harassment as actions that "would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear" certain harms, but does not require that the defendant actually intend to cause such fear or actually threaten violence. This objective-only standard sweeps in protected speech and is therefore facially unconstitutional.

The Plaintiff argues that the statute is content-neutral because it "does not regulate speech based on its subject matter or viewpoint." However, this argument ignores that the statute still regulates speech based on its effect—whether it would cause a reasonable person to fear—rather than requiring subjective intent. A content-neutral regulation of speech must still satisfy intermediate scrutiny and cannot be overly broad. The statute here is not narrowly tailored to serve a significant government interest; it captures substantially more speech than necessary to achieve the government interest in preventing stalking.

The Defense further submits that the statute is overbroad because it criminalizes a wide range of conduct that may be protected under the First Amendment. The statute's definition of "harassing" includes "verbal threats, written, telephonic or other electronically communicated threats, or conduct without consent." This broad language could encompass constitutionally protected speech such as political criticism, personal grievances expressed in writing, or other forms of expression that might cause discomfort but do not constitute true threats. As the Supreme Court explained in *United States v. Stevens*, 559 U.S. 460, 473 (2010), a statute is facially invalid if it "prohibits a substantial amount of protected speech."

---

## II. THE STATUTE IS UNCONSTITUTIONAL AS APPLIED TO DEFENDANT

Even if the statute could be constitutionally applied in some circumstances, it was unconstitutionally applied to the Defendant in this case. The evidence presented at trial demonstrated that the vast majority of the alleged "stalking" conduct consisted of protected speech that does not constitute true threats.

The Plaintiff cites numerous incidents in the record, but many of these incidents involve speech that is protected under the First Amendment. For example:

1. The 2007 emails, including the "berating" email and the wedding night phone call, constitute personal expressions of grievance that do not rise to the level of true threats.

2. The 2011 website comments, while offensive and disturbing, constitute protected political speech. The Defendant criticized the complainant's art for being "pro-military" and expressed political opinions. As the Supreme Court held in *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 452 (2011), "speech on matters of public concern" is "at the heart of the First Amendment's protection."

3. The 2015 self-published book, while perhaps defamatory, constitutes protected speech. The book is an autobiography that discusses the Defendant's relationship with the complainant and expresses his opinions about her actions. The book does not contain a direct threat to commit violence; rather, it expresses the Defendant's subjective feelings and interpretations of events.

The Plaintiff argues that the "course of conduct" including "his actions at complainant's college in January 2008, his following complainant in a remote parking lot in 2010, and his dissemination of the book in 2015" constitute conduct that would cause a reasonable person to fear. However, this argument fails for several reasons.

First, the January 2008 incident at the college, while concerning, did not involve a direct threat of violence. The Defendant appeared at the college, spoke to the complainant's supervisor, and was escorted off campus. This conduct, while inappropriate, does not constitute a "true threat" as defined in *Virginia v. Black*.

Second, the August 2010 parking lot incident, while frightening to the complainant, does not constitute a true threat. The Defendant simply sat in his car and stared at the complainant. This conduct, while possibly constituting civil harassment, does not rise to the level of a criminal threat.

Third, the 2015 book, as discussed above, does not contain a true threat. The Plaintiff points to the statement "Shoot the terrorist?" as a threat, but this must be read in context. The full passage discusses the complainant's art and expresses the Defendant's opinion about her political choices. It does not contain a serious expression of intent to commit violence against the complainant.

The Defense submits that the statute, as applied to the Defendant, criminalizes protected speech and conduct that does not constitute true threats. The conviction must be reversed.

---

## III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT THE CONVICTION

The Plaintiff bears the burden of proving every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The Defendant moved for a judgment of acquittal at the close of the State's evidence, arguing that the State failed to establish the elements of criminal stalking. The trial court denied this motion, and the Defense submits that this was error.

To convict the Defendant of criminal stalking under 13 V.S.A. § 1062, the State was required to prove that the Defendant: (1) intentionally engaged in a course of conduct; (2) the purpose of which was to harass the complainant; (3) that would cause a reasonable person to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death.

The Plaintiff argues that the Defendant's conduct throughout the course of events satisfies these elements. However, the Plaintiff fails to address the statute of limitations issue. The Defendant was charged in June 2015. Under 13 V.S.A. § 4501, the State was required to prove that at least one act constituting the crime of stalking occurred within three years of the charge—i.e., after June 2012.

The trial court held that "if there was at least one event within the overall course of conduct that occurred within the limitations period, the State could bring the charge," and concluded that "defendant's 2015 dissemination of his book met this requirement." However, this holding is constitutionally deficient because it allowed the jury to convict based on acts that occurred outside the statute of limitations.

The 2015 book, as discussed above, does not constitute a true threat. The book is an autobiography that expresses the Defendant's opinions about the complainant. It does not contain a serious expression of intent to commit violence. Therefore, even if the book is considered part of the "course of conduct," it does not satisfy the elements of criminal stalking.

The Defense submits that the evidence was insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the Defendant committed criminal stalking within the statute of limitations period. The conviction must be reversed.

---

## IV. THE JURY INSTRUCTION ALLOWED CONVICTION BASED ON TIME-BARRED ACTS

The trial court's jury instruction allowed the jury to convict based on two or more acts committed by the Defendant, even if some of those acts occurred outside the statute of limitations. The instruction stated:

"It is the entire course of conduct of two or more acts committed by [defendant] which you must consider as meeting all of the requirements and stated elements of the crime, even if a particular event or incident in that course of conduct does not necessarily satisfy all of the required elements."

This instruction is constitutionally deficient because it allows the jury to convict based on acts that are time-barred. The Defendant objected to this instruction both during the charge conference and after the instruction was given. The trial court overruled the objection, and the Defense submits that this was error.

The statute of limitations is a fundamental protection against the prosecution of stale claims. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Lovasco*, 431 U.S. 783, 791 (1977), the statute of limitations "protects defendants from the prejudice of stale charges." The trial court's instruction allowed the jury to convict based on acts that occurred in 2007, 2008, 2010, and 2011—acts that were clearly outside the three-year statute of limitations.

The Plaintiff argues that the "course of conduct" exception applies because the statute allows prosecution for a "course of conduct" rather than individual acts. However, this argument fails because the statute of limitations still applies to the underlying acts. The State cannot circumvent the statute of limitations by characterizing a series of isolated acts as a "course of conduct."

The Defense submits that the jury instruction was erroneous and that the Defendant is entitled to a new trial.

---

## V. THE JURY INSTRUCTION FAILED TO ADEQUATELY DESCRIBE THE PARAMETERS OF TRUE THREATS

The trial court's jury instruction stated that "an expressed or actual threat need not have been made, so long as the act or acts would cause the stated effect on the reasonable person." The Defense submits that this instruction is constitutionally deficient because it fails to adequately describe the parameters of true threats.

The Supreme Court has held that "true threats" are not protected by the First Amendment, but has also emphasized that the definition of "true threat" must be narrowly drawn. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), the Court held that a statement made at a political rally—"if they ever make me carry a rifle the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J."—did not constitute a true threat because it was "conditional" and "political hyperbole."

The trial court's instruction failed to distinguish between true threats and protected speech. By stating that "an expressed or actual threat need not have been made," the instruction effectively eliminated the requirement that the Defendant actually intend to threaten violence. This is constitutionally impermissible under *Elonis*.

The instruction should have required the jury to find that the Defendant: (1) subjectively intended to threaten violence; or (2) made a statement that a reasonable person would understand as a serious expression of intent to commit violence. The instruction as given allowed the jury to convict based solely on the objective effect of the Defendant's conduct, without requiring any finding of subjective intent.

The Defense submits that the jury instruction on true threats was constitutionally deficient and that the Defendant is entitled to a new trial.

2. Alternatively, reverse the conviction and hold that the statute is unconstitutional as applied to the Defendant;

3. Alternatively, reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial based on the erroneous jury instructions;

4. Alternatively, reverse the conviction and dismiss the charges based on insufficient evidence.

The Defense submits that the Plaintiff's opening argument fails to address the fundamental constitutional deficiencies in the criminal stalking statute and the trial court's proceedings. The conviction must be reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court has reviewed the record, the jury instructions, and the opening arguments presented by counsel. Based on the facts provided and the applicable legal standards, the Court renders the following preliminary ruling.

**I. FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY OF THE STATUTE**

The Defendant challenges the facial constitutionality of 13 V.S.A. § 1062 (2015), arguing it is unconstitutional in all its applications because it lacks a mens rea requirement for the threat element, violating the First Amendment under *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015). The State contends the statute is facially valid because it targets only "true threats," a category of speech unprotected by the First Amendment, and the trial court properly read an intent requirement into the statute.

A facial challenge to a legislative Act is, as the State correctly notes, "the most difficult challenge to mount successfully," as the challenger must establish that no set of circumstances exists under which the Act would be valid. *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987). The statute at issue criminalizes intentionally engaging in a course of conduct directed at a specific person that would cause a reasonable person to fear bodily injury or death. The statute does not criminalize mere offensive speech or political hyperbole; rather, it prohibits conduct intended to harass that places a person in reasonable fear of physical harm.

The Defendant's reliance on *Elonis* is misplaced in the context of a facial challenge. *Elonis* addressed the mens rea required for conviction under a specific federal threat statute, holding that negligence is not sufficient. *Elonis*, 135 S. Ct. at 2011-2012. However, the Vermont statute here explicitly requires that the defendant "intentionally engaged in a course of conduct, the purpose of which was to harass." By requiring the specific purpose of harassment—which is defined as causing fear of unlawful violence—the statute incorporates the necessary subjective mental state. Furthermore, the trial court read the "intentionally" requirement to apply to all elements of the statute, thereby curing any potential constitutional deficiency. Because the statute can be constitutionally applied to situations where a defendant intentionally engages in a course of conduct threatening another with physical harm, it is not facially invalid. *Salerno*, 481 U.S. at 745.

**II. AS-APPLIED CONSTITUTIONALITY AND SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The Defendant argues the statute was unconstitutionally applied to him because his conduct—specifically the 2015 book and 2011 website comments—constituted protected speech rather than "true threats." The State counters that the Defendant's course of conduct, viewed in its totality, constituted a true threat.

The First Amendment protects speech on matters of public concern, *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 452 (2011), but it does not protect "true threats." *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). A "true threat" encompasses those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. Whether a statement constitutes a true threat depends on the context in which it is made.

Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, as the Court must on a sufficiency review, the record supports the jury's finding that the Defendant's actions constituted a true threat. The Defendant's 2015 book contained the passage, "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" While the Defendant argues this was political commentary referencing the complainant's art, the context transforms its meaning. This statement followed years of the Defendant's unwanted pursuit, including appearing at the complainant's workplace, following her in his car, and staring at her in a remote parking lot. The complainant testified that the book made her feel "absolutely threatened" that the Defendant was going to shoot her. This history of conduct provides the necessary context for a reasonable jury to conclude that the book's language was not mere political hyperbole, as in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), but a serious expression of intent to cause harm. Therefore, the statute was not unconstitutionally applied, and the evidence was sufficient to support the conviction.

**III. JURY INSTRUCTIONS**

**A. Statute of Limitations**

The Defendant argues the jury instruction erroneously allowed the jury to convict based on acts occurring outside the three-year statute of limitations. The trial court instructed the jury that they must consider the "entire course of conduct," even if a particular event did not satisfy all elements.

The statute of limitations is designed to protect defendants from the prejudice of stale claims. *United States v. Lovasco*, 431 U.S. 783, 791 (1977). However, criminal stalking is defined as a "course of conduct," which inherently comprises a series of acts over time. The trial court correctly determined that if at least one act constituting the course of conduct occurred within the limitations period, the charge is timely. The 2015 dissemination of the book fell within the limitations period. The jury was properly instructed to consider the entire course of conduct to establish the "pattern of conduct" and "continuity of purpose" required by the statute. The instruction did not permit conviction based solely on time-barred acts, but rather allowed the jury to view the timely act (the book) as part of a larger, illegal pattern. This was not error.

**B. True Threats Parameters**

The Defendant contends the jury instruction was constitutionally deficient because it stated that "an expressed or actual threat need not have been made," and allegedly failed to require subjective intent to threaten.

The instruction required the State to prove that the Defendant "intentionally engaged in a course of conduct, the purpose of which was to harass." By defining harassment as actions that would cause a reasonable person to fear bodily injury or death, and requiring the purpose to be such harassment, the instruction effectively mandated that the Defendant acted with the purpose of causing fear of violence. This satisfies the mens rea requirement highlighted in *Elonis*. Furthermore, the statement that "an expressed or actual threat need not have been made" accurately reflects the statutory language, which prohibits "conduct" as well as "threats." Stalking can occur without a verbalized threat; for example, following or staring can constitute harassment if it causes a reasonable fear of injury. The instruction properly informed the jury that they could convict based on conduct that caused fear, not just explicit verbal threats.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the criminal stalking statute is facially constitutional, was constitutionally applied to the Defendant, and that the trial court's jury instructions were proper. The evidence presented was sufficient for a reasonable jury to find the Defendant guilty of criminal stalking beyond a reasonable doubt.

Accordingly, the Defendant's arguments are rejected, and the conviction is **AFFIRMED**.

**UPDATED RULING**

The Court has carefully considered the rebuttals submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. These arguments address the facial constitutionality of the stalking statute, the sufficiency of the evidence regarding "true threats," the application of the statute of limitations, and the adequacy of the jury instructions. Upon review, the Court adheres to its preliminary conclusion that the conviction must be affirmed, though for the reasons detailed below.

**I. FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY OF THE STATUTE**

The Defense renews its argument that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 (2015) is facially unconstitutional because it lacks a specific mens rea requirement regarding the "threat" element, violating *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015). The Defense contends that the statute only requires intent to "harass," which is distinct from intent to "threaten," and that judicial construction cannot cure this omission.

The Court is not persuaded. As the Prosecution correctly notes, a facial challenge requires the Defendant to demonstrate that "no set of circumstances exists under which the Act would be valid." *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987). The Defense has not met this heavy burden. The statute defines "harassing another" as "actions directed at a specific person which would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death." By requiring the State to prove that the defendant acted with the "purpose of harassment," the statute effectively requires the State to prove that the defendant acted with the purpose of causing the victim to fear bodily injury or death. This subjective intent to cause fear satisfies the mental state requirement articulated in *Elonis*. In *Elonis*, the Supreme Court held that negligence regarding the threatening nature of the communication is insufficient; however, the Vermont statute's requirement that the defendant act with the *purpose* of causing fear goes well beyond negligence. *Elonis*, 135 S. Ct. at 2012.

Furthermore, the Defense argues that the trial court's construction of the statute was not "fairly possible" under *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994). The Court disagrees. Reading the "purpose" requirement to apply to the "harassment" element—which is statutorily defined by the victim's fear—is a natural and necessary construction to ensure the statute's constitutionality. It does not rewrite the statute but rather harmonizes its elements. Therefore, the statute is not facially invalid.

**II. AS-APPLIED CONSTITUTIONALITY AND SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The Defense argues that the statute was unconstitutionally applied to the Defendant because his speech constituted protected political commentary rather than "true threats." The Defense relies on *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), and *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 452 (2011), to assert that the book passage "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" is political hyperbole.

While the First Amendment protects robust political debate, it does not protect "true threats." *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). A true threat is defined as "a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual." *Id. The context in which the speech is made is critical to this determination. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708.

Here, the evidence supports the jury's finding that the Defendant's statements were true threats. The book was not a detached political critique but a personalized attack on the complainant, detailing their past relationship, accusing her of specific wrongdoings, and referencing her "breaking point." The statement "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" was made in the context of a years-long campaign of unwanted contact, including following the complainant, appearing at her workplace, and staring at her in isolated parking lots. Viewing this evidence in the light most favorable to the State, a reasonable jury could conclude that the Defendant's language was not mere political hyperbole but a serious expression of intent to cause harm. The statute was therefore constitutionally applied.

**III. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS**

The Defense challenges the jury instruction regarding the statute of limitations, arguing that it permitted the jury to convict based on acts occurring outside the three-year limitations period (specifically acts from 2007 to 2011). The Defense cites *United States v. Lovasco*, 431 U.S. 783, 791 (1977), for the proposition that the statute of limitations protects defendants from stale claims.

The Court finds the trial court's instruction to be proper. Criminal stalking is defined by a "course of conduct," which inherently involves a pattern of behavior over time. The statute of limitations for a continuing course of conduct does not necessarily bar the prosecution of the entire course if at least one act constituting the offense occurred within the limitations period. Here, the Defendant's dissemination of the book in 2015 fell squarely within the limitations period. The jury was instructed to consider the "entire course of conduct" to establish the "pattern of conduct" and "continuity of purpose" required for the offense. This instruction did not allow the jury to convict based solely on time-barred acts; rather, it allowed the jury to view the 2015 act as the culmination of a larger, illegal pattern. This is consistent with the nature of the offense and does not violate the principles of *Lovasco*.

**IV. JURY INSTRUCTION ON TRUE THREATS**

Finally, the Defense argues that the jury instruction was constitutionally deficient because it failed to require subjective intent to threaten, relying solely on a reasonable person standard. The Defense contends this violates *Elonis*.

The Court disagrees. The jury instruction required the State to prove that the Defendant "intentionally engaged in a course of conduct, the purpose of which was to harass." As established in the analysis of the facial challenge, the statutory definition of harassment ties the "purpose" element to causing fear of bodily injury or death. Therefore, the instruction effectively required the jury to find that the Defendant acted with the subjective purpose of causing the complainant to fear for her safety. This satisfies the mens rea requirement of *Elonis*. The instruction's statement that "an expressed or actual threat need not have been made" correctly reflects that stalking can be accomplished through non-verbal conduct, such as following or staring, provided the conduct is intended to harass and causes reasonable fear.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the criminal stalking statute is facially constitutional and was constitutionally applied to the Defendant. The trial court's jury instructions regarding the statute of limitations and the definition of true threats were proper. The evidence presented was sufficient for a reasonable jury to find the Defendant guilty of criminal stalking beyond a reasonable doubt.

Accordingly, the Defendant's arguments are rejected, and the conviction is **AFFIRMED**.

**FINAL RULING**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. These arguments focus on the facial constitutionality of the stalking statute, the sufficiency of the evidence regarding "true threats," the application of the statute of limitations, and the adequacy of the jury instructions. After careful consideration, the Court affirms its preliminary conclusion that the conviction must be upheld. The Defendant's arguments, while well-structured, do not overcome the statutory construction applied by the trial court or the sufficiency of the evidence presented.

**I. FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY OF THE STATUTE**

The Defense argues that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 (2015) is facially unconstitutional because it lacks a specific mens rea requirement regarding the "threat" element, violating *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015). Specifically, the Defense contends that the statute's requirement of "purpose to harass" is distinct from a "purpose to threaten," and that the statute effectively allows for conviction based on negligence regarding the threatening nature of the conduct.

The Court is not persuaded. As the Prosecution correctly notes, a facial challenge requires the Defendant to demonstrate that "no set of circumstances exists under which the Act would be valid." *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987). The Defense has not met this heavy burden. The statute defines "harassing another" as "actions directed at a specific person which would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death." By requiring the State to prove that the defendant acted with the "purpose of harassment," the statute effectively requires the State to prove that the defendant acted with the purpose of causing the victim to fear bodily injury or death. This subjective intent to cause fear satisfies the mental state requirement articulated in *Elonis*. In *Elonis*, the Supreme Court held that negligence regarding the threatening nature of the communication is insufficient; however, the Vermont statute's requirement that the defendant act with the *purpose* of causing fear goes well beyond negligence. *Elonis*, 135 S. Ct. at 2012.

The Defense argues that judicial construction cannot cure this omission, citing *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994). The Court disagrees. Reading the "purpose" requirement to apply to the "harassment" element—which is statutorily defined by the victim's fear—is a natural and necessary construction to ensure the statute's constitutionality. It does not rewrite the statute but rather harmonizes its elements. Therefore, the statute is not facially invalid.

**II. AS-APPLIED CONSTITUTIONALITY AND SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The Defense argues that the statute was unconstitutionally applied to the Defendant because his speech constituted protected political commentary rather than "true threats." The Defense relies on *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), and *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 452 (2011), to assert that the book passage "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" is political hyperbole.

While the First Amendment protects robust political debate, it does not protect "true threats." *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). A true threat is defined as "a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual." *Id.* The context in which the speech is made is critical to this determination. *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708.

Here, the evidence supports the jury's finding that the Defendant's statements were true threats. The book was not a detached political critique but a personalized attack on the complainant, detailing their past relationship, accusing her of specific wrongdoings, and referencing her "breaking point." The statement "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist'?" was made in the context of a years-long campaign of unwanted contact, including following the complainant, appearing at her workplace, and staring at her in isolated parking lots. Viewing this evidence in the light most favorable to the State, a reasonable jury could conclude that the Defendant's language was not mere political hyperbole but a serious expression of intent to cause harm. The statute was therefore constitutionally applied.

**III. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS**

The Defense challenges the jury instruction regarding the statute of limitations, arguing that it permitted the jury to convict based on acts occurring outside the three-year limitations period (specifically acts from 2007 to 2011). The Defense cites *United States v. Lovasco*, 431 U.S. 783, 791 (1977), for the proposition that the statute of limitations protects defendants from stale claims.

The Court finds the trial court's instruction to be proper. Criminal stalking is defined by a "course of conduct," which inherently involves a pattern of behavior over time. The statute of limitations for a continuing course of conduct does not necessarily bar the prosecution of the entire course if at least one act constituting the offense occurred within the limitations period. Here, the Defendant's dissemination of the book in 2015 fell squarely within the limitations period. The jury was instructed to consider the "entire course of conduct" to establish the "pattern of conduct" and "continuity of purpose" required for the offense. This instruction did not allow the jury to convict based solely on time-barred acts; rather, it allowed the jury to view the 2015 act as the culmination of a larger, illegal pattern. This is consistent with the nature of the offense and does not violate the principles of *Lovasco*.

**IV. JURY INSTRUCTION ON TRUE THREATS**

Finally, the Defense argues that the jury instruction was constitutionally deficient because it failed to require subjective intent to threaten, relying solely on a reasonable person standard. The Defense contends this violates *Elonis*.

The Court disagrees. The jury instruction required the State to prove that the Defendant "intentionally engaged in a course of conduct, the purpose of which was to harass." As established in the analysis of the facial challenge, the statutory definition of harassment ties the "purpose" element to causing fear of bodily injury or death. Therefore, the instruction effectively required the jury to find that the Defendant acted with the subjective purpose of causing the complainant to fear for her safety. This satisfies the mens rea requirement of *Elonis*. The instruction's statement that "an expressed or actual threat need not have been made" correctly reflects that stalking can be accomplished through non-verbal conduct, such as following or staring, provided the conduct is intended to harass and causes reasonable fear.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the criminal stalking statute is facially constitutional and was constitutionally applied to the Defendant. The trial court's jury instructions regarding the statute of limitations and the definition of true threats were proper. The evidence presented was sufficient for a reasonable jury to find the Defendant guilty of criminal stalking beyond a reasonable doubt.

Accordingly, the Defendant's arguments are rejected, and the conviction is **AFFIRMED**.
