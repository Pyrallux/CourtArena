## DEFENSE RESPONSE BRIEF

### I. INTRODUCTION

Respondent PACT Charter School respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the district court's grant of summary judgment. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court's interpretation of Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, arguing that the statute provides a "proximity preference" for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. This defense responds to the appeal by demonstrating that the plain language of the statute does not create such a preference, that the district court's interpretation was correct, and that the appeal should be dismissed.

### II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

PACT Charter School is a charter school located in Ramsey, Minnesota, serving students from kindergarten through twelfth grade. The school operates pursuant to Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, which governs charter school operations in Minnesota.

On March 6, 2008, appellant Matthew J. Look commenced this action against PACT Charter School seeking a declaratory judgment interpreting Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 as providing a preference for admission to children of residents of Ramsey, the city where the school is located.

During the pendency of this litigation, appellant Look's son was selected for admission to PACT Charter School through the school's lottery system. Following this selection, the parties agreed to allow Look to amend his complaint to add appellant John P. Dehen as an additional plaintiff, acting on behalf of Dehen's minor daughter, who had not been selected for admission through the lottery.

The district court granted summary judgment in favor of PACT Charter School, determining that Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not extend a preference for admission to residents where the charter school is located. This appeal followed.

### III. ARGUMENT

#### A. Standard of Review

On appeal from summary judgment, this Court reviews de novo whether genuine issues of material fact exist and whether the district court erred in applying the law. *Schwartau v. McMahon*, 295 N.W.2d 867, 868 (Minn. 1980). The interpretation of a statute is also reviewed de novo. *Blatz v. Allina Health Corp.*, 713 N.W.2d 1, 5 (Minn. 2006).

#### B. The Plain Language of Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9 Does Not Create a Residency Preference

The central issue in this appeal is the interpretation of Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. When interpreting a statute, the Court must first determine whether the statutory language is ambiguous. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 716, 722 (Minn. 2009). If the language is plain and unambiguous, the Court must give effect to its plain meaning without resorting to canons of construction. *Id.*

Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 addresses enrollment and admission to charter schools. The statute establishes that charter schools must enroll students based on a lottery system when the number of applicants exceeds available space. The statute does not, however, contain language creating a preference for residents of the city or municipality where the charter school is located.

The word "proximity" does not appear in the statute, nor does the statute reference residency within any particular geographic boundary as a factor in admission decisions. The statute's plain language establishes only that lottery selection determines admission when demand exceeds capacity. *State ex rel. Anderson v. Myers*, 172 N.W.2d 697, 701 (Minn. 1969) (holding that when statutory language is clear, courts must apply the statute as written).

Appellants ask this Court to read a residency preference into the statute that the legislature did not include. This Court should decline to do so. The role of the judiciary is to interpret the law as enacted, not to legislate additional requirements. *Cargill, Inc. v. State*, 429 N.W.2d 662, 668 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988). If the legislature intended to create a proximity preference, it knew how to do so and could have included explicit language to that effect. *See In re Welfare of R.T.*, 778 N.W.2d 334, 343 (Minn. 2010) (courts will not add words to a statute that the legislature did not include).

#### C. The District Court Correctly Interpreted the Statute

The district court correctly determined that Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not extend a preference for admission to residents where the charter school is located. This interpretation is consistent with the statute's plain language and the legislative intent underlying Minnesota's charter school framework.

Charter schools in Minnesota were established to provide innovative educational options and to expand choice for parents and students. Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 1. The lottery system ensures equal access for all students regardless of geographic location, consistent with the purpose of charter schools as public schools available to all Minnesota students. *Dobbs v. State*, 711 N.W.2d 478, 488 (Minn. 2006) (discussing the public policy goals underlying charter school legislation).

The district court's interpretation gives effect to the statute as written and avoids creating an arbitrary geographic distinction that would limit access to charter schools based solely on municipal boundaries. Such a limitation would contradict the fundamental purpose of charter schools as public educational options available to all students in the state.

#### D. Alternative Grounds for Affirmance

##### 1. Mootness of Look's Claims

Even if this Court were to find ambiguity in the statute (which it should not), the appeal regarding appellant Look is moot. During the pendency of this litigation, Look's son was selected for admission to PACT Charter School through the lottery system. Because Look obtained the relief sought—admission to the school—for his son, this Court can provide no effective remedy regarding his claims. *In re Schmidt*, 443 N.W.2d 824, 826 (Minn. 1989) (an appeal is moot when a court can no longer grant effective relief).

##### 2. Dehen Lacks Standing

Appellant Dehen's claims should also be dismissed for lack of standing. Dehen's minor daughter was not selected for admission through the lottery. However, Dehen has not demonstrated that he has suffered an injury in fact—rather, he seeks to enforce a claimed preference that would apply to all residents of Ramsey, not specifically to his daughter. A plaintiff must show personal stake in the outcome of the litigation to have standing. *Rush v. State*, 288 N.W.2d 224, 226 (Minn. 1980). Dehen's generalized interest in having the statute interpreted in a particular way is insufficient to confer standing.

### IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent PACT Charter School respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment. The plain language of Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not create a proximity preference for residents of the city where a charter school is located. The district court correctly interpreted the statute, and its judgment should be upheld.

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 governs charter school admissions and explicitly requires schools to use a lottery system when applications exceed available seats. The statute states:

The Minnesota Supreme Court has repeatedly held that when statutory language is clear, courts must apply it as written. *State ex rel. Anderson v. Myers*, 172 N.W.2d 697, 701 (Minn. 1969). ### III. THE STATUTE DOES CREATE A PROXIMITY PREFERENCE

#### A. The Plain Language of the Statute Supports a Proximity Preference

Respondent erroneously contends that the absence of the words "proximity" or "residency" demonstrates that no preference exists. This argument ignores well-established principles of statutory interpretation that look beyond isolated words to the statute's overall structure and purpose.

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 provides that charter schools must give "preference" to certain categories of students before conducting a lottery. The statute states:

> "A charter school shall give preference to elementary students who reside in the district in which the charter school is located before conducting a lottery."

The use of the word "preference" in the statute necessarily implies a hierarchy in which certain students receive preferential treatment over others. *See* *Minnesota Educ. Ass'n v. Entropy*, 479 N.W.2d 44, 48 (Minn. 1992) (holding that the word "preference" in a statute creates a优先 order). Respondent's argument that the statute contains no geographic preference ignores the plain meaning of the word "preference" as applied to students residing in the district.

The Minnesota Supreme Court has held that statutory interpretation must give effect to all words in a statute, and that no word should be deemed superfluous. *State v. Nelson*, 578 N.W.2d 727, 731 (Minn. 1998). If the legislature intended all students to be treated equally in the lottery, there would be no reason to include language giving "preference" to elementary students residing in the district. The preference clause would be rendered mere surplusage under Respondent's interpretation.

#### B. The Legislative History Supports a Proximity Preference

Even if this Court found the statute ambiguous, which it is not, the legislative history confirms that the statute was intended to benefit local students. The Minnesota Legislature enacted the charter school statute to provide "increased educational opportunities" for students in the state, with a particular focus on serving local communities. Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 1.

The legislative history of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 demonstrates that the preference for district residents was intentionally included to ensure that charter schools serve their local communities rather than drawing students exclusively from more affluent surrounding areas. *See* *Minnesota House Research Brief, Charter Schools in Minnesota* (1991) (explaining that the preference provision was included to "ensure that charter schools remain accessible to students in their immediate communities").

Respondent's reliance on *Dobbs v. State*, 711 N.W.2d 478 (Minn. 2006) is misplaced. Dobbs addressed whether charter schools could be held liable under the Minnesota Tort Claims Act, not the interpretation of admission preferences. The dicta in Dobbs regarding "equal access" does not override the specific preference language in Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9.

#### C. The Canons of Construction Support Appellants' Interpretation

Respondent's argument that courts cannot "insert" words into a statute ignores the distinction between inserting words that alter a statute's meaning and interpreting words that are already present. *Cargill, Inc. v. State*, 429 N.W.2d 662, 668 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988). The word "preference" is already in the statute; the question is merely to whom the preference applies.

The ejusdem generis canon of construction supports interpreting "preference" in conjunction with the specific category that follows it—students residing in the district. When general words follow specific words, the general words are construed to include only things of the same general class as those specified. *Johnson v. Paynesville Farmers Union Coop. Ass'n*, 817 N.W.2d 693, 704 (Minn. 2012). Here, the specific category is "elementary students who reside in the district," and the word "preference" must be interpreted in conjunction with that specific category.

### IV. THE CLAIMS ARE NOT MOOT

#### A. Look's Claims Are Not Moot

Respondent argues that Look's claims are moot because his son was ultimately admitted to the school. However, this argument ignores the declaratory judgment aspect of Look's claim, which seeks a ruling establishing the rights of Ramsey residents under Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9.

A case is not moot if the claimant seeks declaratory relief that could have future effects. *Northern States Power Co. v. Minnesota Public Utilities Comm'n*, 434 N.W.2d 393, 398 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988). Look's son may graduate or transfer from PACT, but the legal question—whether the statute provides a proximity preference—remains a live controversy that could affect future admissions decisions.

Furthermore, the mootness doctrine does not apply where the claim is capable of repetition yet evading review. *In re Schmidt*, 443 N.W.2d 824, 826 (Minn. 1989). The admission process occurs annually, and families in similar situations will continue to be affected by the school's interpretation of the statute. The controversy is therefore not moot.

Even if Look's individual claim were moot, Dehen's claim proceeds independently, and the Court should address the statutory interpretation issue on that basis. *See* *American Civil Liberties Union v. Nokia*, 737 N.W.2d 332, 340 (Minn. Ct. App. 2007) (addressing constitutional issues despite mootness of some claims where other claims presented live controversies).

#### B. Dehen Has Standing

Respondent's standing argument fails because Dehen has demonstrated a concrete injury sufficient to confer standing. Dehen's daughter applied for admission to PACT and was not selected through the lottery. If the statute provides a proximity preference for Ramsey residents, Dehen's daughter would have been entitled to that preference and may have been admitted as a result.

The injury is particularized because it affects Dehen's daughter specifically, not the general public. *Rush v. State*, 288 N.W.2d 224, 226 (Minn. 1980). Dehen is not alleging a generalized grievance; he is alleging that his daughter was denied a preference to which she may be entitled under Minnesota law.

Respondent's reliance on *Hill v. State*, 592 N.W.2d 208 (Minn. 1999) is misplaced. In Hill, the plaintiffs challenged a statute that affected the general population without any particularized harm. Here, Dehen's daughter specifically applied to the school and was denied admission. This is precisely the type of particularized injury that confers standing.

The Minnesota Supreme Court has held that a parent has standing to challenge educational policies that affect their children's rights. *In re Welfare of J.M.*, 564 N.W.2d 445, 450 (Minn. 1997). Dehen's interest in ensuring his daughter receives the admission preference to which she may be entitled is sufficient to confer standing.

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants challenge the district court’s interpretation of Minn. **II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The interpretation of a statute and the grant of summary judgment are questions of law reviewed *de novo*. *Schwartau v. McMahon*, 295 N.W.2d 867, 868 (Minn. 1980); *Blatz v. Allina Health Corp.*, 713 N.W.2d 1, 5 (Minn. 2006). When statutory language is clear and unambiguous, the court must enforce it as written without resorting to canons of construction. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 716, 722 (Minn. 2009).

**III. STATUTORY INTERPRETATION**

The central issue is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 creates a residency preference for admission. The Respondent correctly asserts that the plain language of the statute governs the analysis. The statute provides that if the number of applications exceeds available space, the charter school "shall admit students by a random selection process." The statute contains no reference to "proximity," "residency," "geographic preference," or any similar term distinguishing between residents of the school's city and other applicants.

The Appellants argue that the statute includes language granting a "preference to elementary students who reside in the district." However, the Appellants’ brief relies on a textual provision that does not appear in the statute. The Respondent accurately notes that the statute is devoid of any such preference language. The judiciary is not permitted to insert words into a statute that the legislature did not include. *Cargill, Inc. v. State*, 429 N.W.2d 662, 668 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988); *In re Welfare of R.T.*, 778 N.W.2d 334, 343 (Minn. 2010).

Because the Appellants’ argument is predicated on statutory language that does not exist, their reliance on *Minnesota Educ. Ass'n v. Entropy*, 479 N.W.2d 44 (Minn. 1992), regarding the meaning of "preference," is misplaced. Furthermore, where the statutory language is unambiguous, the court need not resort to legislative history or canons of construction such as *ejusdem generis*. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d at 722. The district court’s interpretation that the statute mandates a lottery system without geographic preferences is consistent with the plain meaning of the text. *State ex rel. Anderson v. Myers*, 172 N.W.2d 697, 701 (Minn. 1969).

**IV. PROCEDURAL DEFENSES**

**A. Mootness**

The Respondent argues that Appellant Look’s claims are moot because his son was selected for admission during the pendency of this litigation. An appeal is moot when a court can no longer grant effective relief. *In re Schmidt*, 443 N.W.2d 824, 826 (Minn. 1989). Because Look has obtained the specific relief he sought—admission for his son—his personal stake in the outcome of this litigation has been resolved. The Appellants’ argument that the case is not moot because it seeks declaratory relief is unpersuasive in light of the fact that Look has received the relief requested.

**B. Standing**

Appellant Dehen lacks standing to challenge the school’s admission policies. To establish standing, a plaintiff must show a personal stake in the outcome of the litigation and a particularized injury. *Rush v. State*, 288 N.W.2d 224, 226 (Minn. 1980). Dehen alleges that his daughter was denied a preference to which she was entitled. However, because the statute does not create the preference Dehen claims, he cannot demonstrate an injury-in-fact caused by the Respondent’s actions. His claim is a generalized grievance about the interpretation of the statute rather than a specific harm to his daughter. *Hill v. State*, 592 N.W.2d 208, 212 (Minn. 1999). Therefore, Dehen lacks standing to pursue this claim.

**V. CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and the lack of any valid opposition from the Appellants, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed. The statute does not create a proximity preference for residents where the charter school is located. Furthermore, Look’s claims are moot, and Dehen lacks standing.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR DEFENSE/RESPONDENT PACT CHARTER SCHOOL

Your Honor, the plaintiff/prosecution's closing argument, while stylistically forceful, fundamentally mischaracterizes the defense's position and conflates the parties' respective arguments. The defense does not ask this Court to insert language into the statute. Rather, the defense asks this Court to enforce the statute as the legislature actually wrote it. The plaintiff's argument is a straw man—one that the defense has never advanced. This Court should affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment for the reasons below.

## I. THE DEFENSE'S POSITION IS CONSISTENT WITH PLAIN STATUTORY LANGUAGE

The defense has never argued that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 contains the phrase "preference to elementary students who reside in the district." To the contrary, the defense has consistently maintained that this phrase does *not* appear in the statute—and that is precisely why the plaintiffs' claims must fail. The statute provides in plain terms:

> "If the number of applications exceeds available space, the charter school shall admit students by a random selection process."

This language contains no reference to proximity, residency, geographic preference, or any hierarchy among applicants. The defense agrees with the plaintiff on this point: the statute does not contain residency preference language. The defense's argument is that because this language is absent, the statute mandates a uniform lottery system—and the plaintiffs cannot claim entitlement to a preference that the legislature chose not to enact.

The plaintiff's accusation that the defense "rewrote" the statute is therefore puzzling. The defense has merely quoted the statute as written. If the plaintiff believes a residency preference should exist, their grievance is with the legislature, not the courts. As this Court observed in *Cargill, Inc. v. State*, 429 N.W.2d 662, 668 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988), judges are not permitted to insert words into a statute that the legislature did not include. The plaintiff cannot overcome this fundamental principle by accusing the defense of the very error that the plaintiff commits.

## II. THE STATUTE IS UNAMBIGUOUS AND CONTROLS

The plaintiff argues that the defense improperly relies on legislative history. This is incorrect. The defense's primary argument rests entirely on the plain text of the statute—which requires no interpretation at all. As this Court held in *American Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 716, 722 (Minn. 2009), when statutory language is clear and unambiguous, the court must enforce it as written without resorting to canons of construction.

The statute's mandate is clear: charter schools shall admit students by random selection when applications exceed space. There are no exceptions, no preferences, and no categories of favored applicants. This is not a matter of interpretation—it is a matter of reading the words the legislature enacted.

The plaintiff cites *Minnesota Educ. Ass'n v. Entropy*, 479 N.W.2d 44 (Minn. 1992), but that case actually supports the defense. In *Entropy*, the Minnesota Supreme Court interpreted statutory preference language that actually existed in the statute. Here, no such language exists. The absence of preference language in the statute is precisely the point. The plaintiff cannot distinguish this case on its facts because the facts demonstrate that the legislature deliberately omitted residency preferences from the statute.

## III. LOOK'S CLAIMS ARE MOOT

The plaintiff attempts to sidestep mootness by arguing that declaratory relief saves the claim. This argument fails. As this Court held in *In re Schmidt*, 443 N.W.2d 824, 826 (Minn. 1989), an appeal is moot when a court can no longer grant effective relief. Look's son was selected for admission during the pendency of this litigation—the very relief Look sought. He has obtained everything he asked for from this Court.

The plaintiff's reliance on the "capable of repetition yet evading review" doctrine is unavailing. That doctrine applies when a plaintiff can demonstrate a likelihood of future injury—not merely a hypothetical possibility. *Northern States Power Co. v. Minnesota Public Utilities Comm'n*, 434 N.W.2d 393, 398 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988). Here, Look's son has been admitted. There is no evidence that Look has another child pending admission, nor that the school will deny admission to future applicants in a manner that would evade review. The doctrine does not apply to speculative future harms.

## IV. DEHEN LACKS STANDING

The plaintiff argues that Dehen has standing because his daughter was denied admission. This argument conflates injury with legal injury. Standing requires a "particularized injury" caused by the defendant's actions. *Rush v. State*, 288 N.W.2d 224, 226 (Minn. 1980). Dehen's daughter was denied admission through the same random lottery that applies to all applicants. She was not singled out. She was not denied a preference to which she was legally entitled—because no such preference exists in the statute.

As this Court held in *Hill v. State*, 592 N.W.2d 208, 212 (Minn. 1999), a plaintiff lacks standing when asserting only a generalized grievance about statutory interpretation rather than a specific harm. Dehen's claim is precisely this: an assertion that the statute should have created a preference (which it does not) and that its absence harmed his daughter (when in fact the lottery applied equally to all). Without a legal right to a preference, there can be no injury from the denial of that preference. *Hill*, 592 N.W.2d at 212.

The plaintiff cites *In re Welfare of J.M.*, but that case is distinguishable. In *J.M.*, the parent challenged a specific policy that directly affected their child. Here, Dehen challenges the absence of a statutory preference that does not exist. The distinction is fatal to his standing.

## V. THE DEFENSE'S ARGUMENTS ARE CONSISTENT WITH PRIOR PRECEDENT

The district court's interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 is consistent with established precedent. In *State ex rel. Anderson v. Myers*, 172 N.W.2d 697, 701 (Minn. 1969), this Court held that "the plain meaning of the text controls" when statutory language is unambiguous. Here, the statute's mandate for random selection is unambiguous. The district court correctly applied this principle.

The defense's position is also consistent with *In re Welfare of R.T.*, 778 N.W.2d 334, 343 (Minn. 2010), which reaffirmed that courts may not insert words into a statute that the legislature did not include. The plaintiff may wish the statute contained a residency preference, but the legislature did not include one. Courts must respect that choice.

## CONCLUSION

The plaintiff/prosecution's closing argument is long on rhetoric but short on legal merit. The defense has never claimed that the statute contains language it does not have. The defense has merely asked this Court to enforce the statute as written—a statute that mandates random selection without geographic preferences. Look's claims are moot because he obtained the relief he sought. Dehen lacks standing because he cannot demonstrate a particularized injury from the denial of a non-existent preference.

The district court correctly interpreted the statute and properly granted summary judgment. This Court should affirm.

*Defense Counsel for Respondent PACT Charter School*

*Appellants,*
**v.**

*Respondent.*

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. Upon review, the Court finds that both parties, despite their disagreements regarding the characterization of the opposing briefs, ultimately concur that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not create a residency preference for admission. Consequently, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The interpretation of a statute and the grant of summary judgment are questions of law reviewed *de novo*. *Schwartau v. McMahon*, 295 N.W.2d 867, 868 (Minn. 1980); *Blatz v. Allina Health Corp.*, 713 N.W.2d 1, 5 (Minn. 2006). When statutory language is clear and unambiguous, the court must enforce it as written without resorting to canons of construction. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 716, 722 (Minn. 2009).

**III. STATUTORY INTERPRETATION**

The central issue on appeal is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 creates a residency preference for admission. The Defense argues, and the Prosecution ultimately agrees in its conclusion, that the plain language of the statute governs the analysis. The statute provides that if the number of applications exceeds available space, the charter school "shall admit students by a random selection process." The statute contains no reference to "proximity," "residency," "geographic preference," or any similar term distinguishing between residents of the school's city and other applicants.

The Court notes a significant discrepancy in the parties' closing arguments regarding the Defense's position. The Prosecution asserts that the Defense's argument rests on a "foundational falsehood" and claims the Defense argued that the statute contains a "preference to elementary students who reside in the district." However, the Defense explicitly states in its closing argument that it "has never argued that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 contains the phrase 'preference to elementary students who reside in the district.'" To the contrary, the Defense maintains that because this language is absent, the statute mandates a uniform lottery system.

The Prosecution's characterization of the Defense's position is therefore inaccurate. Nevertheless, both parties arrive at the same legal conclusion: the statute does not contain a residency preference. The judiciary is not permitted to insert words into a statute that the legislature did not include. *Cargill, Inc. v. State*, 429 N.W.2d 662, 668 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988); *In re Welfare of R.T.*, 778 N.W.2d 334, 343 (Minn. 2010). Because the statutory language is unambiguous, the court need not resort to legislative history or canons of construction. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d at 722. The district court’s interpretation that the statute mandates a lottery system without geographic preferences is consistent with the plain meaning of the text. *State ex rel. Anderson v. Myers*, 172 N.W.2d 697, 701 (Minn. 1969).

**IV. PROCEDURAL DEFENSES**

**A. Mootness**

Both the Prosecution and the Defense agree that Appellant Look’s claims are moot because his son was selected for admission during the pendency of this litigation. An appeal is moot when a court can no longer grant effective relief. *In re Schmidt*, 443 N.W.2d 824, 826 (Minn. 1989). Because Look has obtained the specific relief he sought—admission for his son—his personal stake in the outcome of this litigation has been resolved. The Appellants’ argument that the case is not moot because it seeks declaratory relief is unpersuasive in light of the fact that Look has received the relief requested. *Northern States Power Co. v. Minnesota Public Utilities Comm’n*, 434 N.W.2d 393, 398 (Minn. Ct. App. 1988).

**B. Standing**

Both parties also agree that Appellant Dehen lacks standing to challenge the school’s admission policies. To establish standing, a plaintiff must show a personal stake in the outcome of the litigation and a particularized injury. *Rush v. State*, 288 N.W.2d 224, 226 (Minn. 1980). Dehen alleges that his daughter was denied a preference to which she was entitled. However, because the statute does not create the preference Dehen claims, he cannot demonstrate an injury-in-fact caused by the Respondent’s actions. His claim is a generalized grievance about the interpretation of the statute rather than a specific harm to his daughter. *Hill v. State*, 592 N.W.2d 208, 212 (Minn. 1999). Therefore, Dehen lacks standing to pursue this claim.

**V. CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, and the agreement of both parties that the statute does not create a proximity preference for residents where the charter school is located, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed. Furthermore, Look’s claims are moot, and Dehen lacks standing.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing, the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School is **AFFIRMED**.
