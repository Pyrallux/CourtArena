**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, this Court is presented with a clear legal error: the Director misapplied *Flor v. Holguin* to a claim involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition. *Flor* does not govern this case because it addresses only whether a *new disease* "results from" the nature of employment. Here, Van Ness’s asthma was pre-existing and merely aggravated by workplace exposure to vog. For such claims, Hawai'i law mandates the application of the unitary test—not *Flor*’s proximate cause standard.  

As this Court held in *Toney v. Hawaii*, 99 Hawai'i 228, 54 P.3d 396 (2002), "[f]or injuries involving aggravation of a pre-existing condition, the unitary test applies: the employment must be a substantial contributing factor to the injury." *Toney* explicitly rejected the "proximate cause" standard for aggravation claims, recognizing that employment need not be the sole cause—only a substantial factor. This is precisely the standard that should control here.  

*Flor* is inapposite. In *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Hawai'i 426, 60 P.3d 328 (2002), the claim involved a new occupational disease—carpal tunnel syndrome—that developed solely due to employment duties. The Court there held that for diseases "resulting from" the nature of employment, proximate cause must be shown. But *Flor* never addressed aggravation of pre-existing conditions, and it does not override *Toney*’s unitary test for such cases. As *Toney* makes clear, the unitary test applies universally to aggravation claims, regardless of whether the underlying condition is a disease or injury.  

The Director’s error is fatal. The Director incorrectly applied *Flor*’s proximate cause standard, ruling that Van Ness’s exposure to vog must be "overwhelming" at work alone to establish compensability. But under *Toney*, the unitary test requires only that employment exposure was a substantial contributing factor—not the exclusive cause. The record overwhelmingly supports this: Van Ness’s treating physician, Dr. Mathison, confirmed that volcanic smoke is a "well-recognized risk factor" for asthmatic exacerbations and that his symptoms "worsened specifically during work exposure on Maui." Dr. Mathison further noted that Van Ness’s condition "improved coincident to his residence in Oahu," and that his employer must avoid assignments with volcanic exposure to prevent "serious and potentially fatal" harm. These findings align with *Toney*’s standard: employment exposure was a substantial factor in aggravating his condition.  

The Director’s focus on vog being present island-wide is legally irrelevant. Under the unitary test, the mere existence of vog elsewhere does not negate that Van Ness’s *employment* required him to be exposed to it daily in a high-risk environment. His job necessitated working in non-air-conditioned buildings with open windows, where vog and dust accumulated—conditions that directly exacerbated his asthma. As Dr. Tom documented, "vog contributing" to his symptoms, and his physician explicitly recommended transfer due to "higher vog exposure" at work.  

*Flor* does not apply to this aggravation claim. The unitary test from *Toney* governs, and the Director erred in misapplying *Flor*’s stricter standard. This Court must reverse and remand for application of the correct legal framework.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Statement of the Case

The Defense respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the Plaintiff's appeal. The Director's April 21, 2008 Decision denying Workers' Compensation benefits should be affirmed. Contrary to the Plaintiff's characterization, the Director correctly analyzed this claim under applicable Hawaii workers' compensation principles. Even applying the unitary test that the Plaintiff advocates, the record does not support a finding that Van Ness's employment was a substantial contributing factor to his injury. The Plaintiff's claims rest on a fundamental mischaracterization of the evidentiary record and misapplication of governing legal standards.

## I. The Director Correctly Applied Governing Legal Standards

The Plaintiff contends that the Director erred by applying *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Hawai'i 426, 60 P.3d 328 (2002), rather than the unitary test from *Toney v. Hawaii*, 99 Hawai'i 228, 54 P.3d 396 (2002). This argument, while creatively constructed, fails to recognize the actual basis for the Director's decision and misapprehends the relationship between these precedents.

The Director's decision explicitly found that "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment" and that "it was claimant's exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition." This analysis is consistent with fundamental workers' compensation principles that require a nexus between the employment and the injury. The Director did not expressly adopt the *Flor* proximate cause standard so much as apply the basic requirement that a work-related injury must arise out of and occur in the course of employment.

More fundamentally, even accepting arguendo that the unitary test applies to aggravation claims, the Plaintiff cannot satisfy this standard on the facts presented. The unitary test requires that employment be a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. *Toney v. Hawaii*, 99 Hawai'i at 237, 54 P.3d at 405. This is not a trivial threshold. The Plaintiff bears the burden of establishing this causal connection by a preponderance of the evidence.

## II. The Evidence Does Not Support a Finding That Employment Was a Substantial Contributing Factor

### A. The Vog Exposure Was Not Peculiar to the Workplace

The central factual problem with the Plaintiff's claim is that the evidence overwhelmingly establishes that vog exposure was a general environmental condition affecting all of Maui, not a condition peculiar to Lahainaluna High School. The Director specifically found this fact, and the record supports it.

Van Ness testified that there was "a significant amount of vog on Maui from late October 2005 through April 2006." He stated that on severe vog days, he was unable to see the administrative building "halfway down the campus" from the library. Vice Principal Joanne Dennis confirmed that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, than at the school. She noted that "on some days the atmosphere was so heavy with vog that we could barely see Lahaina town from Lahainaluna High School."

Critically, Van Ness himself testified that he did not have problems with volcanic ash inside his car or his home. He stated that while living on Maui he spent "little time outside, other than when he was at work." This admission is devastating to his claim. If vog was causing him respiratory distress, and he was exposed to vog both at home and at work, the mere fact that he worked at a particular location does not establish that employment was a substantial contributing factor. His own testimony establishes that he chose to remain indoors when not at work, yet still experienced symptoms.

### B. The Workplace Conditions Did Not Materially Increase Exposure Risk

The Plaintiff argues that Van Ness's job required him to work in non-air-conditioned environments where vog and dust accumulated. However, the record does not establish that these conditions materially increased his vog exposure beyond what he would have experienced elsewhere on Maui.

Van Ness testified that he lived in an air-conditioned home in Kihei with HEPA filters. He had "Bionic Breeze" filter units placed around his home, also HEPA filters, which had been recommended by his doctor. He had a HEPA filter in his car. This evidence demonstrates that Van Ness took extensive measures to filter his home environment, yet he claims his minimal exposure at work—where he estimated spending less than five percent of his time in his air-conditioned office—was the cause of his aggravation.

The Plaintiff cites Dr. Tom's notes referencing "vog contributing" and "vog on Maui." However, Dr. Tom's notes do not establish that the vog exposure at Lahainaluna was materially different from or greater than the exposure Van Ness experienced island-wide. Dr. Tom's March 10, 2006 note states only that Van Ness had "an asthma condition which was exacerbated by vog" and that his symptoms "have worsened since moving to Maui due to the higher vog exposure there." The note attributes the worsening to Maui's vog exposure generally, not to exposure specifically at Lahainaluna.

### C. The Medical Evidence Does Not Support Causation

The Plaintiff relies heavily on Dr. Mathison's consultation report. However, a careful examination of Dr. Mathison's findings actually undermines rather than supports the Plaintiff's claim.

Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness experienced a "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms" during a "40-day spell of rainy weather with vog (moisture, pollutants including volcanic smoke/ash) exposure from about November 2005 and continuing into the spring of 2006." This describes a general environmental event affecting Maui during this period—not a condition unique to the workplace.

Dr. Mathison also noted that Van Ness's symptoms "worsened specifically during work exposure on Maui." However, this statement is conclusory and unsupported by any analysis distinguishing work exposure from general island exposure. Van Ness lived on Maui; his work was on Maui; the vog was on Maui. Dr. Mathison provides no basis for attributing the worsening to work exposure specifically rather than to general residence on Maui.

The Court should note that Dr. Mathison's opinion that Van Ness "can be considered to be disabled" and that his employer "should not place him at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution" addresses the question of reasonable accommodation, not the question of causation for workers' compensation purposes. An employer may have an obligation to reasonably accommodate an employee's disability without that accommodation creating workers' compensation liability.

### D. The Timeline Undermines Causation

The temporal sequence of events also weighs against finding that employment was a substantial contributing factor. Van Ness began experiencing symptoms in late 2005, when the severe vog period began. However, he continued working at Lahainaluna until November 2006, and his respiratory difficulties persisted even after his transfer to Oahu in November 2006. Dr. Tom restricted him from "walking too far" as late as June 16, 2007, because "that just kept [Van Ness] in a situation of overworking [his] lungs in the recovery period."

This extended recovery period is consistent with a condition caused by general environmental exposure and surgery complications rather than workplace exposure. If work exposure were the cause, one would expect improvement to begin shortly after the transfer, but the record shows persistent limitations.

## III. The Plaintiff's Characterization of the Director's Ruling Is Inaccurate

The Plaintiff argues that the Director required vog exposure to be "overwhelming" at work alone. This mischaracterizes the Director's decision. The Director found that "there was insufficient evidence" to support the claim "that his exposure to vog at only Lahainaluna School was so overwhelming." This is a factual finding about the sufficiency of evidence, not a legal standard. The Director's point was simply that the evidence did not establish that the Plaintiff's workplace exposure was materially different from or greater than general island exposure.

The Plaintiff cannot transform the Director's fact-finding into a legal error. The question under the unitary test is whether employment was a substantial contributing factor. The Director found that the evidence did not establish that Van Ness's employment exposure was meaningfully distinguishable from his general environmental exposure. This factual determination is supported by the record and should not be overturned.

## IV. Even Under the Unitary Test, the Plaintiff Fails

The Plaintiff urges this Court to apply the unitary test from *Toney*. We do not dispute that this test applies to aggravation claims. However, the Plaintiff cannot satisfy this standard on the facts presented.

In *Toney*, the Hawaii Supreme Court held that the unitary test requires proof that "the employment was a substantial contributing factor to the injury." 99 Hawai'i at 237, 54 P.3d at 405. This requires more than mere exposure to a general condition that exists both at work and elsewhere. The employment must contribute in a meaningful way to the injury.

Here, the evidence shows that vog was a general environmental condition on Maui. Van Ness was exposed to vog both at work and at home. He himself testified that he spent little time outdoors other than at work, suggesting that his home exposure was limited. Yet he experienced symptoms throughout the period, including at home. The evidence does not establish that his workplace exposure was a meaningful contributing factor beyond the general exposure all Maui residents experienced.

The Plaintiff cites Dr. Mathison's statement that volcanic smoke is a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients." This is true but irrelevant. The question is not whether vog can cause asthma exacerbations—it manifestly can—but whether Van Ness's employment exposure was a substantial contributing factor to his particular exacerbation. The evidence does not establish this causal link.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the Director's April 21, 2008 Decision denying Workers' Compensation benefits. The Plaintiff has failed to establish that his employment was a substantial contributing factor to his respiratory condition. The vog exposure was a general environmental condition affecting all of Maui, not a condition peculiar to his workplace. The Director's factual findings are supported by the record, and the legal conclusion denying benefits was correct.

Thank you, Your Honor.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Claimant contends that the Director committed legal error by applying the incorrect standard for determining compensability, specifically by utilizing the proximate cause standard applicable to new occupational diseases rather than the unitary test applicable to the aggravation of pre-existing conditions. The Defense argues the Director’s decision was correct and, alternatively, that the Claimant cannot meet the burden of proof even under the unitary test. Based upon the record and the arguments presented, the Court finds that the Director misapplied the governing legal standard. Accordingly, the Director’s decision is vacated, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**II. APPLICABLE LEGAL STANDARDS**

The determination of whether an injury is compensable under Hawai‘i workers’ compensation law depends upon the nature of the injury claimed. The Hawai‘i Supreme Court has distinguished between claims for "new" occupational diseases and claims involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Hawai‘i 426, 60 P.3d 328 (2002), the Court addressed a claim for carpal tunnel syndrome, a "new" disease resulting from the nature of employment. The Court held that for such diseases, the claimant must demonstrate that the employment was the proximate cause of the disease. *Flor*, 100 Hawai‘i at 432, 60 P.3d at 334.

Conversely, in *Toney v. Hawaii*, 99 Hawai‘i 228, 54 P.3d 396 (2002), the Court established the standard for claims involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition. The Court explicitly rejected the application of a proximate cause standard for these claims, holding instead that the "unitary test" applies. *Toney*, 99 Hawai‘i at 237, 54 P.3d at 405. Under the unitary test, a claimant must prove that the employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. *Toney*, 99 Hawai‘i at 237, 54 P.3d at 405. This standard does not require the employment to be the sole cause of the injury, nor does it require the risk to be unique to the workplace; it only requires that the employment contribute substantially to the aggravation.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director Misapplied the Legal Standard**

The record establishes that Van Ness had a long-standing history of "mild persistent asthma" prior to his employment at Lahainaluna High School. His claim is predicated on the assertion that his exposure to vog and dust at work "aggravated" this pre-existing condition. Therefore, this case falls squarely within the ambit of *Toney* and the unitary test.

The Director’s decision, however, reveals a misapplication of this standard. The Director found that "[Van Ness’s] exposure to vog aggravated his long standing asthma condition" but denied the claim because "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment." The Director further concluded that there was "insufficient evidence" to show that exposure at Lahainaluna was "so overwhelming" that it occurred only at the place of employment.

By focusing on the island-wide presence of vog and requiring the exposure to be "overwhelming" at work, the Director effectively applied a standard closer to the "proximate cause" or "unique risk" analysis associated with *Flor* or general "arising out of" employment jurisprudence, rather than the "substantial contributing factor" test mandated by *Toney*. The Defense’s assertion that the Director merely applied basic nexus requirements is unpersuasive; the Director’s specific language regarding the need for the exposure to be "overwhelming" at work indicates a legal error in the burden placed on the Claimant. Under *Toney*, the relevant inquiry is not whether the vog was present only at work, but whether the conditions of employment—such as the non-air conditioned environment, the accumulation of dust, and the physical exertion required—were a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of Van Ness’s asthma compared to his environmental exposure elsewhere.

**B. The Record Supports Remand Under the Correct Standard**

The Defense argues that, even under the unitary test, the Claimant fails to establish that employment was a substantial contributing factor because vog was a general environmental condition and Van Ness’s home was located in an area with potentially worse vog. While the Defense raises factual disputes regarding the comparative exposure at home versus work, these disputes must be weighed under the correct legal standard.

The record contains evidence from which a reasonable fact-finder could conclude that employment was a substantial contributing factor. Van Ness testified that his home was air-conditioned and equipped with HEPA filters, whereas his work environment was non-air conditioned, dusty, and required physical exertion that exacerbated his symptoms. Furthermore, Dr. Mathison’s consultation report noted that Van Ness’s symptoms "worsened specifically during work exposure on Maui" and that volcanic products are a "well-recognized risk factor" for asthmatic patients.

Because the Director did not evaluate this evidence under the "substantial contributing factor" standard, the Court cannot determine whether the Director’s factual findings would support a denial under the correct legal framework. The Defense’s argument that the evidence is insufficient is better addressed by the Director on remand, applying the proper *Toney* standard to the factual record.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the Director committed legal error by applying a standard inconsistent with the unitary test for aggravation of pre-existing conditions as defined in *Toney v. Hawaii*, 99 Hawai‘i 228, 54 P.3d 396 (2002). The Director’s decision is therefore reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon consideration of the rebuttals filed by the Prosecution and the Defense following the Court’s preliminary ruling. The Prosecution urges the Court to affirm the vacatur of the Director’s decision, arguing that the Director misapplied the law and that the record supports compensation under the correct standard. The Defense, while conceding the applicability of the unitary test for the sake of argument, contends that the evidence is legally insufficient to establish that employment was a substantial contributing factor to Van Ness’s injury, and thus the Director’s denial should be upheld regardless of the legal error.

After careful review of the arguments and the record, the Court adheres to its preliminary finding that the Director committed legal error. Furthermore, the Court finds that the Defense’s assertion of legal insufficiency is unavailing; the record contains sufficient evidence to create a genuine dispute of material fact regarding whether employment was a substantial contributing factor to Van Ness’s injury. Accordingly, the Director’s decision is vacated, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**II. THE DIRECTOR MISAPPLIED THE LEGAL STANDARD**

The Court reiterates its finding that the Director misapplied the governing legal standard. As established in the preliminary ruling, claims involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition are governed by the "unitary test" set forth in *Toney v. Hawaii*, 99 Hawai'i 228, 54 P.3d 396 (2002). Under *Toney*, a claimant need not prove that the employment was the proximate cause of the injury or that the risk was unique to the workplace; rather, the claimant must only show that the employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the aggravation. *Toney*, 99 Hawai'i at 237, 54 P.3d at 405.

The Director’s decision, which denied the claim because "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment," improperly imported the "unique risk" or "proximate cause" analysis applicable to new occupational diseases under *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Hawai'i 426, 60 P.3d 328 (2002). The Defense’s rebuttal effectively concedes that *Toney* is the correct standard but argues the Director’s error was harmless. The Court disagrees. The Director’s explicit reliance on the island-wide prevalence of vog demonstrates that the Director did not weigh the evidence under the "substantial contributing factor" standard. Because the legal standard applied tainted the factual findings, the decision cannot stand.

**III. THE EVIDENCE IS NOT LEGALLY INSUFFICIENT UNDER THE UNITARY TEST**

The Defense argues that, even under the unitary test, the Claimant cannot meet his burden of proof because the evidence shows that vog was a general environmental condition and that Van Ness’s exposure at home was equal to or greater than his exposure at work. The Defense further contends that Dr. Mathison’s report is conclusory and fails to isolate workplace exposure. Upon review, the Court finds that the record contains sufficient evidence from which a reasonable fact-finder could conclude that employment was a substantial contributing factor to Van Ness’s injury.

**A. Disputed Factual Issues Regarding Comparative Exposure**

The Defense relies heavily on Vice Principal Dennis’s statement that vog was "even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, to argue that home exposure was greater. However, this statement does not resolve the issue of "substantial contribution" as a matter of law. Van Ness testified that his home was air-conditioned and equipped with HEPA filters recommended by his doctor, whereas his work environment was non-air conditioned, dusty, and required him to clean computer filters clogged with dust.

This creates a classic dispute of fact regarding the *nature* and *intensity* of the exposure in each environment. While the ambient vog may have been denser in Kihei, the conditions at Lahainaluna—specifically the lack of air conditioning, the accumulation of dust, and the physical exertion required to traverse the campus—could have intensified the effect of that exposure. It is the role of the fact-finder, not this Court on appeal, to weigh the Vice Principal’s observation against Van Ness’s testimony regarding his home filtration system and the specific conditions at the school. *See Toney*, 99 Hawai'i at 237, 54 P.3d at 405 (holding that the unitary test focuses on whether employment contributed substantially to the injury, not whether it was the sole cause).

**B. Medical Evidence Supporting Causation**

The Defense attacks Dr. Mathison’s report as failing to distinguish between work and home exposure. However, Dr. Mathison’s report contains specific language linking the aggravation to work. Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness had "significant asthma exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke (high in sulfur dioxide) and ash" and that his condition "improved coincident to his residence in Oahu." Crucially, Dr. Mathison observed that Van Ness’s symptoms "worsened specifically during work exposure on Maui."

While the Defense correctly points out that Dr. Mathison did not provide a detailed quantitative analysis of exposure levels, his opinion that the symptoms worsened during work exposure constitutes competent medical evidence supporting the claimant’s burden. The Director credited Dr. Arora, who explicitly stated he could not address the referral questions without medical records, over Dr. Mathison, who reviewed the complete medical history. On remand, the Director must weigh this conflicting medical evidence under the correct legal standard, rather than dismissing Dr. Mathison’s opinion because the hazard was not unique to the workplace.

**C. Physical Exertion and Employment Conditions**

The Defense argues that physical exertion, such as walking uphill, is not a condition unique to employment. This argument misapprehends the unitary test. *Toney* does not require the condition to be unique; it requires that the employment conditions substantially contribute to the injury. Van Ness testified that the physical exertion required by his job—walking up and down a steep campus to service computers—caused him to sweat, increased his heart rate, and directly exacerbated his shortness of breath. This exertion, performed in a dusty, non-air conditioned environment during a vog event, is qualitatively different from the exertion involved in daily life in a controlled, air-conditioned home. A fact-finder could reasonably conclude that the combination of exertion and environmental exposure at work was a substantial contributing factor to his respiratory decline.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Defense has not demonstrated that the evidence is legally insufficient to support a finding for Van Ness under the unitary test. Genuine disputes of material fact exist regarding the comparative exposure at home versus work, the impact of the specific workplace conditions (dust, lack of air conditioning, exertion), and the weight of the conflicting medical opinions. Because the Director applied the wrong legal standard in resolving these disputes, the Court cannot uphold the denial of benefits.

The Director’s decision is vacated. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling, wherein the Director must apply the "substantial contributing factor" standard of *Toney v. Hawaii*, 99 Hawai'i 228, 54 P.3d 396 (2002), to the facts of this case.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the Prosecution (Claimant) and the Defense (Employer) following the Court’s preliminary ruling. The Prosecution urges the Court to affirm the vacatur of the Director’s decision, arguing that the Director misapplied the law and that the record contains substantial evidence to support a finding of compensability under the correct standard. The Defense, while conceding the applicability of the unitary test for the sake of argument, contends that the evidence is legally insufficient to establish that employment was a substantial contributing factor to Van Ness’s injury.

After a thorough review of the record, the arguments presented, and the applicable law, the Court adheres to its preliminary finding. The Director committed legal error by applying the incorrect standard for determining compensability. Furthermore, the Defense’s assertion that the evidence is legally insufficient is rejected; the record contains substantial evidence from which a reasonable fact-finder could conclude that Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his pre-existing asthma. Accordingly, the Director’s decision is vacated, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**II. THE DIRECTOR MISAPPLIED THE GOVERNING LEGAL STANDARD**

The Court reiterates its finding that the Director misapplied the law. As established in the preliminary ruling, claims involving the aggravation of a pre-existing condition are governed by the "unitary test" set forth in *Toney v. Hawaii*, 99 Hawai'i 228, 54 P.3d 396 (2002). Under *Toney*, a claimant need not prove that the employment was the proximate cause of the injury or that the risk was unique to the workplace; rather, the claimant must only show that the employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the aggravation. *Toney*, 99 Hawai'i at 237, 54 P.3d at 405.

The Director’s decision, which denied the claim because "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment," improperly imported the "unique risk" or "proximate cause" analysis applicable to new occupational diseases under *Flor v. Holguin*, 100 Hawai'i 426, 60 P.3d 328 (2002). The Defense’s closing statement effectively concedes that *Toney* is the correct standard but argues the Director’s error was harmless. The Court disagrees. The Director’s explicit reliance on the island-wide prevalence of vog demonstrates that the Director did not weigh the evidence under the "substantial contributing factor" standard. Because the legal standard applied tainted the factual findings, the decision cannot stand.

**III. THE EVIDENCE IS LEGALLY SUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT A FINDING OF COMPENSABILITY**

The Defense argues that, even under the unitary test, the Claimant cannot meet his burden of proof because the evidence shows that vog was a general environmental condition and that Van Ness’s exposure at home was equal to or greater than his exposure at work. The Defense further contends that Dr. Mathison’s report is conclusory and fails to isolate workplace exposure. Upon review, the Court finds that the record contains sufficient evidence from which a reasonable fact-finder could conclude that employment was a substantial contributing factor to Van Ness’s injury.

**A. Disputed Factual Issues Regarding Comparative Exposure**

The Defense relies heavily on Vice Principal Dennis’s statement that vog was "even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, to argue that home exposure was greater. However, this statement does not resolve the issue of "substantial contribution" as a matter of law. Van Ness testified that his home was air-conditioned and equipped with HEPA filters recommended by his doctor, whereas his work environment was non-air conditioned, dusty, and required him to clean computer filters clogged with dust.

This creates a genuine dispute of material fact regarding the *nature* and *intensity* of the exposure in each environment. While the ambient vog may have been denser in Kihei, the conditions at Lahainaluna—specifically the lack of air conditioning, the accumulation of dust, and the physical exertion required to traverse the campus—could have intensified the effect of that exposure. It is the role of the fact-finder, not this Court on appeal, to weigh the Vice Principal’s observation against Van Ness’s testimony regarding his home filtration system and the specific conditions at the school. *See Toney*, 99 Hawai'i at 237, 54 P.3d at 405 (holding that the unitary test focuses on whether employment contributed substantially to the injury, not whether it was the sole cause).

**B. Medical Evidence Supporting Causation**

The Defense attacks Dr. Mathison’s report as failing to distinguish between work and home exposure. However, Dr. Mathison’s report contains specific language linking the aggravation to work. Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness had "significant asthma exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke (high in sulfur dioxide) and ash" and that his condition "improved coincident to his residence in Oahu." Crucially, Dr. Mathison observed that Van Ness’s symptoms "essentially resolved" while he was in Pennsylvania, but "as soon as he returned to Maui he started to have wheezing and [shortness of breath]."

While the Defense correctly points out that Dr. Mathison did not provide a detailed quantitative analysis of exposure levels, his opinion that the symptoms worsened during work exposure constitutes competent medical evidence supporting the claimant’s burden. The Director credited Dr. Arora, who explicitly stated he could not address the referral questions without medical records, over Dr. Mathison, who reviewed the complete medical history. On remand, the Director must weigh this conflicting medical evidence under the correct legal standard, rather than dismissing Dr. Mathison’s opinion because the hazard was not unique to the workplace.

**C. Physical Exertion and Employment Conditions**

The Defense argues that physical exertion, such as walking uphill, is not a condition unique to employment and therefore cannot constitute a substantial contributing factor. This argument misapprehends the unitary test. *Toney* does not require the condition to be unique; it requires that the employment conditions substantially contribute to the injury. Van Ness testified that the physical exertion required by his job—walking up and down a steep campus to service computers—caused him to sweat, increased his heart rate, and directly exacerbated his shortness of breath. This exertion, performed in a dusty, non-air conditioned environment during a vog event, is qualitatively different from the exertion involved in daily life in a controlled, air-conditioned home. A fact-finder could reasonably conclude that the combination of exertion and environmental exposure at work was a substantial contributing factor to his respiratory decline.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Defense has not demonstrated that the evidence is legally insufficient to support a finding for Van Ness under the unitary test. Genuine disputes of material fact exist regarding the comparative exposure at home versus work, the impact of the specific workplace conditions (dust, lack of air conditioning, exertion), and the weight of the conflicting medical opinions. Because the Director applied the wrong legal standard in resolving these disputes, the Court cannot uphold the denial of benefits.

The Director’s decision is vacated. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling, wherein the Director must apply the "substantial contributing factor" standard of *Toney v. Hawaii*, 99 Hawai'i 228, 54 P.3d 396 (2002), to the facts of this case.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
