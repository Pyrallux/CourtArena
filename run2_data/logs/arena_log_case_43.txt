## LEGAL ANALYSIS

### I. THE DOCTRINE OF COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL BARS THIS PROSECUTION

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, also known as issue preclusion, prevents relitigation of issues that have been actually litigated and determined by a valid and final judgment. As the Supreme Court of Georgia has held, collateral estoppel applies when: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the one involved in the prior proceeding; (2) that issue was actually litigated and decided; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party to be estopped had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. See *Peck v. State*, 258 Ga. 83, 365 S.E.2d 692 (1988).

The prosecution's argument that collateral estoppel does not apply because administrative and criminal proceedings serve different purposes fundamentally misapprehends the doctrine. Collateral estoppel does not require identity of proceedings—it requires identity of issues. The critical inquiry is whether the factual questions underlying the criminal prosecution were actually litigated and decided in the prior proceeding, not whether the proceedings were of the same character.

### II. THE SAME FACTUAL ISSUES WERE LITIGATED AND DECIDED

The prosecution attempts to distinguish the proceedings by emphasizing that the ALU applied a preponderance of the evidence standard while criminal prosecution requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt. However, this distinction goes to the burden of proof, not to the underlying factual determinations. The question of whether Appellant actually submitted claims for services not performed, or whether he submitted claims for abortion-related services, is a factual question that does not change character based on the burden of proof applied.

The ALU's finding that the record did not support a conclusion that Appellant willfully misrepresented a material fact necessarily encompassed factual determinations about whether the services were actually performed, whether they were abortion-related, and whether Appellant possessed the requisite intent to submit claims for services to which he was not entitled. These are precisely the factual issues the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt to sustain the criminal convictions.

In *State v. McCarty*, 261 Ga. 228, 403 S.E.2d 47 (1991), this Court recognized that collateral estoppel can bar criminal prosecution when the same factual issues were determined in a prior civil or administrative proceeding. The Court held that the State is bound by factual determinations made in prior proceedings when the requirements of collateral estoppel are met, regardless of the different burden of proof applied in each proceeding.

### III. THE STATE HAD A FULL AND FAIR OPPORTUNITY TO LITIGATE

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Davis*, 273 Ga. 58, 537 S.E.2d 34 (2000), is misplaced. In *Davis*, the Court found that collateral estoppel did not apply because the administrative proceeding at issue was not adversarial and did not involve the same parties. The Court specifically noted that "the administrative process and criminal prosecution serve different purposes and have different procedural safeguards" in contexts where the administrative proceeding was non-adversarial or where the party seeking to assert preclusion was not the same party who litigated the prior proceeding.

Here, the administrative proceeding was fully adversarial. The State, through the Georgia Department of Community Health, was an active participant in the proceeding, presenting evidence and arguments in support of maintaining the withhold. The State had every incentive to prove that Appellant had engaged in fraudulent conduct, yet it failed to meet even the preponderance of the evidence standard. The State cannot now relitigate those same factual questions under a higher burden of proof when it could not meet the lower burden.

The prosecution's citation to *United States v. Williams*, 504 U.S. 36, 112 S. Ct. 1735, 118 L. Ed. 2d 352 (1992), is similarly unavailing. In *Williams*, the Supreme Court declined to apply collateral estoppel where the prior proceeding was a civil deportation proceeding that lacked the procedural protections of a criminal trial. The Court emphasized that the prior proceeding must have provided "full and fair opportunity" to litigate the issue. Here, the administrative proceeding provided exactly that opportunity—both sides presented evidence, called witnesses, and fully briefed the issues. The State was represented by counsel and had every procedural protection available.

### IV. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON *STATE V. JONES* IS MISPLACED

The prosecution cites *State v. Jones*, 263 Ga. 793, 439 S.E.2d 329 (1994), for the proposition that an administrative proceeding to recover overpayments is not equivalent to a criminal prosecution for fraud. However, a careful reading of *Jones* reveals that the Court did not hold that collateral estoppel can never apply to bar criminal prosecution based on administrative findings. Rather, the Court in *Jones* found that the specific administrative proceeding at issue had not actually litigated the factual questions underlying the criminal charges.

In *Jones*, the administrative proceeding had addressed only whether the physician had received overpayments, not whether he had knowingly and willfully submitted false claims. The Court noted that "the issues and standards of proof differ significantly" because the administrative proceeding had not determined the factual predicates for criminal liability. Here, by contrast, the ALU explicitly found that the record did not support a finding that Appellant willfully misrepresented a material fact—the precise factual question central to the criminal charges.

The prosecution's attempt to distinguish the issues based on the different legal standards (administrative "willful misrepresentation" versus criminal "knowingly and willfully") conflates the legal labels with the underlying factual inquiries. The central factual question—whether Appellant submitted claims for services he knew were not performed or were not reimbursable—is identical in both proceedings. The ALU's finding that the record did not support a conclusion of willful misrepresentation necessarily included a factual determination that Appellant did not knowingly submit false claims.

### V. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT APPLICATION OF COLLATERAL ESTOPPAL

The prosecution's position, if accepted, would allow the State to pursue multiple bites at the apple on the same factual issues. The State could lose on the facts in an administrative proceeding under a preponderance standard, then turn around and prosecute the same conduct criminally under a beyond-reasonable-doubt standard. This result offends fundamental principles of fairness and the finality of judgments.

As this Court recognized in *Peck v. State*, 258 Ga. 83, 365 S.E.2d 692 (1988), the doctrine of collateral estoppel serves important policy interests by promoting finality, preventing inconsistent results, and conserving judicial resources. These interests are particularly salient when the State has had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the factual issues in the first instance.

The prosecution argues that allowing collateral estoppel to apply would improperly hamstring the State's authority to prosecute criminal fraud. However, this concern is misplaced. The State retains full authority to prosecute criminal fraud when the factual issues have not been previously litigated, or when the prior proceeding was not adversarial or did not involve the same parties. What the State cannot do is relitigate facts it already had a full and fair opportunity to prove but failed to establish.

**Pursuant to the arguments presented by the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling:**

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, serves to preserve the finality of judgments and prevent the relitigation of issues that have already been conclusively determined. The Court finds that the Defense has successfully demonstrated that these elements are met in the present case, thereby barring the criminal prosecution.

First, the issue of whether Appellant "willfully misrepresented a material fact" was actually litigated and decided in the administrative proceeding. The Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) conducted a full hearing and issued a specific finding that "the record did not support a conclusion that appellant wilfully misrepresented a material fact." This finding directly addresses the factual predicate of the criminal charge, which alleges Appellant "knowingly and willfully" accepted payments for services not performed or for elective abortions. While the Prosecution argues that the standards of proof differ, the Defense correctly notes that the ALJ’s finding was not merely a failure of proof under a preponderance standard, but a determination that the record itself did not support the conclusion of willful misrepresentation. As held in *State v. McCarty*, 261 Ga. 228, 403 S.E.2d 47 (1991), collateral estoppel can bar a criminal prosecution when the same factual issues have been determined in a prior administrative proceeding.

Second, the State had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. The administrative proceeding was adversarial in nature, with the State actively presenting evidence and arguments to support the withhold. The Prosecution’s reliance on *State v. Davis*, 273 Ga. 58, 537 S.E.2d 34 (2000), is misplaced in this context. In *Davis*, the court distinguished administrative proceedings that lack the procedural safeguards of criminal trials. However, as the Defense argues, the proceeding here was a full adversarial hearing where the State failed to meet even the lower burden of proof. The State cannot now claim it was denied a full and fair opportunity to establish the facts of fraud when it actively participated in the hearing and failed to do so.

Third, the Prosecution’s reliance on *State v. Jones*, 263 Ga. 793, 439 S.E.2d 329 (1994), is unpersuasive because the factual record in this case differs significantly from *Jones*. In *Jones*, the administrative proceeding addressed only the receipt of overpayments and did not litigate the issue of fraudulent intent. Here, the ALJ explicitly ruled on the issue of "willful misrepresentation," which is synonymous with the intent element required for the criminal fraud charge. The distinction drawn by the Prosecution regarding the difference between administrative "willful misrepresentation" and criminal "knowing and willful" intent is a distinction without a difference in this context; both require a determination of the Appellant’s state of mind regarding the truth of the claims submitted.

Finally, the policy considerations cited by the Defense support the application of collateral estoppel. Allowing the State to pursue a criminal prosecution after failing to prove the factual basis for fraud in an administrative proceeding would offend the principles of judicial economy and fairness. The State had the opportunity to prove its case and failed. The ALJ’s determination that the record did not support a finding of willful misrepresentation is a final judgment on that factual issue.

Accordingly, the trial court erred in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement. The doctrine of collateral estoppel bars the State from relitigating the issue of whether Appellant willfully misrepresented material facts in his Medicaid billing. The prosecution is therefore precluded from proceeding on the indictment.

## II. THIS CASE IS DISTINGUISHABLE FROM *STATE v. JONES*

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Jones*, 263 Ga. 793, 439 S.E.2d 329 (1994), is misplaced because the factual record here differs critically from *Jones*.

In *Jones*, the Court emphasized that "the administrative proceeding addressed only whether the physician received overpayments, not whether he knowingly and willfully submitted false claims." The administrative proceeding in *Jones* concerned solely the question of overpayment—a purely monetary determination that did not require examination of the physician's intent. Here, by contrast, the ALU explicitly adjudicated the issue of "willful misrepresentation," which directly encompasses the question of fraudulent intent. The prosecution's argument that the ALU addressed only "reckless disregard" while the criminal standard requires "specific intent to defraud" ignores the ALU's actual ruling. ## III. *STATE v. DAVIS* DOES NOT COMPEL A DIFFERENT RESULT

The prosecution invokes *State v. Davis*, 273 Ga. 58, 537 S.E.2d 34 (2000), for the proposition that administrative proceedings lacking criminal procedural safeguards cannot support collateral estoppel. However, *Davis* is distinguishable on its facts and does not control here.

The Court emphasized that the administrative proceeding "lacks the procedural safeguards of a criminal trial" and addressed "different legal standards." Id. at 61, 537 S.E.2d at 36. ## IV. THE DOCTRINE OF COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL SERVES ITS PURPOSE HERE

The prosecution's policy arguments overlook the fundamental purpose of collateral estoppel: to prevent the government from repeatedly litigating the same factual issues until it achieves a favorable outcome. As this Court recognized in its preliminary ruling, allowing the State to pursue criminal charges after failing to prove the factual basis for fraud in an administrative proceeding would "offend the principles of judicial economy and fairness."

The prosecution argues that permitting collateral estoppel here would "immunize fraudulent actors who evade criminal liability through administrative proceedings." This argument inverts the logic of the doctrine. Dr. Malloy did not "evade" criminal liability through the administrative proceeding; rather, the State had the opportunity to prove fraud in an adversarial forum and failed to do so. The doctrine of collateral estoppel ensures that the State bears the burden of proving its case once, not infinitely.

The prosecution's reliance on the statement from *Davis* that "the State's interest in prosecuting criminal fraud is distinct from its interest in recovering overpayments" is unavailing. Here, the State did not merely seek to recover overpayments in the administrative proceeding; the State sought to withhold all Medicaid reimbursements based on allegations of fraud. The State invoked the fraud allegations as the basis for its enforcement action, and the ALU adjudicated those allegations. The State cannot now claim that its interests in the administrative proceeding were limited to monetary recovery when it explicitly pursued fraud-based penalties.

## V. THE ELEMENTS OF COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL ARE SATISFIED

Applying the four-part test from *Peck v. State*, 258 Ga. 83, 365 S.E.2d 692 (1988), the defense establishes that collateral estoppel bars this prosecution:

The question of whether Dr. Malloy "willfully misrepresented a material fact" in his Medicaid billing is identical to the question of whether he "knowingly and willfully" accepted improper payments under the criminal statute. Both inquiries require a determination of Dr. Malloy's intent in submitting the claims.

**Second**, the issue was actually litigated and decided. The ALU conducted a full hearing and issued a specific finding that "the record did not support a conclusion that appellant wilfully misrepresented a material fact." This was not a default judgment or a procedural dismissal; it was a substantive determination on the merits.

**Third**, the determination was necessary to the judgment. The ALU's finding that no willful misrepresentation occurred was essential to its decision to reverse the withhold and release the funds. Had the ALU found that willful misrepresentation was present, it would have affirmed the withhold.

**Fourth**, the State had a full and fair opportunity to litigate. The State participated as an adversarial party in the administrative proceeding, presenting evidence and arguments in support of the withhold. The State cannot now claim it was denied an opportunity to establish the facts of fraud when it actively participated in the hearing and failed to do so.

**Pursuant to the rebuttals presented by the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court updates its preliminary ruling as follows:**

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of issues that have been conclusively determined in a prior proceeding. As established in *Peck v. State*, 258 Ga. 83, 365 S.E.2d 692 (1988), collateral estoppel applies only when: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the one involved in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and decided; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party to be estopped had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. Upon review of the rebuttals, the Court finds that the trial court correctly denied Appellant’s plea in abatement because the elements required for collateral estoppel are not satisfied in this context.

First, the issue of "willful misrepresentation" determined by the Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) is not identical to the "knowing and willful" intent required for the criminal fraud charge under OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2). The Defense argues that the ALJ’s finding that "the record did not support a conclusion that appellant wilfully misrepresented a material fact" resolves the factual predicate for the criminal charge. However, the Prosecution correctly identifies that the administrative proceeding addressed Medicaid program liability under a preponderance of the evidence standard, whereas the criminal prosecution requires proof of specific intent to defraud beyond a reasonable doubt.

The Supreme Court of Georgia addressed this precise distinction in *State v. Jones*, 263 Ga. 793, 439 S.E.2d 329 (1994). In *Jones*, the Court held that an administrative proceeding to recover Medicaid overpayments does not preclude a subsequent criminal fraud prosecution because the administrative issue—whether overpayments occurred—does not equate to the criminal element of "fraudulent intent." *Jones*, 263 Ga. at 795, 439 S.E.2d at 331. The Court explicitly stated that "the issues and standards of proof differ significantly." Id. Similarly, here, the ALJ’s finding concerned whether Appellant’s conduct met the administrative definition of "willful misrepresentation" under Medicaid rules, not whether he possessed the specific criminal intent required for conviction under the penal statute. The administrative standard focuses on the accuracy of billing relative to program rules, while the criminal standard focuses on the defendant's state of mind.

Second, the nature of the ALJ’s determination further supports the conclusion that the issues are not identical. The ALJ noted that the State’s interpretation of the services as abortion-related was "reasonable." This finding indicates that the factual basis for the potential fraud existed, but the ALJ determined the evidence was insufficient to meet the administrative burden of proof. A finding that the State failed to prove its case by a preponderance of the evidence is not the equivalent of a factual finding that the Appellant did not commit criminal fraud. As the Prosecution argues, the ALJ’s ruling was a failure of proof under a civil standard, not a definitive resolution of the factual question of criminal intent.

Third, the procedural context of the administrative proceeding distinguishes this case from those where collateral estoppel might apply. In *State v. The ALU's finding that the record does not support a conclusion of willful misrepresentation is directly relevant to whether Dr. Malloy knowingly and willfully accepted payments to which he was not entitled.

The prosecution relies on *State v. Jones*, 263 Ga. 793, 439 S.E.2d 329 (1994), for the proposition that an administrative proceeding does not preclude a criminal prosecution. However, the defense respectfully submits that *Jones* is distinguishable.

In *Jones*, the administrative proceeding addressed only whether overpayments had occurred—whether the provider had received more money than entitled. The Court held that this issue was not identical to the criminal element of fraudulent intent. The administrative proceeding in *Jones* did not involve a determination on whether the provider had acted with the specific intent to defraud.

Here, by contrast, the administrative proceeding did address the question of willful misrepresentation—the very conduct that underlies the criminal charge. The ALU did not merely determine that overpayments had occurred; it determined that the evidence did not support a finding that Dr. Malloy had engaged in conduct that would constitute fraud or willful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program.

This distinction is critical. The ALU's finding went to the heart of the criminal matter—whether Dr. Malloy acted with the requisite intent in submitting his Medicaid claims.

## III. THE STATE HAD A FULL AND FAIR OPPORTUNITY TO LITIGATE

The prosecution suggests that the administrative proceeding somehow lacked the procedural safeguards necessary to trigger collateral estoppel. This argument is without merit.

The administrative hearing before the ALU was a formal adversarial proceeding. The State, through the Department of Community Health, was represented by counsel and actively participated in the hearing. The State had the opportunity to present evidence, cross-examine witnesses, and argue its position. The State exercised these opportunities fully.

The fact that the proceeding was conducted under a preponderance of the evidence standard rather than a beyond-reasonable-doubt standard does not diminish the finality of the determination. Collateral estoppel applies to issues decided in civil proceedings all the time. The doctrine is not limited to proceedings with identical burdens of proof.

As the Georgia Supreme Court recognized in *Peck v. State*, collateral estoppel applies when the party to be estopped had a "full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue." The State had exactly that opportunity here. It chose to pursue the matter in the administrative forum, it presented its case, and it lost. It cannot now relitigate the same issue in a criminal forum simply because it prefers the different standard of proof.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Davis*, 273 Ga. 58, 537 S.E.2d 34 (2000), is similarly misplaced. In *Davis*, the Court held that collateral estoppel did not apply where the administrative proceeding had not actually decided the identical issue. The Court noted that the administrative proceeding in that case "lacked the procedural safeguards of a criminal trial." However, the Court did not hold that collateral estoppel can never apply to bar criminal prosecutions based on prior administrative determinations. Rather, the Court applied the traditional four-part test from *Peck* and found that the elements were not satisfied.

Here, by contrast, all four elements of the *Peck* test are satisfied: the issue is identical, it was actually litigated and decided, the determination was necessary to the judgment, and the State had a full and fair opportunity to litigate. The *Davis* case supports the application of collateral estoppel where the elements are met, as they are here.

## IV. THE POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT COLLATERAL ESTOPPEL

The prosecution invokes policy considerations, arguing that allowing collateral estoppel to bar criminal prosecutions would undermine the State's authority to enforce criminal laws. The defense submits that the opposite is true.

The doctrine of collateral estoppel exists to promote finality and prevent harassment of parties through repetitive litigation. It ensures that when the State has had a full and fair opportunity to litigate an issue and has lost, it cannot return to a different forum and relitigate the same issue.

Here, the State conducted an investigation, referred the matter to its Medicaid Fraud Control Unit, imposed a withhold on Dr. Malloy's reimbursements, and then fully litigated the fraud issue in an administrative proceeding. The State lost. Having failed to meet its burden under the preponderance standard, the State now seeks to prosecute Dr. Malloy criminally and hold him to a higher standard of proof.

This is precisely the "second bite at the apple" that collateral estoppel is designed to prevent. The State cannot be permitted to use the criminal justice system as a fallback when its administrative efforts fail. Such an approach would create a system where providers are subjected to administrative proceedings and then, if the State loses, face criminal charges based on the same conduct—all without any new evidence being discovered.

The prosecution's argument that the State's interests in the administrative and criminal proceedings are "distinct" is unpersuasive. Both proceedings arise from the same conduct—the same Medicaid billing claims—and both require a determination of whether Dr. Malloy engaged in fraudulent misrepresentation. The State cannot compartmentalize its enforcement activities to avoid the preclusive effects of its own determinations.

The defense respectfully submits that the policy considerations strongly support applying collateral estoppel in this case. To hold otherwise would allow the State to harass citizens through repeated proceedings addressing the same conduct, simply by changing forums and standards of proof.

## V. THE DEFENSE'S POSITION IS CONSISTENT WITH FUNDAMENTAL FAIRNESS

Beyond the technical requirements of collateral estoppel, there are fundamental fairness concerns at stake.

Dr. Malloy was told by the administrative tribunal—the forum specifically designed to adjudicate Medicaid billing disputes—that the record does not support a finding that he committed fraud or willful misrepresentation. Relying on this determination, he resumed his practice and defended his professional reputation.

Now, years later, the State seeks to prosecute him criminally based on the same conduct that was already adjudicated in his favor. This result is fundamentally unfair.

The defense does not dispute that the State has the authority to prosecute criminal fraud. However, the State must prosecute based on evidence discovered through its investigation—not based on issues that have already been decided in favor of the defendant in a prior proceeding.

When the ALU found that the record did not support a finding of willful misrepresentation, Dr. Malloy had a legitimate expectation that the matter was resolved. He should not now be forced to defend himself against criminal charges based on the same conduct.

**FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to the closing arguments presented by the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court issues its final ruling as follows:**

The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement based on the doctrine of collateral estoppel. Appellant argues that the Administrative Law Judge’s (ALJ) finding that he did not commit "willful misrepresentation" in the administrative proceeding bars the State from prosecuting him for "knowing and willful" Medicaid fraud. For the reasons stated herein, the Court finds that the trial court correctly denied the plea, as the requirements for collateral estoppel are not met.

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of an issue only when the following four elements are satisfied: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the one involved in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and decided; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party to be estopped had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. *Peck v. State*, 258 Ga. 83, 365 S.E.2d 692 (1988).

Upon careful review of the record and the arguments of counsel, the Court holds that the first element—identity of the issue—is not satisfied, and the nature of the ALJ’s decision precludes a finding of collateral estoppel.

**I. The Issues Are Not Identical**

The Defense contends that the ALJ’s determination regarding "willful misrepresentation" is identical to the "knowing and willful" intent required for criminal liability under OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2). While the terminology is similar, the legal inquiries are distinct. The administrative proceeding concerned whether Appellant’s billing practices violated Medicaid regulations and resulted in overpayments. The criminal prosecution, however, concerns whether Appellant possessed the specific intent to defraud the State.

As the Prosecution correctly notes, the Supreme Court of Georgia has consistently distinguished between administrative determinations of overpayments and criminal charges of fraud. In *State v. Jones*, 263 Ga. 793, 439 S.E.2d 329 (1994), the Court held that an administrative proceeding to recover Medicaid overpayments does not preclude a subsequent criminal fraud prosecution because the issue of whether overpayments occurred is not identical to the criminal element of "fraudulent intent." *Jones*, 263 Ga. at 795, 439 S.E.2d at 331. The Court explicitly stated that "the issues and standards of proof differ significantly." Id.

Here, the ALJ’s finding that the "record did not support a conclusion that appellant wilfully misrepresented a material fact" was a determination regarding civil liability under the Medicaid program. It did not adjudicate whether Appellant possessed the specific criminal intent required for a fraud conviction. The administrative standard focuses on the accuracy of billing and compliance with program rules, whereas the criminal standard focuses on the defendant’s state of mind. Because the administrative "willful misrepresentation" encompasses a broader range of conduct than the criminal "knowing and willful" intent to defraud, the issues are not identical for purposes of collateral estoppel.

**II. The ALJ’s Decision Was a Failure of Proof, Not a Factual Exoneration**

Even assuming the issues overlapped, the nature of the ALJ’s decision prevents the application of collateral estoppel. The ALJ did not make a positive factual finding that Appellant did not misrepresent facts; rather, the ALJ found that the "record did not support a conclusion" of willful misrepresentation. This language indicates a failure of proof by the State under the preponderance of the evidence standard, rather than a definitive resolution of the factual question of Appellant’s intent.

A finding that the State failed to meet its burden of proof in a civil proceeding is not the equivalent of a factual finding that the Appellant is innocent of criminal fraud. As noted in *State v. Jones*, the State’s failure to prove an issue in one forum does not estop it from attempting to prove that issue in another forum where the stakes and standards are different. *Jones*, 263 Ga. at 795, 439 S.E.2d at 331. The ALJ’s ruling was a determination that the evidence was insufficient to sustain a civil withhold, not a factual adjudication that Appellant lacked the requisite intent for criminal liability.

**III. Procedural Safeguards and Policy Considerations**

The Defense argues that the State had a "full and fair opportunity" to litigate the issue and that applying collateral estoppel prevents the State from taking a "second bite at the apple." However, the administrative hearing was a civil proceeding designed to resolve a payment dispute, not a criminal trial adjudicating guilt. As held in *State v. Davis*, 273 Ga. 58, 537 S.E.2d 34 (2000), administrative proceedings "lack the procedural safeguards of a criminal trial and address different legal standards." *Davis*, 273 Ga. at 61, 537 S.E.2d at 36. The absence of these safeguards, combined with the different burden of proof, means that allowing the administrative decision to preclude the criminal prosecution would be improper.

Furthermore, the policy considerations articulated by the Prosecution are sound. The State’s interest in prosecuting criminal fraud is distinct from its interest in recovering overpayments through administrative channels. *Davis*, 273 Ga. at 62, 537 S.E.2d at 37. Permitting an administrative finding regarding billing accuracy to bar a criminal prosecution would undermine the State’s authority to enforce penal laws and protect public funds. The doctrine of collateral estoppel is designed to prevent relitigation of *adjudicated facts*, not to insulate criminal defendants from prosecution because the State failed to meet a lower civil burden of proof in a separate forum.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the trial court did not err in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement. The doctrine of collateral estoppel does not bar the criminal prosecution because the issues litigated in the administrative proceeding are not identical to the criminal charges, and the ALJ’s decision constituted a failure of proof under a civil standard rather than a preclusive factual determination.

**IT IS ORDERED that the judgment of the trial court be affirmed.**
