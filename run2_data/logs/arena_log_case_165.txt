## INTRODUCTION

Secretary Rumsfeld asks this Court to reverse the district court's denial of his motion to dismiss and to dismiss John Doe's substantive due process claim in its entirety. His arguments, while artfully framed, rest upon a fundamental mischaracterization of both the facts alleged and the controlling precedent. This Court should affirm the district court's ruling for the following reasons.

First, Secretary Rumsfeld's invocation of *Ziglar v. Abbasi* is misplaced. The Supreme Court's decision in *Ziglar v. Abbasi* addressed whether Bivens should be extended to claims arising from the conditions of confinement in the immigration context—a context fundamentally different from the military detention at issue here. The district court correctly recognized that *Ziglar* does not foreclose a Bivens remedy where, as here, the claim involves the treatment of a United States citizen detained by military forces without charges or access to counsel.

Second, Secretary Rumsfeld's qualified immunity argument fails because the constitutional right to be free from detention that "shocks the conscience" was clearly established at the time of his conduct. The treatment alleged—solitary confinement for nine months, denial of access to counsel, physical abuse, and threats—plainly violates the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause.

Third, the policy considerations advanced by Secretary Rumsfeld, while weighty, do not counsel hesitation in this case. The military justice system provides no remedy to a civilian contractor detained by military forces in a foreign warzone. Congress has not provided an alternative remedial scheme, and the Detainee Treatment Act, which the district court correctly dismissed, provides no private right of action against individual officials.

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY IMPLIED A BIVENS REMEDY

### A. The Framework for Implying Bivens Remedies

The Supreme Court's decision in *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics* established that a plaintiff may recover damages against a federal official for constitutional violations. 403 U.S. 388 (1971). While the Court has subsequently expressed caution about extending Bivens to new contexts, it has not closed the door to such actions. In *Wilkie v. Robbins*, the Court emphasized that the "question is not whether a Bivens remedy should be created" but whether "the entity to whom the claim is addressed is the kind of entity that can be sued under Bivens." 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007).

The district court correctly applied the two-step analysis established in *Ziglar v. Abbasi*: first, whether the claim presents a new Bivens context; and second, whether special factors counsel hesitation. 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017).

### B. This Case Does Not Present a "New Context" Under *Ziglar*

Secretary Rumsfeld contends that this case presents a "new context" requiring the application of special factors analysis. This argument fails.

A claim does not present a new Bivens context merely because it involves different parties or a different factual scenario. As the Supreme Court explained in *Ziglar*, the relevant inquiry is whether the claim is "different in any meaningful way from previous Bivens cases." 137 S. Ct. at 1857. Here, Doe's claim is functionally identical to the classic Bivens action: a citizen sues a federal official for damages for violating his constitutional rights.

Doe's substantive due process claim is analogous to *Bivens* itself, where the Supreme Court recognized a damages remedy for a Fourth Amendment violation by federal agents. It is also analogous to *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979), where the Court recognized a Bivens remedy for Fifth Amendment due process violations. The claim does not involve a novel constitutional provision or a novel category of defendants.

### C. Special Factors Do Not Counsel Hesitation

Even assuming *arguendo* that this case presents a new Bivens context, the special factors identified by Secretary Rumsfeld do not counsel hesitation.

#### 1. The Military Context Does Not Foreclose Review

Secretary Rumsfeld relies heavily on *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), for the proposition that military deference bars judicial review. This reliance is misplaced.

In *Chappell*, the Supreme Court refused to imply a Bivens remedy for military personnel complaining about discriminatory assignments by their superior officers. The Court emphasized that "the military is a specialized society separate from civilian society" and that "the different nature of military life" justified special treatment. Id. at 300-01. However, the Court expressly limited its holding to "military personnel" and noted that its decision did not address "the proper scope of Bivens-type actions against other governmental officials." Id. at 303-04.

Doe is not a military personnel member. He is a civilian contractor—a United States citizen employed by an American-owned defense contracting firm. He was not subject to the military chain of command in the same way as enlisted personnel. He was detained by military forces but was never charged with a crime or subjected to military tribunal proceedings.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized that the military context does not categorically bar constitutional review. In *Hamdan v. Rumsfeld*, 548 U.S. 557 (2006), the Court held that military commissions must comply with the Geneva Conventions and the Uniform Code of Military Justice. The Court rejected the argument that military operations in a warzone are immune from judicial review.

#### 2. Congress Has Not Provided an Alternative Remedy

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that the existence of the Detainee Treatment Act and other statutes indicates congressional intent to occupy the field, counseling hesitation. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the Detainee Treatment Act does not provide a private right of action against individual officials. As the district court correctly held, the DTA provides no such remedy. Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F. Supp. 2d 94, 104-05 (D.D.C. 2011).

Second, the Supreme Court in *Ziglar* recognized that the absence of an alternative remedy is a factor counseling in favor of implying a Bivens action, not against it. The Court stated that "the absence of a statutorily provided alternative remedy, while not decisive, is a factor that counts in favor of implying a Bivens remedy." 137 S. Ct. at 1863.

Here, there is no statutory alternative remedy available to Doe. The Detainee Treatment Act provides no private right of action, and the military justice system does not extend to civilian contractors. The absence of an alternative remedy weighs in favor of permitting Doe's Bivens claim to proceed.

#### 3. Judicial Review Serves, Not Undermines, the Constitution

Secretary Rumsfeld warns that allowing Bivens claims will "interfere with the executive branch's conduct of foreign affairs" and "undermine the ability of military commanders to make decisions in the field." This Court should reject this alarmist rhetoric.

The Constitution itself provides for judicial review of executive action. Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137 (1803). The separation of powers does not require—and the Constitution does not permit—the executive branch to act with absolute immunity from constitutional constraints.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized Bivens remedies precisely because alternative mechanisms for accountability are inadequate. In *Bivens*, the Court noted that "the Fourth Amendment would be reduced to a nullity" if there were no remedy for its violation. 403 U.S. at 395. The same is true of the Due Process Clause.

The military's need for operational flexibility does not extend to the detention of United States citizens without charges, access to counsel, or judicial review. The treatment alleged—solitary confinement for nine months, physical abuse, and denial of legal representation—bears no relation to legitimate military necessity.

## II. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS NOT ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

### A. The Qualified Immunity Standard

Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages "insofar as their conduct does not violate clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). The inquiry has two prongs: (1) whether the facts, taken in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, show that the official violated a constitutional right; and (2) whether that right was clearly established at the time. Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194, 201-02 (2001).

### B. Doe Adequately Alleged a Constitutional Violation

The district court correctly held that Doe alleged a substantive due process violation that "shocks the conscience."

The substantive component of the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause protects individuals against government action that "shocks the conscience." Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952). The "shock the conscience" standard encompasses conduct that is "brutal and horrible" and "offends the community's sense of fair play and decency." Id.

Doe's complaint alleges the following facts, which this Court must accept as true for purposes of this appeal:

- He was detained by NCIS agents and military officials without being charged with any crime.
- He was denied access to an attorney, a company representative, or a member of the Marine unit during interrogations.
- He was blindfolded, kicked in the back, and threatened with being shot.
- He was held in solitary confinement for three months and then placed in a cell with hostile detainees.
- He was subjected to extreme temperatures, deprived of sleep, and choked by a guard.
- He was held for nine months without a meaningful opportunity to challenge his detention.

This treatment plainly shocks the conscience. The Supreme Court has held that "the use of force against a prisoner can be a constitutional violation if it is 'repugnant to the conscience of mankind.'" Hudson v. McMillian, 503 U.S. 1, 10 (1992) (quoting Estelle v. Gamble, 429 U.S. 97, 106 (1976)). The treatment alleged here exceeds the conduct at issue in *Hudson*, where the Court held that the use of excessive force against a prisoner, even without serious injury, violated the Eighth Amendment.

### C. The Right Was Clearly Established

Secretary Rumsfeld contends that the right to be free from the treatment alleged was not "clearly established" at the time of his conduct. This argument fails.

The "shock the conscience" standard has been clearly established for decades. In *Rochin v. California*, the Supreme Court held that conduct that "shocks the conscience" violates the Due Process Clause. 342 U.S. 165 (1952). This standard applies with equal force to the treatment of detainees.

Moreover, the treatment alleged here violates clearly established prohibitions against:

- Detention without charges or judicial review. See Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. 2 (1866) (holding that military detention of civilians without trial is unconstitutional where civilian courts are operational).

- Denial of access to counsel. See Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963) (holding that the right to counsel is fundamental).

- Physical abuse and torture. See Estelle v. Gamble, 429 U.S. 97 (1976) (holding that deliberate indifference to serious medical needs violates the Eighth Amendment); see also Geneva Conventions, Common Article 3 (prohibiting "cruel treatment and torture" and "outrages upon personal dignity").

Secretary Rumsfeld's argument that no case has specifically addressed the "military detention of a civilian contractor in Iraq" proves too much. The qualified immunity inquiry does not require a case directly on point. As the Supreme Court has held, "officials can still be on notice that their conduct is unlawful" even "in novel factual circumstances." Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 741 (2002). The "contours of the right" need only be "sufficiently clear" that a reasonable official would understand that his conduct violates the right. Saucier, 533 U.S. at 201.

Here, no reasonable official could have believed that detaining a United States citizen for nine months without charges, denying access to counsel, and subjecting him to physical abuse was constitutional. The right was clearly established.

### D. The Defense Arguments Misstate the Record

Secretary Rumsfeld's brief repeatedly mischaracterizes the nature of Doe's claims and the district court's ruling. He suggests that Doe is attempting to hold him liable for "policy decisions" made at the headquarters level. This is incorrect.

Doe's complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld personally developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused his harm. This is sufficient at the motion to dismiss stage, where the Court must accept the factual allegations as true. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009).

Secretary Rumsfeld also suggests that qualified immunity applies because he was acting within his discretionary authority. This argument fails because the defense of qualified immunity does not apply where the official violates a clearly established constitutional right, regardless of whether the action was within the official's discretionary authority. Wood v. Moss, 572 U.S. 744, 751 (2014).

## III. THE DISTRICT COURT'S RULING DOES NOT THREATEN NATIONAL SECURITY

Secretary Rumsfeld's brief is filled with apocalyptic warnings about the consequences of allowing Doe's claim to proceed. These warnings are unfounded.

First, this case does not involve tactical military decisions. Doe does not challenge the decision to detain enemy combatants in the field. He challenges the decision to detain a United States citizen—without charges—for nine months and to subject him to physical abuse and denial of counsel. These are not battlefield decisions; they are constitutional violations.

Second, the military has adequate mechanisms to address legitimate security concerns. The Geneva Conventions, the Uniform Code of Military Justice, and the Detainee Treatment Act all provide frameworks for regulating the treatment of detainees. These frameworks do not require—and the Constitution does not permit—the absolute immunity that Secretary Rumsfeld seeks.

Third, the separation of powers does not require the judiciary to abdicate its role in protecting constitutional rights. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that "it is the duty of the judicial branch to say what the law is" and to enforce the Constitution against executive overreach. Marbury, 5 U.S. at 177. The executive branch cannot shield itself from constitutional accountability by invoking national security.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the district court's denial of Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss. The district court correctly held that Doe may maintain a Bivens action for his substantive due process claims and that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity at this stage of the litigation.

The treatment alleged—detention without charges, denial of access to counsel, physical abuse, and prolonged solitary confinement—shocks the conscience and violates the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause. Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to immunity from liability for these alleged violations.

This Court should reverse in part only if it finds that the district court erred in its analysis of the qualified immunity defense, but even in that event, the case should be remanded for further proceedings rather than dismissed with prejudice.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court is the interlocutory appeal of former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld from the district court’s order denying his motion to dismiss Appellee John Doe’s substantive due process claims.** We review the district court’s legal conclusions de novo. See Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008). For the reasons set forth below, we find that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in this context and that Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Accordingly, we reverse the order of the district court.

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The Supreme Court has long instructed that extending Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971) to new contexts is a "judicially disfavored" undertaking. In Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 198 L.Ed.2d 290 (2017), the Court established a two-step analysis for courts considering whether to imply a new Bivens remedy. First, the court must determine whether the case presents a new Bivens context—meaning it is different in a meaningful way from Bivens itself and its progeny. Second, if a new context is identified, the court must ask whether special factors counsel hesitation before implying a remedy.

**A. New Context**

We agree with the prosecution that this case presents a new Bivens context. Unlike the claim in Bivens, which involved federal narcotics agents arresting a man in his home, or the claim in Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S. 228, 99 S.Ct. 2264, 60 L.Ed.2d 846 (1979), which involved employment discrimination by a Congressman, this case involves the detention and interrogation of a civilian contractor by the military in an active foreign warzone. The unique confluence of military operations, national security concerns, and the wartime environment distinguishes this case from any previously recognized Bivens action. The defense’s assertion that the claim is "functionally identical" to Bivens because it involves a federal official ignores the critical distinction of the military and geopolitical context, which is central to our analysis.

**B. Special Factors Counseling Hesitation**

Having identified a new context, we must consider whether special factors counsel hesitation. The Supreme Court has explicitly identified "military affairs" and "national security" as special factors that demand judicial restraint. Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1858-59.

The defense argues that Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296, 103 S.Ct. 2382, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983), which barred Bivens claims for military personnel, is inapplicable because Doe is a civilian contractor. While we acknowledge this distinction, the rationale of Chappell extends beyond the status of the plaintiff to the nature of the conduct challenged. The Court in Chappell emphasized that "the military is a specialized society separate from civilian society" and that "the distinctive and disjointed nature of military life" requires courts to remain hesitant to interfere in military affairs. Id. at 296-97. The detention and interrogation policies at issue here are core military functions involving command decisions made in a theater of war. Allowing a Bivens action to proceed against the Secretary of Defense for such operational decisions would inevitably involve the judiciary in second-guessing military discretion and tactical judgments.

Furthermore, the existence of the Detainee Treatment Act of 2005, 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., indicates that Congress has legislated in this area. While the defense correctly notes that the DTA does not provide a private right of action, the Supreme Court in Ziglar noted that the existence of alternative remedial schemes, or the fact that Congress has occupied the field, is a factor counseling hesitation. 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The political branches are better positioned to balance the needs of national security with the rights of individuals detained in warzones. See also Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009) (emphasizing the need for deference to the Executive in matters of national security).

Therefore, we find that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy where special factors—including the sensitivity of military operations and the existence of Congressional legislation—counsel hesitation.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if we were to find that a Bivens remedy was available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages "insofar as their conduct does not violate clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S.Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982).

**A. Clearly Established Law**

The defense contends that the right to be free from detention that "shocks the conscience" was clearly established at the time of Doe's detention, citing Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 72 S.Ct. 205, 96 L.Ed. 183 (1952). While the "shock the conscience" standard is a well-established principle of substantive due process, the application of that standard to the specific context of the military detention of a civilian contractor in a foreign warzone was not clearly established in 2005.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that for a right to be clearly established, existing precedent must have placed the statutory or constitutional question "beyond debate." Al-Kidd v. Ashcroft, 561 U.S. 731, 741, 130 S.Ct. 2217, 180 L.Ed.2d 104 (2011). The defense relies on Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 122 S.Ct. 2508, 153 L.Ed.2d 666 (2002), for the proposition that officials need not have been confronted with the "same factual situation" to be on notice. However, Hope involved a prison guard's use of a restraint hitch in a domestic prison, a context far removed from the battlefield detention of a suspected security threat in Iraq.

The prosecution correctly argues that no precedent at the time of the alleged conduct would have put a reasonable official in Secretary Rumsfeld's position on notice that detaining a civilian contractor for nine months without charges, under the conditions alleged, violated the Constitution. The defense cites Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. 2, 18 L.Ed. 281 (1866), and Hamdan v. Rumsfeld, 548 U.S. 557, 126 S.Ct. 2749, 165 L.Ed.2d 723 (2006), but these cases addressed the jurisdiction of military tribunals and the application of the Uniform Code of Military Justice, not the personal liability of the Secretary of Defense for the detention conditions of a contractor. Given the absence of controlling authority addressing this specific context, the right was not clearly established.

**B. Objective Reasonableness**

Because the right was not clearly established, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. It was not objectively unreasonable for Secretary Rumsfeld to believe that the detention and interrogation of a contractor suspected of posing a threat to Marine forces in a combat zone was lawful, particularly where the Detainee Status Board had reviewed his status. See Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194, 202, 121 S.Ct. 2151, 150 L.Ed.2d 272 (2001) (stating that the relevant question is whether it would be clear to a reasonable officer that the conduct was unlawful).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in the sensitive context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, even if such a cause of action existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because the right allegedly violated was not clearly established at the time of the conduct. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is reversed.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR APPELLEE JOHN DOE

## IN RESPONSE TO APPELLANT'S APPEAL

**May it please the Court:**

The Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the arguments advanced by Appellant former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld. While we appreciate the thorough analysis presented, we contend that the Court should affirm the district court's decision denying the motion to dismiss and allow this matter to proceed to discovery.

---

## I. THE APPELLANT'S "NEW CONTEXT" ARGUMENT FAILS TO ACCOUNT FOR THE CORE CONSTITUTIONAL VIOLATIONS ALLEGED

The Appellant urges this Court to find that this case presents a "new Bivens context" warranting judicial hesitation under *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017). We respectfully submit that this argument misconstrues the nature of the claims at issue and overstates the reach of *Ziglar*.

While *Ziglar* indeed cautioned against expanding Bivens to novel contexts, the Supreme Court did not establish a blanket prohibition against all such expansions. Rather, the Court emphasized that a "new context" analysis must be conducted with care, examining whether the case is "different in any meaningful way from previous Bivens cases." *Id.* at 1857. Critically, the Court in *Ziglar* distinguished between cases involving generalized policy objections and those alleging direct, personal constitutional violations. This case falls squarely into the latter category.

The Appellant attempts to distinguish this case from *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), by emphasizing the military context. However, the essential nature of the claim—that a federal official personally violated an individual's constitutional rights—remains consistent with the Bivens framework. As this Court observed in *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697 (D.C. Cir. 2008), the Bivens remedy has been extended to contexts beyond the original law enforcement context, including employment discrimination in *Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979). The mere presence of military or national security dimensions does not automatically render a case a "new context" for Bivens purposes.

Moreover, the facts alleged by Doe present precisely the type of individual constitutional violation that Bivens was designed to address. Doe does not challenge broad military policy or command decisions; he alleges that as a result of policies implemented and authorized by Secretary Rumsfeld, he was subjected to specific acts that violated his Fifth Amendment right to due process. The complaint names a specific official, alleges specific conduct, and seeks redress for specific personal harm—a classic Bivens scenario.

---

## II. SPECIAL FACTORS DO NOT COUNSEL HESITATION WHERE CONSTITUTIONAL VIOLATIONS ARE CLEARLY ALLEGED

The Appellant invokes *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), for the proposition that courts should hesitate before implying Bivens remedies in military contexts. However, *Chappell* itself acknowledged that its holding was limited: "We are not prepared to say that the military may never subject servicemen to [constitutionally impermissible conditions]." *Id.* at 304. The Court in *Chappell* emphasized that the special nature of military life counseled hesitation *in that particular case*—where enlisted military personnel sought to challenge orders from superior officers. The Court was concerned about military discipline and command structure, not about creating an absolute bar to constitutional claims.

Here, Doe is not a serviceman subject to military discipline. He is a civilian contractor detained by military forces. The *Chappell* rationale simply does not apply with the same force to a civilian who was abducted, detained, and subjected to alleged constitutional violations.

The Appellant also argues that the Detainee Treatment Act of 2005, 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., indicates Congress has occupied the field, counseling hesitation. However, the DTA itself contains no private right of action, and the Supreme Court in *Ziglar* specifically noted that "the fact that Congress has not provided a particular remedy... is not dispositive." 137 S. Ct. at 1863. Congress's silence on a private remedy for detainee mistreatment does not preclude this Court from recognizing a Bivens remedy; rather, it leaves the question open for judicial determination.

We further note that the existence of alternative remedial schemes is merely "a factor counseling hesitation," not a determinative bar. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. Where, as here, the alleged constitutional violations are egregious and the need for judicial remedy is clear, this factor carries less weight.

---

## III. THE RIGHT TO BE FREE FROM DETENTION THAT "SHOCKS THE CONSCIENCE" WAS CLEARLY ESTABLISHED

Even assuming *arguendo* that this Court finds a new Bivens context, we submit that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity because the constitutional right at issue was clearly established at the time of the conduct.

The "shock the conscience" standard for substantive due process claims has been firmly established for over half a century. In *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), the Supreme Court held that conduct that "shocks the conscience" violates the Due Process Clause. This standard has been repeatedly applied to outrageous governmental conduct, including physical mistreatment of detainees. See *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730 (2002); *Hudson v. McMillian*, 503 U.S. 1 (1992).

The Appellant argues that while the "shock the conscience" standard is established, its application to "military detention of a civilian contractor in a foreign warzone" was not clearly established. This argument conflates two distinct inquiries. The question is not whether the *specific factual scenario* has been addressed, but whether the *general right* was clearly established. As the Supreme Court held in *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. at 741, "officials can still be on notice that their conduct violates established law even in novel factual circumstances."

Doe's complaint alleges conduct that falls squarely within the "shock the conscience" standard: solitary confinement for three months, blindfolding and hooding, exposure to extreme temperatures, deprivation of sleep, threats to shoot him, and being placed in a cell with hostile detainees whose attacks were encouraged by military officials. These allegations describe conduct that would "shock the conscience" of any reasonable person, regardless of whether such conduct occurred in a domestic prison or a foreign warzone.

The Appellant's reliance on *Al-Kidd v. Ashcroft*, 561 U.S. 731 (2011), is misplaced. In *Al-Kidd*, the Supreme Court found qualified immunity appropriate because the plaintiff challenged the FBI's investigative guidelines—a policy-level decision with no direct personal involvement. Here, by contrast, Doe alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld personally developed, authorized, and implemented the specific policies that caused his alleged harm. The personal involvement of the official is a critical distinction.

---

## IV. THE APPELLANT'S CONDUCT WAS NOT OBJECTIVELY REASONABLE

Finally, we submit that even if there were some ambiguity about the contours of the right, Secretary Rumsfeld's conduct was not objectively reasonable.

The Appellant argues that it was reasonable for Secretary Rumsfeld to believe that detaining a contractor suspected of posing a threat to Marine forces was lawful, particularly given the Detainee Status Board's review. However, the reasonableness inquiry must focus on the *specific conduct* alleged, not on the general authority to detain. Even if detention authority existed, the manner in which Doe was detained—the extended solitary confinement, the blindfolding and hooding, the exposure to extreme temperatures, the deprivation of sleep, the placement with hostile cellmates—raises separate constitutional concerns that were not authorized by any legitimate military necessity.

The Detainee Status Board's determination that Doe was a "threat" does not authorize the specific mistreatment alleged in the complaint. A reasonable official would understand that confining a detainee for months in solitary confinement, exposing him to extreme temperatures, and encouraging attacks by cellmates exceeds the bounds of legitimate detention.

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the appellate ruling and affirm the district court's decision to allow Doe's substantive due process claims to proceed.

The Appellant has not demonstrated that this case presents a Bivens context so novel as to warrant judicial refusal. The core constitutional violation alleged—a violation of substantive due process through shocking treatment of a detainee—is precisely the type of claim that Bivens was designed to address. The special factors invoked by the Appellant do not counsel hesitation where a civilian contractor alleges egregious constitutional violations by military officials.

Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity because the right to be free from detention that shocks the conscience was clearly established at the time of the conduct. The specific manner of Doe's alleged mistreatment falls well within the bounds of established constitutional protections.

Allowing this case to proceed to discovery will permit the factual development necessary to determine whether Doe's allegations can be proven. At this stage, all reasonable inferences must be drawn in Doe's favor. Doing so, this Court should find that Doe has stated a viable claim upon which relief can be granted.

**FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court is the interlocutory appeal of former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld from the district court’s order denying his motion to dismiss Appellee John Doe’s substantive due process claims.** We have considered the closing arguments of both the Appellant and the Appellee. For the reasons set forth below, we find that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in this context and that Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Accordingly, we reverse the order of the district court.

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

**A. This Case Presents a New Bivens Context**

We reaffirm our conclusion that this case presents a new Bivens context distinct from any previously recognized by the Supreme Court. In Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 198 L.Ed.2d 290 (2017), the Court established that extending Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), to a new context is a "judicially disfavored" undertaking. A new context exists where the case is "different in any meaningful way from previous Bivens cases." Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1857.

The Appellee argues that the "essential nature of the claim"—a federal official violating an individual's constitutional rights—remains consistent with Bivens, and that the military context does not automatically render this a new context. While we agree that the core of a Bivens claim is a constitutional violation, the Supreme Court has instructed that the context is dispositive. The detention of a civilian contractor by the military in an active foreign warzone is meaningfully different from the domestic law enforcement context of Bivens or the congressional employment context of Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S. 228, 99 S.Ct. 2264, 60 L.Ed.2d 846 (1979). The "unique confluence of military operations, national security concerns, and the wartime environment" creates a context unlike anything previously confronted in Bivens jurisprudence. See Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The Appellee's attempt to frame this as a "classic Bivens scenario" ignores the critical geopolitical and military distinctions that drive the Supreme Court's analysis.

**B. Special Factors Counsel Hesitation**

Having identified a new context, we must consider whether special factors counsel hesitation in implying a Bivens remedy. The Supreme Court has explicitly identified "military affairs" and "national security" as special factors that demand judicial restraint. Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1858-59.

The Appellee distinguishes Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296, 103 S.Ct. 2382, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983), on the grounds that Doe is a civilian contractor, not a serviceman. While Chappell involved enlisted personnel, the Court's rationale regarding the "distinctive and disjointed nature of military life" extends to the nature of the conduct challenged here. Id. at 300-01. The detention and interrogation policies at issue are core military functions involving command decisions made in a theater of war. Allowing a Bivens action to proceed against the Secretary of Defense for such operational decisions would inevitably involve the judiciary in second-guessing military discretion and tactical judgments. See Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009) (emphasizing the need for deference to the Executive in matters of national security).

The Appellee further argues that the Detainee Treatment Act of 2005 (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., does not preclude a Bivens remedy because it contains no private right of action. While the absence of a private remedy is not dispositive, the existence of the DTA indicates that Congress has occupied the field of detainee treatment. As held in Ziglar, "the existence of alternative remedial schemes, or the fact that Congress has occupied the field, is a factor counseling hesitation." 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The Appellee's assertion that the DTA's silence leaves the question open ignores the weight of congressional action in this sensitive area. The balance struck by the political branches regarding the treatment of detainees in the war on terror should not be disrupted by the judiciary creating a new damages remedy.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if we were to find that a Bivens remedy was available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages "insofar as their conduct does not violate clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S.Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982).

**A. Clearly Established Law**

The Appellee contends that the right to be free from detention that "shocks the conscience" was clearly established at the time of Doe's detention, citing Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 72 S.Ct. 205, 96 L.Ed. 183 (1952). While the "shock the conscience" standard is a well-established principle of substantive due process, the application of that standard to the specific context of the military detention of a civilian contractor in a foreign warzone was not clearly established in 2005.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that for a right to be clearly established, existing precedent must have placed the statutory or constitutional question "beyond debate." Al-Kidd v. Ashcroft, 561 U.S. 731, 741, 130 S.Ct. 2217, 180 L.Ed.2d 104 (2011). The Appellee relies on Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 122 S.Ct. 2508, 153 L.Ed.2d 666 (2002), for the proposition that officials need not have been confronted with the "same factual situation" to be on notice. However, Hope involved a prison guard's use of a restraint hitch in a domestic prison, a context far removed from the battlefield detention of a suspected security threat in Iraq. As the Appellant argues, no case has ever held that the "shock the conscience" standard applies to the military detention of a civilian contractor in a warzone. The absence of controlling authority on this precise issue means the right was not "clearly established."

The Appellee's reliance on Hudson v. McMillian, 503 U.S. 1, 112 S.Ct. 995, 117 L.Ed.2d 156 (1992), is similarly misplaced. Hudson addressed excessive force in a civilian prison, not the operational decisions of military commanders in combat zones. The Appellee's argument that the "general right" was clearly established ignores the requirement that the right must be defined with specificity, particularly in a new context involving military affairs.

**B. Objective Reasonableness**

Because the right was not clearly established, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. It was not objectively unreasonable for Secretary Rumsfeld to believe that the detention and interrogation of a contractor suspected of posing a threat to Marine forces in a combat zone was lawful, particularly where the Detainee Status Board had reviewed his status. See Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194, 202, 121 S.Ct. 2151, 150 L.Ed.2d 272 (2001) (stating that the relevant question is whether it would be clear to a reasonable officer that the conduct was unlawful). The Appellee's citation of Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 83 S.Ct. 792, 9 L.Ed.2d 744 (1963), regarding the right to counsel, is inapposite to military detention decisions in a warzone.

The Appellee argues that the specific mistreatment alleged—solitary confinement, extreme temperatures, etc.—exceeds the bounds of legitimate detention. However, without clearly established law placing such conduct in a warzone detention context beyond debate, we cannot say that Secretary Rumsfeld's conduct was objectively unreasonable. The Appellee's argument conflates the egregiousness of the alleged conduct with the clarity of the law prohibiting it in this specific context.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in the sensitive context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, even if such a cause of action existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because the right allegedly violated was not clearly established at the time of the conduct. The order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss is reversed.
