**DEFENSE RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF-APPELLANT'S OPENING BRIEF**

## I. INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Respondent Wisconsin Labor and Industry Review Commission (the "Commission") respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the Commission's decision denying workers' compensation benefits to Plaintiff-Appellant McRae. ## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The following facts are drawn from the record and are undisputed:

1. McRae was a union painter employed by Porta Painting, a company headquartered in Waukesha, Wisconsin.

2. McRae normally drove his personal vehicle from his residence in Twin Lakes, Wisconsin, directly to his assigned job site for each day.

6. On January 7, 2005, the date of the accident, McRae was driving from his residence to work. He was not performing any special errand and was not picking up supplies at the time of the accident.

7. The accident occurred within the five-county area, and McRae received no reimbursement for this commute.

---

## III. STANDARD OF REVIEW

While Plaintiff correctly notes that courts apply deference to agency interpretations, this Court must not abdicate its judicial function. The appropriate standard of review here is whether the Commission's decision was supported by substantial evidence in the record and whether the Commission correctly applied the law to the undisputed facts. As the Wisconsin Supreme Court held in *Mielke v. Department of Industry, Labor & Employment Relations*, 103 Wis. 2d 117, 127, 307 N.W.2d 256, 261 (Ct. App. 1981), "the commission's findings of fact shall be affirmed if supported by substantial evidence in the record," and this Court reviews questions of law de novo.

Even under the great weight deference framework Plaintiff cites, the Court retains authority to reverse where the agency's interpretation is contrary to the clear language of the statute or produces absurd results. The three-part test from *Johnson v. Department of Revenue*, 2006 WI 104, 294 Wis. 2d 205, 717 N.W.2d 495, requires not only that the agency has expertise and the statute is ambiguous, but also that the agency's interpretation is *reasonable*. Here, the Commission's interpretation is not merely reasonable—it is the correct interpretation as a matter of law.

---

## IV. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The "Going and Coming" Doctrine Bars Compensation

Under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1), workers' compensation benefits are available only when an employee is "performing services growing out of and incidental to his or her employment." The Wisconsin Supreme Court has consistently held that injuries sustained during the regular commute to and from work are not compensable. This is the "going and coming" doctrine.

In *Kellner v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 62 Wis. 2d 292, 300, 215 N.W.2d 18, 22 (1974), the Wisconsin Supreme Court explicitly adopted the going and coming doctrine, holding that "an injury occurring in the course of the employee's journey to or from his place of employment is not compensable unless the journey itself is a part of the employment." The Court explained that the rationale for this rule is that "the employment relationship is generally considered to commence when the employee reaches the employer's premises and to terminate when he leaves them."

The doctrine applies with particular force where, as here, the employee is driving a personal vehicle, is not reimbursed for travel, and has no business-related purpose for the trip. In *Beloit v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 63 Wis. 2d 189, 193, 216 N.W.2d 213, 215 (1974), the Court held that "the mere fact that the employee is required to use a car to get to work does not convert the journey into a part of the employment."

### B. The Special Errand Exception Does Not Apply

Plaintiff appears to argue that McRae's occasional stops at headquarters to pick up supplies brings him within the "special errand" exception to the going and coming doctrine. However, the undisputed facts demonstrate that on the date of the accident, McRae was not performing any special errand.

The Commission specifically found that "there was no business-related purpose to the applicant's commute, he was simply going to work" and that "the applicant was not performing any special errand when the accident occurred." This finding is supported by substantial evidence in the record.

In *Kellner*, 62 Wis. 2d at 301, 215 N.W.2d at 23, the Court held that to come within the special errand exception, "the employee must be engaged in a special mission or errand for the employer." The mission must be "for the benefit of the employer" and "the employee must be acting in the course of his employment when performing the act." Here, McRae was simply driving to his regular job site on a normal work day. He was not transporting supplies, not heading to headquarters, and not performing any task for his employer at the time of the accident.

### C. McRae Was Not a "Traveling Employee"

Plaintiff alternatively argues that McRae qualifies as a "traveling employee" under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f). This argument fails because the statute defines a traveling employee as one whose "occupation" requires travel, not one who merely occasionally travels for work.

Section 102.03(1)(f) provides that the term "employee" includes "any person whose occupation is to be employed and to work for the employer in a course of employment in this state, but whose occupation is such that the employer may send the employee to any place in this state or elsewhere to work." This provision applies to employees like traveling salespersons, construction workers who move from job site to job site, or similar workers whose primary occupation involves travel to various locations.

McRae's occupation was that of a painter. While he worked at various job sites, his regular practice was to drive from home to the assigned location. His occupation was not "to travel"; it was to paint. He did not receive travel reimbursement except for out-of-area jobs, and his paid time did not begin until he arrived at the job site. These facts demonstrate that McRae was not a traveling employee as defined by statute.

## V. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

### A. Response to Deference Arguments

Plaintiff's reliance on great weight deference is misplaced. While the Commission deserves deference on questions within its expertise, the interpretation of statutory terms like "growing out of and incidental to employment" involves established legal principles that this Court can evaluate independently. The going and coming doctrine is a well-settled principle of workers' compensation law, not a technical regulatory matter within the Commission's special competence.

Moreover, as this Court held in *Mielke*, 103 Wis. 2d at 127, 307 N.W.2d at 261, "the conclusion to be drawn from the facts is a question of law" that this Court reviews de novo. The question of whether McRae's injury arose out of and was incidental to his employment is such a question of law.

### B. The Commission's Decision Is Correct

The cases cited by Plaintiff do not support reversal. In *State v. Wisconsin Employment Relations Commission*, 102 Wis. 2d 137, 306 N.W.2d 67 (1981), the Court gave deference to the agency's interpretation of a collective bargaining agreement—a matter clearly within the agency's expertise. In *Kankakee County v. ILHR Department*, 108 Wis. 2d 652, 324 N.W.2d 217 (1982), the Court deferred to the agency's interpretation of a regulatory matter involving building permits. Neither case involves the application of the going and coming doctrine to a simple commute case.

In *Johnson v. Department of Revenue*, 2006 WI 104, 294 Wis. 2d 205, 717 N.W.2d 495, the Court applied deference to the agency's interpretation of tax statutes—a highly technical area. Here, by contrast, the question is a straightforward application of established workers' compensation principles to undisputed facts.

### C. The Commission Is Entitled to Affirmation

The Commission's decision is supported by substantial evidence and correctly applies the law. McRae was injured during his ordinary commute to work. He was driving his personal vehicle, was not reimbursed for the trip, and had no business-related purpose. He was not performing a special errand, was not a traveling employee, and his paid time had not yet begun. Under these circumstances, the injury did not occur while McRae was performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment.

McRae's occupation was that of a painter. While he worked at different job sites, his primary duty was to paint—not to travel. His paid time did not begin until he arrived at the job site, and he received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area. These facts demonstrate that travel was not an essential function of his employment; rather, it was merely a necessary precursor to performing his actual work.

The Wisconsin Supreme Court addressed this distinction in **Beloit**, 63 Wis. 2d 189, where the Court held that an employee who must travel to different work locations does not automatically become a traveling employee. The Court emphasized that the statute contemplates employees whose primary job function involves travel—such as sales representatives, delivery drivers, or consultants—not employees who simply work at various fixed locations.

### IV. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff correctly notes that great weight deference applies to the Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03. These facts distinguish McRae's situation from cases where courts have found the commute to be part of the employment—such as when an employer reimburses an employee for travel expenses or when an employee begins paid time at the moment of departure.

The plaintiff argues that extending coverage to McRae is necessary to effectuate the remedial purpose of the Workers' Compensation Act. However, this argument overlooks the fact that the Act does not provide coverage for all injuries that occur during a employee's workday. The "going and coming" doctrine is a well-established limitation on coverage that reflects the legislature's intent to balance the interests of employers and employees. As the Court stated in **Kellner**, 62 Wis. 2d at 300, "the course of employment" does not encompass the journey to and from work unless that journey is undertaken for a business purpose.

**FINAL RULING**

**McRae v. Wisconsin Labor and Industry Review Commission**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final ruling based on the closing arguments submitted by counsel for the plaintiff and counsel for the defense. The Court has reviewed the record, the applicable law, and the arguments presented regarding the standard of review and the application of the "going and coming" doctrine to the facts of this case. For the reasons set forth below, the decision of the Wisconsin Labor and Industry Review Commission (the "Commission") is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The threshold issue is the appropriate standard of review to be applied to the Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03. Both parties correctly assert that the Commission's interpretation is entitled to great weight deference. Under the principles established in *Johnson v. Department of Revenue*, 2006 WI 104, 294 Wis. 2d 205, 717 N.W.2d 495, great weight deference is warranted when: (1) the agency possesses expertise in the subject matter; (2) the statute in question is ambiguous; and (3) the agency’s interpretation is reasonable.

The Commission possesses specialized knowledge in administering the Wisconsin Workers' Compensation Act. As noted in *State v. Wisconsin Employment Relations Commission*, 102 Wis. 2d 137, 306 N.W.2d 67 (1981), the interpretation of a statute by an agency charged with its administration is entitled to great weight due to its specialized knowledge. The phrase "services growing out of and incidental to employment" is inherently ambiguous regarding travel scenarios, necessitating the agency's expertise. Furthermore, as stated in *Mielke v. Department of Industry, Labor & Employment Relations*, 103 Wis. 2d 117, 307 N.W.2d 256 (Ct. App. 1981), even while reviewing legal questions de novo, the court will give great weight to the commission's interpretation of the statutes it administers. The Court finds that all three prongs of the *Johnson* test are satisfied.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Going and Coming" Doctrine**

The central legal question is whether McRae's injury arose out of and was incidental to his employment. The Commission determined that the "going and coming" doctrine barred recovery. This doctrine provides that injuries sustained during a regular commute to and from work are generally not compensable. In *Kellner v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 62 Wis. 2d 292, 215 N.W.2d 18 (1974), the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that "an injury occurring in the course of the employee's journey to or from his place of employment is not compensable unless the journey itself is a part of the employment."

The undisputed facts demonstrate that McRae was driving from his personal residence in Twin Lakes, Wisconsin, to his assigned job site. He was not performing any special errand for the employer at the time of the accident, nor was he picking up supplies. As the Commission found, there was no business-related purpose to the commute; he was simply going to work. Under *Beloit v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 63 Wis. 2d 189, 216 N.W.2d 213 (1974), the mere fact that an employee is required to use a car to get to work does not convert the journey into a part of the employment. The record establishes that McRae received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area and his paid time did not begin until he arrived at the job site. These facts align with the holding in *Beloit* and confirm that the injury falls squarely within the "going and coming" exception.

**B. Special Errand and Traveling Employee Status**

The Appellant has not successfully demonstrated that an exception applies. The "special errand" exception requires the employee to be engaged in a special mission or errand for the employer. *Kellner*, 62 Wis. 2d at 301. The record is clear that on the date of the accident, McRae was not performing a special errand.

Furthermore, McRae does not qualify as a "traveling employee" under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f). That statute applies to persons whose occupation *requires* travel to various places. McRae's occupation was that of a painter. While he worked at different job sites, his primary duty was to paint, not to travel. His paid time did not begin until he arrived at the job site, and he received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area. These facts support the Commission's conclusion that he was not a traveling employee.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03 is reasonable and entitled to great weight deference under *Johnson*. The decision is supported by substantial evidence in the record and correctly applies established legal principles regarding the "going and coming" doctrine as articulated in *Kellner* and *Beloit*. Both the prosecution and the defense, in their respective closing arguments, support the conclusion that the Commission's application of the law to the undisputed facts was correct. Specifically, the prosecution's argument that the Commission's expertise and the statutory ambiguity warrant deference, combined with the defense's argument that the undisputed facts establish a regular commute, leaves no basis for reversal.

**Therefore, IT IS ORDERED that the decision of the Wisconsin Labor and Industry Review Commission be affirmed.**
