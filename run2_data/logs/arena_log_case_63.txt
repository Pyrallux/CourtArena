Your Honors, the Director’s denial of Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim rests on a critical legal misapplication. The Director incorrectly invoked *Flor v. Holguin* to evaluate whether the aggravation of Van Ness’s asthma was proximately caused by his employment. But as this Court has unequivocally held, *Flor v. Holguin* applies solely to injuries by disease that "result from the nature of the employment"—not to determinations of proximate cause for aggravation of pre-existing conditions. *Flor v. Holguin*, 74 Haw. 222, 232, 843 P.2d 662, 668 (1992). Here, Van Ness’s asthma was a pre-existing condition aggravated by work-related exposures. For such aggravation claims, the unitary test governs—not *Flor*. *Kaua’i County v. State*, 83 Haw. 317, 323, 926 P.2d 776, 782 (1996) (holding that "arising out of" for all injuries—including disease aggravations—requires only that employment be a proximate cause of the injury, not that the hazard be unique to the workplace).  

The Director’s error is fundamental. By misapplying *Flor*, the Director imposed a requirement that vog exposure must be "unique to the workplace" to establish compensability. But under the unitary test, that is irrelevant. What matters is whether Van Ness’s employment proximately caused the aggravation. The record is unequivocal: Van Ness worked in non-air-conditioned buildings with open louvered windows, where volcanic vog and ash accumulated, requiring him to clean dust-clogged computer filters daily. Medical experts confirmed this exposure directly worsened his condition. Dr. Mathison’s report states that Van Ness’s symptoms "exacerbated" during "a 40-day spell of rainy weather with vog (moisture, pollutants including volcanic smoke/ash) exposure," and that "exposures to volcanic smoke (high in sulfur dioxide) and ash" are a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients." Dr. Mathison further noted that Van Ness’s respiratory symptoms "essentially resolved" when he was away from Maui—such as during his hospitalization in Philadelphia—and "worsened" upon returning to Maui. This evidence satisfies the unitary test: employment was a proximate cause of the aggravation, even if vog existed island-wide.  

The Director’s focus on "island-wide" vog ignores controlling law. *Kaua’i County* explicitly rejects the notion that a hazard must be "unique to the workplace" to establish proximate cause. Instead, the question is whether employment conditions contributed to the injury. Here, Van Ness’s job required him to work in environments where vog exposure was intensified by the school’s ventilation practices—conditions his medical providers identified as directly triggering his symptoms. By conflating *Flor*’s "resulting from the nature of employment" standard with proximate cause, the Director misapplied the law and mischaracterized the evidence.  

For these reasons, this Court must reverse the Director’s decision. The unitary test—properly applied—confirms that Van Ness’s employment proximately caused the aggravation of his asthma. Any other outcome would contravene Hawaii’s workers’ compensation scheme, which is designed to protect employees when work conditions exacerbate pre-existing health issues. Thank you.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Summary of Argument

The Defense respectfully submits that the Director's denial of Van Ness's workers' compensation claim should be affirmed. The Director properly applied the governing legal standards and reached a conclusion supported by substantial evidence in the record. The prosecution's argument mischaracterizes both the Director's reasoning and the applicable law, and ignores the critical distinction between exposure to a general environmental hazard present across an entire island versus exposure attributable to specific workplace conditions.

The prosecution asks this Court to impose liability on the basis that vog existed on Maui and that Van Ness worked on Maui. This position, if accepted, would transform workers' compensation into a general health insurance program covering any aggravation of a pre-existing condition that occurs anywhere an employee happens to work. Such an expansion of compensability finds no support in Hawaii law.

For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the Director's decision.

---

## I. The Director's Decision Is Entitled to Substantial Deference

The standard of review for workers' compensation decisions in Hawaii requires this Court to affirm the Director's findings of fact if supported by substantial evidence in the record. The Director's determination that Van Ness's exposure to vog was island-wide rather than workplace-specific is a factual finding entitled to deference. The prosecution's argument, in essence, asks this Court to reweigh the evidence—a function that lies with the Director, not with this appellate tribunal.

The prosecution argues that the Director misapplied *Flor v. Holguin*. However, even assuming *arguendo* that some legal error occurred, the prosecution cannot demonstrate prejudice because the record fully supports the Director's ultimate conclusion that Van Ness failed to establish that his employment was a proximate cause of his injury. The result would be the same under any correct application of the law.

---

## II. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Record Evidence

The prosecution asserts that Van Ness's "job required him to work in environments where vog exposure was intensified by the school's ventilation practices." This characterization finds insufficient support in the record.

The evidence established that while Van Ness worked in non-air-conditioned buildings with open windows, the vog itself was not created by or concentrated at the school. Vice Principal Dennis's letter confirming the presence of vog on Maui specifically stated that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, than at the school. Van Ness himself testified that he "spent little time outside, other than when he was at work" and that he lived in an air-conditioned home with HEPA filters. The record demonstrates that Van Ness was exposed to vog both at work and at home, with the exposure at home being characterized as "even worse" than at school.

The prosecution cites Dr. Mathison's report for the proposition that vog exposure at work exacerbated Van Ness's condition. However, Dr. Mathison's report does not establish that exposure at Lahainaluna was quantitatively or qualitatively different from exposure elsewhere on Maui. Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness's symptoms "exacerbated" during a period of vog exposure "from about November 2005 and continuing into the spring of 2006," but this timeline corresponds precisely to the period when vog was present across the entire island of Maui, as documented in the record. The prosecution cannot selectively attribute this island-wide phenomenon to the workplace.

The prosecution also relies on Dr. Mathison's observation that Van Ness's symptoms "essentially resolved" while in Philadelphia and "worsened" upon returning to Maui. This evidence demonstrates only that removal from Maui improved Van Ness's condition—it does not establish that the workplace was the source of exposure. If anything, this evidence supports the Director's finding that Van Ness's exposure was island-wide.

---

## III. The Prosecution's Legal Arguments Fail on Their Merits

### A. The Prosecution Incorrectly Conflates the "Arising Out Of" Test with Proximate Cause

The prosecution argues that under *Kauai County v. State*, 83 Haw. 317, 323, 926 P.2d 776, 782 (1996), "arising out of" employment requires only that employment be "a" proximate cause, not "the" proximate cause. This argument misreads *Kauai County*.

In *Kauai County*, the employee was a pesticide applicator who was exposed to pesticides while performing his job duties in an orchard. The hazard—exposure to pesticides—was directly related to the nature of his employment. The court held that the employment need not be the sole cause, but it must be a causative factor. The prosecution omits the critical qualifier in *Kauai County*: employment must be "a" proximate cause, meaning the employment must contribute to the injury in a meaningful way.

Here, by contrast, the hazard—vog—was not related to the nature of Van Ness's employment as a technology coordinator. Van Ness was not hired to work with vog or to be exposed to volcanic emissions. His job duties involved maintaining computers and technical equipment. The vog was an ambient environmental condition present across the entire island, affecting all residents regardless of their employment.

*Kauai County* is distinguishable because the pesticide exposure there was directly tied to the employee's work tasks. Van Ness's exposure to vog was not connected to his job duties in any meaningful way—the vog happened to be present on Maui during the relevant time period, and Van Ness happened to work on Maui. This does not establish the required nexus between employment and injury.

### B. The Prosecution's Reliance on Workplace-Intensified Exposure Is Unsupported

The prosecution argues that Van Ness's "job required him to work in environments where vog exposure was intensified by the school's ventilation practices." This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the record does not support a finding that vog exposure was "intensified" at the school. The evidence established that the school had louvered windows and fans to circulate air, but there is no evidence that these ventilation practices concentrated vog within the buildings. To the contrary, open windows and circulating fans would presumably allow vog to disperse, not accumulate.

Second, Van Ness testified that he spent less than five percent of his time in his air-conditioned office and worked "all over campus" in buildings with louvered windows. However, he also testified that his home was air-conditioned with HEPA filters, and that he spent most of his non-working hours at home. The record does not establish that his workplace exposure exceeded his home exposure.

Third, and most importantly, even if the school environment marginally increased vog exposure above home levels, this does not establish that employment was a proximate cause of the injury. The question under *Kauai County* is whether employment contributed to the injury in a meaningful way—not whether employment contributed marginally more than some alternative location. Van Ness's employment placed him on Maui, but so did his choice to live on Maui. The Director reasonably concluded that this connection was insufficient to establish compensability.

---

## IV. The Director Properly Applied the Governing Legal Standards

The prosecution argues that the Director erred by requiring that vog exposure be "unique to the workplace." However, the prosecution overstates the Director's position. The Director found that "it was claimant's exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition," and that there was "insufficient evidence" to establish that exposure at Lahainaluna "was so overwhelming" that the exposure "occurred at this place of employment only."

This reasoning is consistent with the requirement that employment be a proximate cause of the injury. The Director did not demand that vog be unique to the workplace; rather, the Director found that the evidence failed to establish that Van Ness's employment was a meaningful contributing cause of his injury, as opposed to his general presence on Maui.

The prosecution argues that the "unitary test" only requires that employment be "a" proximate cause. Even accepting this formulation, the prosecution cannot demonstrate that employment was "a" proximate cause rather than merely the occasion for the injury. Van Ness's injury resulted from his presence on Maui during a period of elevated vog—not from any condition of his employment specifically. His job as a technology coordinator did not require exposure to vog; the exposure occurred because he lived and worked on an island with active volcanic emissions.

---

## V. Even Assuming Error, Reversal Is Not Warranted

Assuming *arguendo* that the Director applied an incorrect legal standard, reversal would not be warranted because the record does not support a finding in Van Ness's favor under any correct formulation of the governing law.

The prosecution bears the burden of establishing that Van Ness's employment arose out of his employment. The evidence demonstrates only that Van Ness's condition worsened during a period when vog was present across Maui. This evidence is insufficient to establish that employment was a proximate cause of the injury.

Dr. Arora's report, which the Director credited, specifically noted that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." This opinion supports the Director's conclusion that the exposure was not workplace-related.

The prosecution cites Dr. Mathison's recommendation that Van Ness "not be placed at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution." However, this opinion addresses future accommodations under the Americans with Disabilities Act—it does not establish that Van Ness's employment caused his injury. Dr. Mathison's recommendation applies to any area with volcanic exposure, not specifically to Van Ness's workplace at Lahainaluna.

---

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the Director's denial of Van Ness's workers' compensation claim. The Director's decision is supported by substantial evidence, and the prosecution has failed to demonstrate that reversal is warranted under applicable legal standards.

The prosecution asks this Court to expand workers' compensation liability to cover any aggravation of a pre-existing condition that occurs because an employee happens to work in a location where a general environmental hazard exists. This expansion finds no support in Hawaii law and would contravene the fundamental principle that workers' compensation covers injuries arising out of employment, not injuries arising out of general geographic presence.

The Director's decision should be affirmed.

The court finds that the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) erred in denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. Dr. Mathison's opinion, properly understood, supports the Defense's position: Van Ness's risk derived from his residence on Maui, not from his specific employment at Lahainaluna. The Court erred in interpreting Dr. Mathison's recommendation as acknowledgment that "the specific workplace conditions (lack of filtration) were the mechanism of the injury." To the contrary, Dr. Mathison's recommendation applies to any assignment on Maui, not to Lahainaluna specifically.

Similarly, Dr. Tom's clinical notes referenced "vog contributing" and "vog on Maui"—not "vog at Lahainaluna." These notes do not establish that Van Ness's workplace exposure was a substantial factor in his injury. At most, they establish that vog on Maui generally contributed to his symptoms.

The Court dismissed Dr. Arora's opinion as irrelevant because it addressed the *Flor* "unique risk" inquiry. However, Dr. Arora's observation that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely" is relevant to the *Kaua'i County* analysis. It establishes that Van Ness's employment did not expose him to a risk greater than that faced by other Maui residents who worked in non-air-conditioned environments. The Court erred in disregarding this evidence.

## III. THE COURT'S RULING OPENS THE DOOR TO EXPANSIVE LIABILITY THAT EXCEEDS THE SCOPE OF WORKERS' COMPENSATION

The Court dismissed the Defense's policy argument as "unavailing." We respectfully disagree. The Court's ruling effectively holds that any employee with a pre-existing condition aggravated by an environmental hazard present on an island-wide basis may recover workers' compensation if their workplace lacks filtration—even if the employee's home is in an area where the hazard is "even worse."

This result contradicts the fundamental principle that workers' compensation covers injuries "arising out of" employment, not injuries that would have occurred regardless of employment. *Kaua'i County*, 83 Haw. at 323, 926 P.2d at 782. If Van Ness's home in Kihei was exposed to "even worse" vog than Lahainaluna, then his employment was not a substantial factor in his injury—it was merely the occasion on which his pre-existing condition was exacerbated by an environmental hazard that affected all Maui residents.

The Defense acknowledges that *Kaua'i County* allows compensation when employment aggravates a pre-existing condition. However, the standard is "substantial factor," not "possible factor" or "contributing factor." The evidence here, at best, establishes that vog on Maui generally contributed to Van Ness's symptoms. It does not establish that his specific employment at Lahainaluna was a substantial factor.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse its preliminary ruling and affirm the Director's decision denying Van Ness's workers' compensation claim. The evidence does not establish that Van Ness's employment was a substantial factor in aggravating his pre-existing asthma. The vog was an island-wide phenomenon, and Van Ness's home in Kihei was in an area where vog was "even worse" than at Lahainaluna. The medical evidence does not support a workplace-specific causal link. To the contrary, Dr. Mathison's recommendation applies to Maui generally, not to Lahainaluna specifically.

Affirming the Director's denial would not deprive Van Ness of all recourse. He may pursue other remedies for his medical condition. However, workers' compensation is not the appropriate vehicle for his claim, as his injury did not arise out of his employment in the manner required by law.

**FINAL RULING**

The court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. After careful consideration of the record, the applicable legal standards, and the arguments presented, the court affirms its preliminary ruling. The Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) erred in denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Defense’s attempts to uphold the denial under the correct legal standard are unpersuasive.

**I. THE LEGAL STANDARD IS GOVERNED BY *KAUA’I COUNTY*, NOT *FLOR***

The Defense argues that the Director’s denial should stand regardless of the legal standard applied. However, the Director’s decision explicitly relied on the "unique risk" standard articulated in *Flor v. Holguin*, 74 Haw. 222, 843 P.2d 662 (1992), finding no nexus because vog was present island-wide. This was a clear error of law. As established in the preliminary ruling and reiterated by the Prosecution, *Flor* applies to injuries by disease resulting from the nature of employment, not to the aggravation of a pre-existing condition. The correct standard for determining compensability in this case is the "proximate cause" test set forth in *Kaua’i County v. State*, 83 Haw. 317, 926 P.2d 776 (1996). Under *Kaua’i County*, the employment must be a "substantial factor in bringing about the injury," and the employment must expose the worker to a risk greater than that faced by the general public. 83 Haw. at 323, 926 P.2d at 782.

**II. THE DEFENSE’S "ISLAND-WIDE" ARGUMENT FAILS UNDER THE "SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR" TEST**

The Defense contends that Van Ness’s employment was not a substantial factor in his injury because vog was an "island-wide phenomenon" and, according to Vice Principal Dennis, was "even worse" in Kihei where Van Ness lived. This argument misconstrues the "substantial factor" inquiry by focusing solely on the geographic presence of the hazard while ignoring the qualitative nature of the exposure.

The record establishes a critical distinction between Van Ness’s home environment and his work environment. While vog may have been more concentrated in Kihei generally, Van Ness’s specific exposure at home was significantly mitigated. He lived in an air-conditioned home equipped with HEPA filters and used a car with a HEPA filter. In contrast, his workplace at Lahainaluna was characterized by non-air-conditioned buildings with louvered windows and fans circulating unfiltered air and dust. Van Ness testified that he spent 95% of his workday in these unfiltered environments.

The Defense’s reliance on Vice Principal Dennis’s statement regarding the severity of vog in Kihei ignores the mitigating measures Van Ness employed at home. The "risk" faced by the general public includes the ability to control one's immediate environment. The general public in Maui had the option to retreat to air-conditioned or filtered spaces. Van Ness’s employment, however, *required* him to remain in an unfiltered, dusty environment for seven to eight hours a day. This mandatory, unfiltered exposure, combined with the physical exertion of walking uphill and maintaining equipment, constituted a risk greater than that faced by the general public. Therefore, the employment was a substantial factor in aggravating his condition.

**III. THE MEDICAL EVIDENCE SUPPORTS A WORKPLACE-SPECIFIC AGGRAVATION**

The Defense argues that the medical evidence, particularly Dr. Mathison’s report, supports only a geographic conclusion (i.e., avoid Maui) rather than a workplace-specific one. We disagree. Dr. Mathison diagnosed Van Ness with "[m]ild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution." He noted that Van Ness’s symptoms "essentially resolved" when he was in Philadelphia and returned upon his arrival in Maui.

Crucially, Dr. Mathison recommended that Van Ness’s employer "not place him at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution." This recommendation directly implicates the employment context. It acknowledges that the *nature* of the assignment—specifically, the requirement to work in areas with high volcanic exposure without filtration—posed a danger to Van Ness. The Defense’s interpretation that this advice applies solely to his residence on Maui is inconsistent with the fact that Dr. Mathison was addressing the employer and the work-related claim.

Furthermore, Dr. Tom’s clinical notes explicitly referenced "vog contributing" to Van Ness’s symptoms during the period he was working at Lahainaluna. The temporal correlation between the severe vog period (late 2005 to early 2006), Van Ness’s increased physical symptoms at work, and the exhaustion of his medication supply supports the conclusion that the workplace conditions were a substantial factor in the aggravation of his asthma.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Defense has not demonstrated that the evidence is insufficient to support compensability under the *Kaua’i County* standard. The record shows that Van Ness’s employment exposed him to unfiltered volcanic pollutants and dust in a manner distinct from his mitigated home environment. This exposure was a substantial factor in aggravating his pre-existing asthma. The Director’s decision was based on an error of law and is not supported by the evidence under the correct legal standard.

Accordingly, the Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim is REVERSED. The case is REMANDED to the Director for a determination of benefits consistent with this ruling.
