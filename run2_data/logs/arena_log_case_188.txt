Your Honor, today we ask this Court to reverse the Director’s denial of Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim because the Director fundamentally misapplied the law governing compensability for disease-related injuries. The controlling precedent establishes that the *Flor* test applies only to injuries by disease that "result from the nature of the employment"—meaning diseases peculiar to or inherent in the job itself. For all other diseases, including those proximately caused by employment but not peculiar to it, the unitary test governs. Here, Van Ness’s asthma aggravation is not peculiar to his employment; it is a general environmental condition exacerbated by his job duties. Yet the Director erroneously applied the *Flor* test’s uniqueness requirement, ignoring the unitary test’s proximate causation standard. This legal error demands reversal.  

As this Court recognized in *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 297, 104 P.3d 556 (2004), the "nature of employment" test is strictly limited to diseases that "result from the nature of the employment." That test asks whether the disease is "peculiar to the employment" or "inherent in the nature of the work." But for diseases not peculiar to the job—such as asthma aggravation triggered by environmental hazards like vog—the unitary test for proximate causation applies. Under the unitary test, the question is not whether the hazard is unique to the workplace, but whether the employment was a proximate cause of the injury—i.e., whether it was a substantial factor in bringing about the harm. *Flor* makes this distinction crystal clear: "Where the disease is one that is not peculiar to the employment, the unitary test for causation applies." Id. at 305, 104 P.3d at 562.  

The Director’s decision directly contravenes this principle. The Director found that "vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and concluded there was "insufficient evidence" of a work-related nexus. But under the unitary test, the widespread presence of vog is irrelevant. Vog is a general environmental hazard, not peculiar to Van Ness’s job as a technology coordinator. Yet his employment required him to engage in strenuous physical activity in high-vog environments for extended periods: he repeatedly climbed steep, unpaved stairs between campus buildings for seven to eight hours daily, serviced computers in non-air-conditioned buildings with open windows (causing dust accumulation that necessitated frequent filter cleaning), and endured severe vog conditions that impaired visibility and respiratory function. Medical evidence confirms this exposure was a proximate cause of his injury. Dr. Mathison, after reviewing Van Ness’s full medical history, diagnosed "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and explicitly warned that "risk for serious and potentially fatal asthma exacerbation with exposure to volcanic products" exists. Van Ness’s symptoms worsened during the vog season on Maui, improved after transfer to Oahu, and required constant medication refills—evidence directly linking his job duties to the aggravation of his condition.  

The Director’s error lies in conflating "uniqueness to the workplace" with proximate causation. Under the unitary test, a hazard need not be exclusive to employment to be work-related; it need only be a substantial contributing factor. For instance, in *Flor*, the court emphasized that even for general diseases, employment can be a proximate cause if it exposes the worker to the hazard in a manner that substantially contributes to the injury. Here, Van Ness’s job duties—not his personal lifestyle—subjected him to prolonged, uncontrolled vog exposure during work hours. His employer’s failure to mitigate these conditions—such as by providing air-conditioned spaces or limiting outdoor tasks during high-vog days—directly contributed to his injury. To hold otherwise would immunize employers from liability whenever a general hazard exists in the community, undermining the very purpose of workers’ compensation law.  

Your Honor, this case is not about whether vog is everywhere on Maui. It is about whether Van Ness’s employment proximately caused his injury. The Director applied the wrong legal standard, and the record is replete with evidence that his job duties were a substantial factor in aggravating his asthma. We respectfully ask this Court to reverse the Director’s decision and remand for application of the correct unitary test for proximate causation. Thank you.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Appeal from the Director's Decision Denying Workers' Compensation Benefits

---

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits that the Director's decision denying Van Ness's workers' compensation claim should be affirmed. The plaintiff mischaracterizes the Director's legal analysis and ignores substantial evidence supporting the finding that Van Ness's respiratory condition was not proximately caused by his employment. ## I. THE DIRECTOR APPLIED THE CORRECT LEGAL STANDARD

The plaintiff contends that the Director erroneously applied the *Flor* test when the unitary test for proximate causation should have governed. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the Director's analysis and the circumstances under which each test applies.

In *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 297, 104 P.3d 556 (2004), the Hawaii Supreme Court articulated the analytical framework for determining compensability of disease-related injuries under the Workers' Compensation Act. The Court explained that where a disease "results from the nature of the employment"—meaning it is peculiar to or inherent in the work performed—the "nature of employment" test applies. *Flor*, 106 Haw. at 305, 104 P.3d at 562. Under this test, the inquiry focuses on whether the employment itself created the risk that resulted in the disease.

However, the *Flor* Court explicitly recognized that the nature of employment test applies with particular force "where the disease is one that is peculiar to the employment." *Id.* at 306, 104 P.3d at 563. The Court emphasized that for diseases that are not peculiar to the employment, the unitary test for proximate causation applies—but this does not mean the unitary test automatically governs every case involving a non-peculiar disease. Rather, the threshold question remains whether the employment was a substantial factor in causing the injury.

The Director's analysis properly addressed both prongs. The Director found that Van Ness's exposure to vog "aggravated his long standing asthma condition," but that "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment." This finding goes to the heart of the compensability inquiry: whether the employment relationship created a risk distinct from the general risks all residents of Maui face.

The Director did not, as the plaintiff suggests, apply the *Flor* test in error. Rather, the Director correctly recognized that for a disease condition not peculiar to the employment, the question becomes whether the particular conditions of employment created a risk sufficient to establish a nexus. The Director's finding that vog was pervasive throughout the island—affecting all residents, not merely workers at Lahainaluna—is legally relevant to this inquiry and supports the denial of benefits.

---

## II. SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE SUPPORTS THE DIRECTOR'S FINDING OF NO NEXUS

The plaintiff's argument rests on the premise that Van Ness's employment was a proximate cause of his respiratory deterioration. However, the record contains substantial evidence supporting the Director's contrary finding.

### A. Vog Was a General Environmental Hazard, Not a Workplace-Specific Risk

The record establishes that vog was not confined to Lahainaluna High School or even to the school district. The plaintiff himself testified that there was "a significant amount of vog on Maui from late October 2005 through April 2006." Van Ness testified that on severe vog days, he was unable to see the administrative building "halfway down the campus" from the library. Vice Principal Joanne Dennis confirmed in her October 19, 2007 letter that "we experienced some unusually severe vog-polluted days during the weeks preceding that winter break" and that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived.

This evidence establishes that vog was a general environmental condition affecting all residents of Maui, not a hazard specific to the workplace. As the Director found, "it was claimant's exposure to vog on the island of Maui that aggravated his asthma condition," and there was "insufficient evidence" that Van Ness's exposure at Lahainaluna School was "so overwhelming" that his exposure occurred at his place of employment only.

The plaintiff argues that the widespread presence of vog is "irrelevant" under the unitary test. This is incorrect. The geographic scope of a hazard bears directly on whether employment was a substantial factor in causing the injury. If a hazard affects all persons in a community regardless of their employment, a claimant must demonstrate that employment exposed them to the hazard in a manner or degree distinct from the general population. The Director's finding that Van Ness failed to make this showing is supported by the evidence.

### B. Van Ness's Employment Duties Did Not Create Distinct Exposure

The plaintiff emphasizes Van Ness's job duties—climbing stairs, servicing computers in non-air-conditioned buildings, cleaning filters—as creating increased exposure to vog. However, the plaintiff overstates the record.

Van Ness testified that his office was located in the school library at the highest point of campus, and that he was required to travel between buildings. He described the campus terrain as steep, with stairs going up the side of the hill. However, Van Ness also testified that he was "not much of an outdoors person" and "there really wasn't much to do there." He stated that his experience at Lahainaluna required "a significant amount of physical activity outside," but this physical activity was a function of the campus geography, not a function of vog exposure specifically.

The evidence does not establish that Van Ness's job duties exposed him to vog to a greater degree than other residents of Maui. The vog was present throughout the island, and Van Ness lived in Kihei, where Vice Principal Dennis noted "the vog was even worse." The Director could reasonably find that Van Ness's exposure was general environmental exposure, not employment-specific exposure.

### C. The Medical Evidence Does Not Establish Work-Related Causation

The plaintiff relies heavily on Dr. Mathison's report, which diagnosed Van Ness with "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution." However, Dr. Mathison's report does not establish that Van Ness's employment was a proximate cause of his injury.

Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness's "allergic diathesis has largely been controlled with avoidances and the several-year course of immunotherapy during the early 90s," but that he "has had significant asthma exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke (high in sulfur dioxide) and ash including that carried by meteorological conditions holding the pollutant in moist air over Maui." Dr. Mathison did not distinguish between Van Ness's exposure at work and his exposure at home. His report refers generally to exposure "over Maui," not exposure at Lahainaluna specifically.

Dr. Mathison's conclusion that Van Ness "can be considered to be disabled by virtue of his asthmatic tendency and susceptibility to the volcanic pollutant effects" and that "it behooves his employer not to place him at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution" is general in nature. It does not establish that Van Ness's employment at Lahainaluna caused his injury; it merely confirms that volcanic pollution affects asthmatics.

The plaintiff argues that Van Ness's symptoms "worsened during the vog season on Maui, improved after transfer to Oahu." This evidence cuts both ways. While it shows that vog affected Van Ness, it also shows that his improvement came from leaving Maui entirely—not from any change in his employment conditions. If employment were the cause, one would expect improvement upon modification of job duties, not merely upon changing residence.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations’ decision denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. Claimant alleges that his employment with the Department of Education (DOE) at Lahainaluna High School exacerbated his asthma and bronchitis due to exposure to volcanic smog (vog) and the physical demands of the job. The Director denied the claim, finding that while vog aggravated Claimant’s condition, the vog was present island-wide and not solely at the workplace, thus failing to establish a nexus between the employment and the injury. The parties dispute whether the Director applied the correct legal standard in reaching this conclusion.

**II. APPLICABLE LEGAL STANDARD**

The compensability of disease-related injuries in Hawaii is governed by the framework established in *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 297, 104 P.3d 556 (2004). The court in *Flor* distinguished between two tests for determining whether an injury "arises out of and in the course of employment."

First, the "nature of employment" test applies where a disease "results from the nature of the employment," meaning the disease is "peculiar to the employment" or "inherent in the nature of the work." *Flor*, 106 Haw. at 305, 104 P.3d at 562.

Second, for diseases that are not peculiar to the employment, the "unitary test" for proximate causation applies. Under this test, the inquiry is whether the employment was a "substantial factor" in bringing about the injury. *Id.* at 306, 104 P.3d at 563. The *Flor* court explicitly stated: "Where the disease is one that is not peculiar to the employment, the unitary test for causation applies." *Id.* at 305, 104 P.3d at 562.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The central issue on appeal is whether the Director erred by applying the "nature of employment" test's requirement of peculiarity to a condition that is conceded to be a general environmental hazard, rather than applying the unitary test's proximate causation standard.

The Director’s decision states: "The vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment." The Director further concluded there was "insufficient evidence" to support Claimant’s assertion that his exposure "occurred at this place of employment only." This reasoning indicates that the Director denied the claim because the hazard (vog) was not unique to the workplace.

The Prosecution correctly argues that this constitutes legal error. Under *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 297, 104 P.3d 556 (2004), the requirement that a hazard be unique to the workplace applies only to the "nature of employment" test. Because asthma aggravated by vog is not a disease peculiar to the employment of a technology coordinator, the unitary test governs. Under the unitary test, the relevant inquiry is not whether the vog existed outside the workplace, but whether the employment conditions were a "substantial factor" in causing the injury.

The record indicates that Claimant was required to perform strenuous physical labor, including repeatedly climbing steep stairs and servicing computers in non-air-conditioned, dusty environments for seven to eight hours a day. Medical evidence from Dr. Mathison links these exposures to a "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms." The Director’s finding that vog was island-wide does not, as a matter of law, preclude a finding that the specific demands of Claimant’s employment were a substantial factor in aggravating his condition. By focusing on the geographic ubiquity of the vog rather than the causal contribution of the employment duties, the Director improperly imported the "peculiarity" requirement of the *Flor* test into a unitary test analysis.

The Defense argues that the Director’s finding is supported by substantial evidence because Claimant failed to show his exposure was distinct from that of the general public. However, the Defense conflates the "substantial factor" standard with a requirement of "distinct exposure." While the "distinct exposure" argument may be relevant to the weight of the evidence, the Director’s explicit reliance on the fact that vog was not "only at claimant's place of employment" demonstrates an application of the incorrect legal standard. The unitary test does not require the hazard to be present only at work; it requires the work to be a substantial cause of the harm.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the opening arguments and the record provided, the Prosecution has demonstrated that the Director likely misapplied the law by requiring the injury to be peculiar to the workplace. The Director’s conclusion that the lack of a unique workplace hazard precluded compensation contradicts the mandate of *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 297, 104 P.3d 556 (2004), that the unitary test for proximate causation applies to general diseases.

**THEREFORE**, the Court preliminarily rules that the Director’s decision must be reversed and the case remanded for a determination of whether Claimant’s employment was a substantial factor in aggravating his respiratory condition under the correct unitary test standard.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations’ decision denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. Claimant alleges that his employment with the Department of Education (DOE) at Lahainaluna High School exacerbated his asthma and bronchitis due to exposure to volcanic smog (vog) and the physical demands of the job. The Director denied the claim, finding that while vog aggravated Claimant’s condition, the vog was present island-wide and not solely at the workplace, thus failing to establish a nexus between the employment and the injury. The parties dispute whether the Director applied the correct legal standard in reaching this conclusion.

**II. APPLICABLE LEGAL STANDARD**

The compensability of disease-related injuries in Hawaii is governed by the framework established in *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 297, 104 P.3d 556 (2004). The court in *Flor* distinguished between two tests for determining whether an injury "arises out of and in the course of employment."

First, the "nature of employment" test applies where a disease "results from the nature of the employment," meaning the disease is "peculiar to the employment" or "inherent in the nature of the work." *Flor*, 106 Haw. at 305, 104 P.3d at 562.

Second, for diseases that are not peculiar to the employment, the "unitary test" for proximate causation applies. Under this test, the inquiry is whether the employment was a "substantial factor" in bringing about the injury. *Id.* at 306, 104 P.3d at 563. The *Flor* court explicitly stated: "Where the disease is one that is not peculiar to the employment, the unitary test for causation applies." *Id.* at 305, 104 P.3d at 562.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The central issue on appeal is whether the Director erred by applying the "nature of employment" test's requirement of peculiarity to a condition that is conceded to be a general environmental hazard, rather than applying the unitary test's proximate causation standard.

**A. The Director Misapplied the Law by Requiring "Uniqueness" Under the Unitary Test**

The Defense argues that the Director’s finding that vog was present "on the entire island of Maui" was a factual finding supported by substantial evidence, and that the Director did not require "uniqueness." The Court disagrees. The Director’s decision explicitly states: "there was insufficient evidence to support Van Ness’s claim that his exposure to vog at only Lahainaluna School was so overwhelming that [Van Ness’s] exposure to vog occurred at this place of employment only."

By framing the insufficiency of evidence in terms of whether exposure occurred "at this place of employment only," the Director imposed a requirement of exclusivity. Under *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 297, 104 P.3d 556 (2004), such a requirement is applicable only to the "nature of employment" test. Because asthma aggravated by vog is not a disease peculiar to the employment of a technology coordinator, the unitary test governs. Under the unitary test, the relevant inquiry is not whether the vog existed outside the workplace, but whether the employment conditions were a "substantial factor" in causing the injury. *Flor*, 106 Haw. at 306, 104 P.3d at 563.

The Defense’s contention that this language constitutes a mere factual determination ignores the legal import of the phrase "at this place of employment only." This standard sets the bar too high for a claim governed by the unitary test. The Director’s conclusion that the lack of a unique workplace hazard precluded compensation contradicts the mandate of *Flor* that the unitary test focuses on proximate causation, not exclusivity.

**B. The Record Supports Remand for a Substantial Factor Inquiry**

The Defense argues that even under the unitary test, the evidence does not support compensability because vog was worse in Kihei where Claimant lived, and physical demands were similar at other schools. While these arguments may be relevant to the weight of the evidence on remand, they cannot cure the Director’s legal error.

The record indicates that Claimant was required to perform strenuous physical labor, including repeatedly climbing steep stairs and servicing computers in non-air-conditioned, dusty environments for seven to eight hours a day. Medical evidence from Dr. Mathison links these exposures to a "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms." The Director’s finding that vog was island-wide does not, as a matter of law, preclude a finding that the specific demands of Claimant’s employment were a substantial factor in aggravating his condition.

The Defense relies on *Flor* to distinguish the case, noting that the security guard in *Flor* had "close contact" with infected inmates, creating a heightened risk. However, the Defense’s argument presupposes that the "substantial factor" inquiry has already been correctly conducted and failed. Because the Director applied the incorrect legal standard—requiring exposure to occur "only" at work—the Director never properly evaluated whether the combination of physical exertion and vog exposure at work was a substantial factor in aggravating Claimant’s asthma.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the rebuttals and the record provided, the Prosecution has demonstrated that the Director misapplied the law by requiring the injury to be peculiar to the workplace. The Director’s conclusion that the lack of a unique workplace hazard precluded compensation contradicts the mandate of *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 297, 104 P.3d 556 (2004), that the unitary test for proximate causation applies to general diseases. The Defense’s arguments regarding the sufficiency of the evidence are better addressed on remand under the correct legal standard.

**THEREFORE**, the Court rules that the Director’s decision must be reversed and the case remanded for a determination of whether Claimant’s employment was a substantial factor in aggravating his respiratory condition under the correct unitary test standard.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. Claimant appeals the Director’s finding that his respiratory injury did not arise out of and in the course of his employment. The Director concluded that because the volcanic smog (vog) aggravating Claimant’s asthma was present island-wide and not unique to his workplace, no nexus existed between his employment and his injury. The parties dispute whether the Director applied the correct legal standard in reaching this conclusion.

**II. APPLICABLE LEGAL STANDARD**

The compensability of occupational diseases in Hawaii is governed by *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 297, 104 P.3d 556 (2004). The court in *Flor* established two distinct tests for determining whether an injury arises out of employment.

First, the "nature of employment" test applies where a disease is "peculiar to the employment" or inherent in the work. *Flor*, 106 Haw. at 305, 104 P.3d at 562.

Second, for diseases that are not peculiar to the employment, the "unitary test" for proximate causation applies. Under this test, the sole inquiry is whether the employment was a "substantial factor" in bringing about the injury. *Id.* at 306, 104 P.3d at 563. The *Flor* court explicitly rejected the requirement that a risk be unique to the workplace for the unitary test to apply, stating: "Where the disease is one that is not peculiar to the employment, the unitary test for causation applies." *Id.* at 305, 104 P.3d at 562.

**III. ANALYSIS**

The central issue is whether the Director erred by requiring Claimant to prove that his exposure to vog occurred "at this place of employment only," rather than evaluating whether his employment was a substantial factor in aggravating his condition.

**A. The Director Misapplied the Law by Requiring Uniqueness Under the Unitary Test**

The Director denied the claim, finding that "there was insufficient evidence to support Van Ness’s claim that his exposure to vog at only Lahainaluna School was so overwhelming that [Van Ness’s] exposure to vog occurred at this place of employment only." By framing the insufficiency of evidence in terms of whether exposure occurred "at this place of employment only," the Director imposed a requirement of exclusivity.

Under *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 297, 104 P.3d 556 (2004), such a requirement is applicable only to the "nature of employment" test. Because asthma aggravated by vog is not a disease peculiar to the employment of a technology coordinator, the unitary test governs. Under the unitary test, the relevant inquiry is not whether the vog existed outside the workplace, but whether the employment conditions were a "substantial factor" in causing the injury. *Flor*, 106 Haw. at 306, 104 P.3d at 563.

The Defense argues that the Director’s language constitutes a factual finding supported by substantial evidence, specifically the testimony of Vice Principal Dennis that vog was island-wide and worse in Kihei. However, the Director’s conclusion that the lack of a unique workplace hazard precluded compensation is a legal conclusion. The Director improperly conflated the "nature of employment" test with the unitary test. The fact that vog is a general environmental hazard does not, as a matter of law, preclude a finding that employment was a substantial factor in aggravating the condition. The Defense’s reliance on the island-wide presence of vog fails to address this legal error.

**B. The Record Supports Remand for a Substantial Factor Inquiry**

The Defense argues that even under the unitary test, the evidence does not support compensability because Claimant’s primary exposure was at home and the physical demands of the job were not unique. While these arguments may be relevant to the weight of the evidence, they cannot cure the Director’s legal error.

The record indicates that Claimant was required to perform strenuous physical labor, including repeatedly climbing steep stairs and servicing computers in non-air-conditioned, dusty environments for seven to eight hours a day. Medical evidence from Dr. Mathison links these specific work conditions to a "serious flare of asthmatic symptoms." The Director’s finding that vog was island-wide does not, as a matter of law, preclude a finding that the specific demands of Claimant’s employment were a substantial factor in aggravating his condition.

The Defense relies on *Flor* to distinguish the case, noting that the security guard in *Flor* had "close contact" with infected inmates, creating a heightened risk. However, the Defense’s argument presupposes that the "substantial factor" inquiry has already been correctly conducted and failed. Because the Director applied the incorrect legal standard—requiring exposure to occur "only" at work—the Director never properly evaluated whether the combination of physical exertion and vog exposure at work was a substantial factor in aggravating Claimant’s asthma.

The Defense further argues that Dr. Arora’s report supports the denial because vog is not associated with schools uniquely. However, Dr. Arora’s report explicitly stated that "the legal implications of this are not clear to me," and he deferred addressing the referral questions without access to medical records. The Director’s reliance on this report to establish a legal standard of uniqueness was misplaced.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the closing arguments and the record provided, the Prosecution has demonstrated that the Director misapplied the law by requiring the injury to be peculiar to the workplace. The Director’s conclusion that the lack of a unique workplace hazard precluded compensation contradicts the mandate of *Flor v. Holguin*, 106 Haw. 297, 104 P.3d 556 (2004), that the unitary test for proximate causation applies to general diseases. The Defense’s arguments regarding the sufficiency of the evidence are better addressed on remand under the correct legal standard.

**THEREFORE**, the Court rules that the Director’s decision must be reversed and the case remanded for a determination of whether Claimant’s employment was a substantial factor in aggravating his respiratory condition under the correct unitary test standard.
