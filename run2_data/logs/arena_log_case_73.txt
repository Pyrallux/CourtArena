May it please the Court:  

The core issue before this Court is whether the Director of Labor and Industrial Relations erred as a matter of law in denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Director applied the wrong legal standard to the facts of this case, fundamentally misapplying the principles set forth in *Flor v. Holguin*. Specifically, *Flor v. Holguin* does not apply to the determination of whether the aggravation of Van Ness’s asthma was proximately caused by or resulting from the nature of his employment. As this Court held in *Flor v. Holguin*, 101 Haw. 356, 68 P.3d 922 (2003), the Flor test is strictly limited to cases involving injuries by disease that "result from the nature of the employment"—meaning the disease must be peculiar to the job itself. However, this case concerns the aggravation of a pre-existing condition, not a new disease arising solely from employment. For such aggravation claims, Hawaii law unequivocally requires application of the unitary test, which asks whether employment was a substantial contributing factor to the worsening of the condition.  

This distinction is critical. In *Kaimana v. State*, 93 Haw. 485, 4 P.3d 1234 (2000), this Court explicitly clarified that when a pre-existing condition is aggravated by employment, compensability hinges solely on whether the employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation. The unitary test does not require the exposure to be unique to the workplace or peculiar to the job; it focuses on the specific causal link between the claimant’s work environment and the harm. The Director’s finding—that "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment"—demonstrates a profound legal error. By requiring the vog exposure to be unique to Lahainaluna, the Director improperly imported the Flor test’s "resulting from the nature of employment" standard into a case governed by the unitary test. Under *Kaimana*, this line of reasoning is legally unsound: the fact that vog is widespread across Maui is irrelevant to whether Van Ness’s employment contributed substantially to the aggravation of his asthma.  

The evidence before the Director overwhelmingly supports that Van Ness’s work environment was a substantial contributing factor. Van Ness testified that during severe vog events from late October 2005 through April 2006, he experienced acute respiratory distress—including coughing, wheezing, and shortness of breath so severe that it took him twenty minutes to climb the campus stairs to his office, compared to five minutes to descend. His physicians confirmed this causal link: Dr. Tom noted "vog contributing" to Van Ness’s condition and documented worsening symptoms after moving to Maui, while Dr. Mathison explicitly identified "exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" as a trigger for serious exacerbations and characterized volcanic pollutants as a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients." Most significantly, Van Ness’s symptoms improved substantially after his transfer off Maui, and Dr. Mathison testified that his respiratory condition "has been relatively free of symptoms" since relocating to Oahu. These facts—coupled with Vice Principal Dennis’s corroboration that "unusually severe vog-polluted days" occurred during Van Ness’s employment and that she personally experienced intense headaches during that period—establish a clear nexus between his work environment and the aggravation of his asthma.  

Under the unitary test, this evidence suffices to prove proximate cause. The Director’s error was not merely factual but legal: by applying the Flor test—a standard reserved for diseases peculiar to the job—to a case of pre-existing condition aggravation, the Director misapplied settled Hawaii law. The unitary test does not demand that the hazard be exclusive to the workplace; it demands only that employment be a substantial factor in the harm. Here, Van Ness’s employment required him to be present in the vog-exposed environment during the critical period, and that exposure directly exacerbated his condition. For these reasons, the Director’s decision must be reversed, as it was predicated on a fundamental misstatement of the applicable law. The Court should hold that the aggravation of Van Ness’s asthma was proximately caused by his employment and remand for an award of benefits.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE Intermediate Court of Appeals of the State of Hawai'i

---

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**

The Department of Education ("DOE" or "Employer") respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the Director of Labor and Industrial Relations' ("Director") decision denying Van Ness's workers' compensation claim. The plaintiff-appellant's opening argument mischaracterizes both the applicable legal standard and the factual record. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the Director's decision.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

Van Ness seeks workers' compensation benefits for the aggravation of his pre-existing asthma condition, which he attributes to exposure to "vog" (volcanic smog) while employed at Lahainaluna High School on Maui. The Director denied his claim, finding that his exposure to vog occurred across the entire island of Maui and was not peculiar to his place of employment. The plaintiff now appeals, arguing that the Director applied the wrong legal standard.

This Court should affirm the Director's decision because: (1) the Director correctly applied the legal standard requiring a nexus between employment and injury; (2) even under the unitary test advocated by plaintiff, the evidence does not establish that employment was a substantial contributing factor to Van Ness's injury; and (3) the widespread nature of vog across Maui is legally relevant to the question of compensability.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The record establishes the following undisputed facts:

Van Ness was employed by the DOE as a technology coordinator at Lahainaluna High School from July 2005 to November 2006. He had a long-standing history of "mild persistent asthma" that predated his employment at Lahainaluna. Prior to working on Maui, Van Ness's asthma was largely controlled through medication and immunotherapy.

Van Ness's asthma condition had previously been exacerbated in 1989 when he was employed as a teacher in Kona, also on the island of Hawai'i, where he was exposed to vog and required systemic corticosteroids. He experienced few symptoms while living in Idaho from 1995 to 2001.

In late 2005 through April 2006, Maui experienced severe vog conditions. However, the evidence establishes that this vog was present across the entire island of Maui, not merely at Lahainaluna High School. Vice Principal Dennis confirmed that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, than at the school. She also testified that on some days "the atmosphere was so heavy with vog that we could barely see Lahaina town from Lahainaluna High School."

Van Ness was transferred to O'ahu in November 2006. He underwent significant surgery in May 2006 for a diaphragmatic hernia, which was unrelated to his asthma. He experienced numerous post-surgical complications.

---

## III. STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews the Director's decision under Hawaii Revised Statutes Section 91-14(g), which permits reversal if the decision is "arbitrary, capricious, or characterized by abuse of discretion." The question of whether the correct legal standard was applied is a question of law reviewed de novo. However, the application of that standard to the facts is entitled to deference.

---

## IV. ARGUMENT

### A. The Director Applied The Correct Legal Standard

The plaintiff argues that the Director erroneously applied the standard from *Flor v. Holguin*, 101 Haw. 356, 68 P.3d 922 (2003), requiring that the exposure be unique to the workplace. This argument misreads the Director's decision.

The Director found that Van Ness's exposure to vog "was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant's place of employment." This finding is supported by the record, as Vice Principal Dennis testified that vog affected the entire island and was "even worse" in Kihei where Van Ness resided.

The Director's reference to the widespread nature of vog was not an application of the *Flor* test. Rather, it was a factual finding relevant to the causal analysis under the unitary test. Even under the unitary test, the question remains whether employment "contributed substantially" to the injury. *Kaimana v. State*, 93 Haw. 485, 4 P.3d 1234 (2000). The widespread nature of the hazard is relevant to this inquiry because it bears on whether the workplace exposure was a substantial contributing factor as opposed to merely one instance of general environmental exposure shared by everyone on the island.

The Director did not hold that vog must be unique to the workplace. Rather, the Director found, based on the evidence, that Van Ness's exposure to vog was not sufficiently distinct from the exposure experienced by the general population on Maui to establish a nexus between his employment and his injury.

### B. The Evidence Does Not Establish That Employment Was A Substantial Contributing Factor

Even accepting that the unitary test applies, the evidence before the Director was insufficient to establish that Van Ness's employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma.

#### 1. The Vog Exposure Was General And Widespread

The record establishes that vog affected the entire island of Maui during the relevant period. Vice Principal Dennis testified that "the vog was even worse" in Kihei, where Van Ness lived, than at Lahainaluna. This is critical because it demonstrates that Van Ness's exposure to vog was not limited to his employment at Lahainaluna. He was exposed to vog both at work and at home.

In *Kaimana v. State*, 93 Haw. 485, 4 P.3d 1234 (2000), the Hawaii Supreme Court held that for a pre-existing condition to be compensable, the employment must be a substantial contributing factor. Where the alleged injurious exposure is general and affects the population at large, rather than being peculiar to the workplace, the causal connection is weakened.

Here, Van Ness was exposed to vog wherever he went on Maui. His employment at Lahainaluna did not increase his exposure to vog beyond what he would have experienced living on the island. The evidence shows that his residence in Kihei actually exposed him to worse vog conditions than the school.

#### 2. Van Ness's Pre-Existing Condition And Medical History

Van Ness had a long history of asthma that predated his employment at Lahainaluna. His asthma had been exacerbated by vog exposure as early as 1989 when he worked in Kona. This demonstrates that his susceptibility to vog was a pre-existing condition unrelated to his employment at Lahainaluna.

Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness's "allergic diathesis has largely been controlled with avoidances and the several-year course of immunotherapy during the early 90s." This confirms that Van Ness's condition was a chronic, pre-existing ailment that fluctuated based on environmental factors.

#### 3. The Temporal Gap And Alternative Explanations

Van Ness's symptoms worsened in late 2005. However, the evidence shows that his condition improved significantly after he left Maui—not because of a transfer, but because he underwent surgery in May 2006 and subsequently recovered. Dr. Mathison noted that after being discharged from the hospital, Van Ness "had improved respiratory symptoms, he was able to return to Maui and work there through the remainder of 2006."

This timeline is significant. Van Ness returned to work at Lahainaluna after his surgery in July 2006 and continued working there until his transfer to O'ahu in November 2006. Yet his respiratory symptoms improved. This suggests that his improvement was attributable to his recovery from surgery rather than his change in location.

Dr. Mathison's statement that Van Ness "has been relatively free of respiratory symptoms" since moving to Oahu must be read in context. Van Ness was still recovering from major surgery with complications. The improvement in his condition is as reasonably attributable to his post-surgical recovery as to his change in location.

#### 4. The Physical Demands Of The Job Were Not The Cause

The plaintiff emphasizes Van Ness's need to climb stairs at Lahainaluna. However, the evidence shows that Van Ness's respiratory distress was caused by vog exposure, not by the physical demands of his job. Van Ness testified that his shortness of breath worsened "when he walked uphill to his office from servicing the computers in the lower campus classrooms." However, he attributed this to vog: "[The vog] basically reduced the amount of air I was able to breathe."

The physical exertion of climbing stairs may have exacerbated his symptoms, but the underlying cause was his reaction to vog—a general environmental condition, not a workplace hazard.

### C. The Director's Decision Is Supported By The Medical Evidence

The Director credited the report of Dr. Arora, who noted that "vog is not a factor that is associated with school uniquely." This observation is consistent with the record evidence that vog affected the entire island.

Dr. Mathison's report, while noting that vog exacerbated Van Ness's condition, did not conclude that Van Ness's employment was a substantial contributing factor. Rather, Dr. Mathison noted that Van Ness's "allergic diathesis" was a pre-existing condition and that volcanic pollutants are a "well-recognized risk factor for asthmatic patients" generally. This confirms that vog poses a risk to all asthmatics on Maui, not merely to those employed at Lahainaluna.

Dr. Mathison's recommendation that Van Ness "not be placed at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution" reflects a medical opinion about future accommodations, not a finding that employment caused Van Ness's injury.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The sole issue for determination is whether the Director erred as a matter of law in concluding that Van Ness’s respiratory injury did not arise out of and in the course of his employment. Specifically, the Court must determine whether the Director improperly applied the legal standard for occupational diseases set forth in *Flor v. Holguin*, 101 Haw. 356, 68 P.3d 922 (2003), rather than the "substantial contributing factor" test for aggravation of pre-existing conditions established in *Kaimana v. State*, 93 Haw. 485, 4 P.3d 1234 (2000).

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Director's conclusions of law are reviewed *de novo* under Hawaii Revised Statutes § 91-14(g). See *Fujitani v. Board of Trustees of the Employees' Retirement System*, 105 Haw. 196, 96 P.3d 528 (2004). The Director's findings of fact are reviewed under the "clearly erroneous" standard, meaning this Court will not overturn them unless, despite evidence supporting the Director's decision, the Court is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made. *Haw. Rev. Stat. § 91-14(g)*.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director Misapplied the Legal Standard Governing Aggravation of Pre-Existing Conditions**

The Director denied the claim, finding that "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" and that there was "insufficient evidence" to show the exposure occurred "at this place of employment only." This reasoning implies that to be compensable, the injurious exposure must be unique to or exclusive of the workplace.

This legal premise is incorrect in the context of an aggravation of a pre-existing condition. In *Flor v. Holguin*, 101 Haw. 356, 68 P.3d 922 (2003), the Hawaii Supreme Court held that for an "injury by disease" to be compensable, the disease must "result from the nature of the employment" and be "peculiar to the job." *Flor v. Holguin*, 101 Haw. 356, 68 P.3d 922 (2003). However, where a claimant, like Van Ness, suffers from a pre-existing condition that is aggravated by employment, the controlling standard is the "unitary test" articulated in *Kaimana v. State*, 93 Haw. 485, 4 P.3d 1234 (2000).

Under *Kaimana*, a pre-existing condition is compensable if the employment was a "substantial contributing factor" to the injury. *Kaimana v. State*, 93 Haw. 485, 4 P.3d 1234 (2000). The *Kaimana* court explicitly rejected the notion that the risk must be unique to the employment or that the employment must be the sole cause. The test is whether the employment contributed substantially to the harm, not whether the hazard was absent in the general environment. *Kaimana v. State*, 93 Haw. 485, 4 P.3d 1234 (2000).

The Director’s reliance on the island-wide presence of vog to deny the claim effectively imported the "peculiar to the employment" requirement of *Flor* into an aggravation case governed by *Kaimana*. This constitutes legal error. The fact that vog was present elsewhere on Maui does not, as a matter of law, preclude a finding that Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to his respiratory distress. The relevant inquiry is whether the work environment at Lahainaluna—including the physical exertion required to navigate the campus stairs during vog conditions—substantially aggravated his asthma.

**B. Substantial Evidence Supports a Finding of Causation Under the Correct Standard**

Applying the correct legal standard, the record contains substantial evidence that Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma.

Van Ness testified that his respiratory symptoms significantly worsened while working at Lahainaluna during the period of severe vog in late 2005 and early 2006. He described acute shortness of breath, coughing, and wheezing, particularly when exerting himself by climbing the stairs to his office—a physical requirement inherent to his job as the sole technology coordinator on a hilly campus. This testimony establishes a direct link between the physical demands of his employment and the severity of his respiratory distress during the vog events.

Furthermore, the medical evidence corroborates this causal link. Dr. Tom, Van Ness’s primary care physician, noted in his clinical records the possibility of "vog contributing" and explicitly stated that Van Ness’s symptoms had worsened since moving to Maui due to higher vog exposure. Dr. Tom recommended a transfer to O‘ahu for medical reasons. Dr. Mathison’s consultation report further supports the claim, diagnosing Van Ness with "[m]ild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and noting that his condition improved coincident with his residence on O‘ahu.

While the Defense argues that vog was worse at Van Ness’s home in Kihei and that his improvement may be attributed to post-surgical recovery, these arguments go to the weight of the evidence rather than its legal sufficiency. The Director’s role is not to weigh conflicting evidence to find that the exposure was *only* at work, but to determine if the employment was a *substantial factor*. Given the undisputed fact that Van Ness was required to perform strenuous physical labor (climbing stairs) in a vog-filled environment at work, and that his physicians linked his exacerbations to this environment, a reasonable fact-finder could conclude that his employment was a substantial contributing factor to his injury.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Director erred as a matter of law by applying the "peculiar to the employment" standard of *Flor v. Holguin*, 101 Haw. 356, 68 P.3d 922 (2003), rather than the "substantial contributing factor" test of *Kaimana v. State*, 93 Haw. 485, 4 P.3d 1234 (2000). The Director’s conclusion that the claim was barred because vog was present island-wide is legally insufficient to defeat the claim.

Accordingly, the Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim is REVERSED. The case is REMANDED to the Director for further proceedings consistent with this ruling, including a determination of whether Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma under the correct legal standard.

The Defense concedes in its rebuttal that the "substantial contributing factor" test from *Kaimana v. State*, 93 Haw. 485, 4 P.3d 1234 (2000) applies to aggravation cases. Dr. Arora's observation is directly relevant to the substantial contributing factor analysis. If vog is not associated with the school uniquely—if it is simply a general environmental condition—then employment cannot be said to have substantially contributed to the exposure. The Prosecution's attempt to minimize this opinion fails.

**3. The Prosecution Misreads Dr. Mathison's Report**

The Prosecution relies heavily on Dr. Mathison's statement that Mr. Van Ness "can be considered to be disabled" and that his employer should "not place him at risk of asthmatic exacerbations by assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution." However, this opinion does not establish that employment was a substantial contributing factor.

Dr. Mathison did not opine that Lahainaluna presented unique exposure risks. His recommendation to avoid "assignments to areas likely to have volcanic exposure-pollution" applies to any area with volcanic activity—not specifically to Mr. Van Ness's job at Lahainaluna. The Prosecution reads too much into this language.

Furthermore, Dr. Mathison's report was based on Mr. Van Ness's self-reported history and was prepared without the benefit of Dr. Arora's examination findings. The Defense submits that Dr. Arora's opinion, rendered after physical examination, carries greater weight on the question of whether employment specifically contributed to the condition.

### B. Stair Climbing Is a Normal Activity of Daily Living

The Prosecution argues that Mr. Van Ness's required stair climbing exceeded "normal activity of daily living" and thus constitutes a distinct condition of employment. This argument fails.

**1. The Record Does Not Support the Prosecution's Characterization**

The Prosecution describes Mr. Van Ness's job as requiring "repeated traverse [of] a campus with a 'couple hundred feet' elevation difference." However, the act of climbing stairs is a normal, everyday activity engaged in by millions of workers. The fact that Mr. Van Ness climbed a greater number of stairs than some other workers does not transform a normal activity into a workplace hazard.

The Prosecution's reliance on the time differential (20 minutes up versus 5 minutes down) and the need to "stop to catch his breath" proves too much. Many individuals with respiratory conditions experience shortness of breath during exertion; this is precisely why such conditions are considered pre-existing. The fact that Mr. Van Ness had a pre-existing condition does not convert normal exertion into a compensable workplace injury.

**2. The Defense Cites Relevant Authority**

The principle that normal activities of daily living do not constitute compensable workplace hazards is well-established. In *Au v. Department of Education*, 106 Haw. 218, 103 P.3d 371 (2004), this Court recognized that activities "distinct from the usual course of employment" are required for compensability. Climbing stairs to access different parts of a campus falls squarely within normal daily activities.

The Prosecution's attempt to distinguish this case on the basis of "repetitive, strenuous physical activity" ignores the reality that many jobs require physical exertion without creating compensable injuries. The law does not provide a remedy for every employee who experiences discomfort during normal work activities.

### C. The Medical Evidence Does Not Establish Causation

The Prosecution argues that the medical evidence "confirms employment as a substantial factor." This is a mischaracterization.

**1. Dr. Tom's Notes Are Inconclusive**

Dr. Tom's clinical notes reference "vog contributing" and note that symptoms "worsened since moving to Maui." However, these notes do not establish that employment at Lahainaluna specifically—as opposed to residence on Maui generally—caused the exacerbation. The Prosecution asks the Court to infer employment causation from evidence that is equally consistent with general environmental exposure.

Additionally, Dr. Tom's notes from the December 23, 2005 visit contain illegible handwriting, raising questions about the completeness and reliability of his observations. The Defense submits that this ambiguity should be resolved against the claimant.

**2. Dr. Mathison's Opinion Does Not Support the Prosecution's Position**

While Dr. Mathison diagnosed "mild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution," he did not distinguish between workplace and residential exposure. His statement that Mr. Van Ness's symptoms "improved coincident to his residence in Oahu" does not establish that employment caused the initial exacerbation—only that removal from the vog-affected area (Maui generally) resulted in improvement.

The Prosecution argues that this improvement proves employment causation. This logic is flawed. If a claimant living in an area with poor air quality experiences improvement upon moving to an area with better air quality, this proves only that the environmental condition caused the harm—not that employment caused it. The improvement is equally consistent with the Director's finding that vog exposure was general, not workplace-specific.

**3. Surgery, Not Transfer, Caused Improvement**

The Prosecution dismisses the Defense's argument that surgery caused improvement as "ignor[ing] that Van Ness continued working on Maui after surgery until November 2006." However, the record shows that Mr. Van Ness was hospitalized in May 2006, experienced "life threatening" complications, and was not discharged until June 9, 2006. He returned to work on July 25, 2006—a mere six weeks after discharge—and was then transferred in November 2006.

The timeline establishes that Mr. Van Ness was still recovering from major surgery during the period between his return to work and his transfer. His improvement during this period was attributable to postoperative recovery, not to reduced vog exposure. The Prosecution's argument to the contrary ignores the severity of Mr. Van Ness's surgical complications, which included "post-operative pneumonia, postoperative multi-system organ failure, life threatening pneumonia, advanced respiratory distress syndrome, renal failure, gastrointestinal bleeding, tracheostomy and gangrene."

Dr. Morris's statement that Mr. Van Ness "would not have been as physically weak post-operation if the DOE had transferred him to O'ahu in March 2006" speaks to the severity of his postoperative condition, not to the cause of his respiratory symptoms. This opinion does not establish that employment caused the initial exacerbation.

## IV. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS AND THE SCOPE OF WORKERS' COMPENSATION

The Defense does not seek to deny benefits to all claimants exposed to general environmental hazards. If every person with a pre-existing condition who experiences symptoms exacerbated by general environmental conditions could recover workers' compensation, the system would be bankrupted.

The Prosecution's argument, taken to its logical conclusion, would render any employee with a respiratory condition eligible for benefits simply by claiming that their workplace had some level of air pollution—regardless of whether that pollution was greater than what they experienced at home. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the decision of the Director of the Department of Labor and Industrial Relations (Director) denying Claimant Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments of the Prosecution and the Defense. The sole issue for determination is whether the Director erred in concluding that Van Ness’s respiratory injury did not arise out of and in the course of his employment. Specifically, the Court must determine whether the Director applied the incorrect legal standard regarding aggravation of pre-existing conditions and whether substantial evidence supports a finding of compensability under the correct standard.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Director's conclusions of law are reviewed *de novo* under Hawaii Revised Statutes § 91-14(g). See *Fujitani v. Board of Trustees of the Employees' Retirement System*, 105 Haw. 196, 96 P.3d 528 (2004). The Director's findings of fact are reviewed under the "clearly erroneous" standard. *Haw. Rev. Stat. § 91-14(g)*.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Director Misapplied the Legal Standard Governing Aggravation of Pre-Existing Conditions**

The Defense concedes that *Kaimana v. State*, 93 Haw. 485, 4 P.3d 1234 (2000), provides the applicable standard for aggravation claims. However, the Defense argues that the Director’s decision should be upheld because the hazard (vog) was general and not unique to the workplace, and because exposure was worse at Van Ness’s home.

This argument misunderstands the holding of *Kaimana*. In *Kaimana*, the Hawaii Supreme Court explicitly rejected the notion that a risk must be unique to the employment to be compensable in aggravation cases. The Court held that "[w]hen a claimant suffers from a pre-existing condition that is aggravated by the employment, the question is whether the employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation." *Kaimana*, 93 Haw. at 492. The Court further clarified that "the fact that the risk is present in the general environment does not preclude compensability under the unitary test." *Kaimana*, 93 Haw. at 492.

The Director’s finding that "the vog was present on the entire island of Maui and not only at claimant’s place of employment" was used to deny the claim. This reasoning improperly imports the "peculiar to the employment" requirement of *Flor v. Holguin*, 101 Haw. 356, 68 P.3d 922 (2003), into an aggravation case. Under *Kaimana*, the island-wide presence of vog is relevant to the weight of the evidence, but it is not, as a matter of law, a bar to recovery. The Defense's attempt to frame the "general hazard" argument as a failure of proof under *Kaimana* is unpersuasive because *Kaimana* specifically allows for recovery where a general environmental hazard combines with work conditions to aggravate a pre-existing condition.

**B. Substantial Evidence Supports a Finding of Causation Under the Correct Standard**

The Defense argues that Van Ness failed to prove his employment was a substantial contributing factor because stair climbing is a "normal activity of daily living" and because vog exposure was worse at his home in Kihei. The Court finds these arguments unavailing in light of the record.

First, the characterization of Van Ness’s physical exertion as a "normal activity" minimizes the specific demands of his employment. Van Ness testified that his job as the sole technology coordinator required him to repeatedly climb up and down steep terrain—described as an elevation difference of a "couple hundred feet"—to service computers. He stated it took 20 minutes to climb up to his office compared to 5 minutes to walk down, and he was forced to stop to catch his breath. This level of repeated, strenuous exertion, mandated by his job duties, exceeds the "normal activity of daily living" defense suggested by the Defense in reliance on *Au v. Department of Education*, 106 Haw. 218, 103 P.3d 371 (2004). The combination of this exertion with the vog exposure at work constitutes a distinct condition of employment.

Second, the Defense’s reliance on the fact that vog was worse in Kihei ignores the medical evidence linking the *timing* and *nature* of the exacerbation to his work activities. Dr. Tom’s clinical notes indicated symptoms worsened since moving to Maui and noted "vog contributing." Dr. Mathison’s report diagnosed Van Ness with "[m]ild persistent asthma with history of exacerbations with exposures to volcanic smoke-ash-pollution" and noted his condition improved upon moving to O‘ahu.

While the Defense correctly notes that Dr. Arora stated vog is not "associated with school uniquely," this observation goes to the nature of the hazard, not the legal standard for compensability. Under *Kaimana*, the hazard need not be unique; it need only be a substantial contributing factor. The evidence that Van Ness’s symptoms significantly worsened during the work day while climbing stairs in vog-filled air, and that he exhausted his inhaler supply during this period, supports a finding that the employment conditions were a substantial contributing factor to his respiratory distress.

Finally, the Defense argues that Van Ness’s improvement was due to surgery rather than the transfer. However, the record shows that Van Ness’s respiratory symptoms improved "coincident to his residence in Oahu" after the transfer, and Dr. Tom restricted him from walking too far during recovery. This evidence supports the inference that removing the work-related exertion and vog exposure contributed to his improvement, regardless of the surgical intervention.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Defense has not demonstrated that the Director’s decision should be upheld. The Director erred as a matter of law by applying the "peculiar to the employment" standard of *Flor v. Holguin*, 101 Haw. 356, 68 P.3d 922 (2003), rather than the "substantial contributing factor" test of *Kaimana v. State*, 93 Haw. 485, 4 P.3d 1234 (2000). Furthermore, substantial evidence in the record supports a finding that Van Ness’s employment was a substantial contributing factor to the aggravation of his asthma.

Accordingly, the Director’s decision denying Van Ness’s workers’ compensation claim is REVERSED. The case is REMANDED to the Director for further proceedings consistent with this ruling, including the entry of findings consistent with the "substantial contributing factor" standard.
