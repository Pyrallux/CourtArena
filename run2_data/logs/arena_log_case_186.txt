Based on the arguments presented and the applicable law, the Court concludes that the lower courts correctly ruled in favor of the employer.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Compassionate Use Act Does Not Create Employment Rights**

Plaintiff argues that his termination violates FEHA because he used marijuana pursuant to the Compassionate Use Act (Health & Saf. Code, § 11362.5). However, the text and history of the Compassionate Use Act are clear: the initiative was intended to provide a defense to criminal prosecution for the possession and use of marijuana, not to regulate the employment relationship. As argued, nothing in the Act suggests that voters intended to address the respective rights and duties of employers and employees. The Act’s sole purpose is to shield individuals from criminal liability; it does not create affirmative rights in the employment context or impose a duty on employers to accommodate conduct that remains unlawful under federal law and the state’s controlled substances laws.

**B. FEHA Does Not Require Accommodation of Illegal Drug Use**

Plaintiff contends that the employer violated FEHA by failing to accommodate his disability. FEHA requires employers to provide reasonable accommodation for the known physical or mental disability of an applicant or employee. However, this mandate is not without boundaries. It does not require an employer to accommodate conduct that is illegal under state law.

The controlling precedent on this issue is *Loder v. City of Glendale*, 14 Cal.4th 846 (1997). In *Loder*, this Court explicitly held that "Under California law, an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." *Loder v. City of Glendale*, 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883 (1997). The employer in this case acted within its rights under *Loder* by requiring a preemployment drug test and terminating plaintiff based on his positive test for tetrahydrocannabinol (THC).

The plaintiff’s argument that his marijuana use was "medical" and authorized by a physician does not alter this analysis. While the Compassionate Use Act provides a defense to criminal charges, it does not transform marijuana use into lawful conduct for all purposes, particularly employment. An employer’s right to maintain a drug-free workplace and to consider illegal drug use in employment decisions remains intact. Therefore, FEHA does not obligate the employer to accommodate the plaintiff's use of marijuana, regardless of its medical necessity or the plaintiff’s satisfactory job performance.

**C. No Public Policy Violation Supports a Wrongful Termination Claim**

Plaintiff further alleges a cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. To establish such a claim, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the termination violates a fundamental public policy that is tethered to a constitutional or statutory provision. The leading authorities on this requirement are *Stevenson v. Superior Court*, 16 Cal.4th 880 (1997), and *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 27 Cal.3d 167 (1980). These cases establish that a policy must be clear, well-established, and fundamental.

The Compassionate Use Act does not articulate a public policy protecting medical marijuana users from employment termination. The Act is silent regarding employer-employee relations and does not create an exemption for employees from neutral workplace drug policies. Consequently, the plaintiff cannot bootstrap a wrongful termination claim from a statute that provides only a criminal defense. Because no public policy supporting the plaintiff’s claim exists, this cause of action fails as a matter of law.

The arguments presented demonstrate that the Compassionate Use Act is limited to the criminal context and does not infringe upon an employer’s right to enforce drug-free workplace policies. Under *Loder v. City of Glendale*, 14 Cal.4th 846 (1997), the employer acted lawfully in considering plaintiff’s positive drug test in its employment decision. Furthermore, under *Stevenson v. Superior Court*, 16 Cal.4th 880 (1997), and *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 27 Cal.3d 167 (1980), the plaintiff cannot state a claim for wrongful termination as no public policy supports his position.

The judgment of the lower courts sustaining the demurrer without leave to amend is correct. The plaintiff’s complaint fails to state a cause of action under FEHA or for wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND PROCEDURAL POSTURE

This case comes before this Court on review of a judgment entered after the superior court sustained a demurrer to the plaintiff's complaint without leave to amend. In this procedural posture, the only question before this Court is whether the plaintiff can state a cause of action. As this Court has held, we treat the demurrer as admitting the complaint's well-pleaded allegations of material fact, but not its contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. *Evans v. The defense acknowledges the well-pleaded facts in the complaint, which establish the following: The plaintiff suffers from a disability (chronic back pain resulting from injuries sustained while serving in the United States Air Force), is a qualified individual with a disability under the FEHA, and receives governmental disability benefits. The plaintiff began using marijuana in September 1999 on his physician's recommendation pursuant to the Compassionate Use Act. The defendant RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc. offered the plaintiff a job as lead systems administrator, required him to take a preemployment drug test, and after the plaintiff tested positive for THC, terminated his employment.

These facts, however, do not give rise to any legally cognizable claim. ## II. THE COMPASSIONATE USE ACT DOES NOT CREATE EMPLOYMENT RIGHTS

The Compassionate Use Act of 1996 (Health & Safety Code, § 11362.5) provides a defense to certain state criminal charges involving marijuana for patients who use the drug on a physician's recommendation. The Act is explicitly limited to criminal defense matters and is silent regarding the rights and duties of employers and employees.

As this Court held in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883: "Nothing in the text or history of the Compassionate Use Act suggests the voters intended the measure to address the respective rights and duties of employers and employees." The Act's sole purpose is to provide a criminal defense—it imposes no obligations on private employers and creates no employment rights.

The plaintiff has correctly conceded that the Compassionate Use Act does not create employment rights. This concession is fatal to his claims. The plaintiff cannot rely on the Compassionate Use Act as a basis for challenging an employer's lawful decision to maintain a drug-free workplace or to terminate an employee who uses marijuana, even with a physician's recommendation.

The defense further notes that the plaintiff's complaint acknowledges that he used marijuana in violation of federal law, as marijuana remains classified as an illegal controlled substance under the Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. § 801 et seq.). The Compassionate Use Act provides only a state-law defense to criminal prosecution; it does not legalize marijuana use for any purpose under federal law, nor does it create workplace protections.

---

## III. FEHA DOES NOT REQUIRE ACCOMMODATION OF ILLEGAL DRUG USE

The plaintiff alleges that the defendant violated the Fair Employment and Housing Act (Government Code § 12940 et seq.) by discharging him because of his disability and by failing to make reasonable accommodation for his disability. This claim fails as a matter of law.

FEHA requires employers to provide reasonable accommodation for the known physical or mental disabilities of an applicant or employee. However, this obligation does not extend to accommodating conduct that violates state law. Government Code section 12940, subdivision (n)(1), provides that an employer is not required to accommodate an employee if the accommodation would require the employer to commit a crime or violation of state law.

As this Court held in *Loder v. City of Glendale* (1997) 14 Cal.4th 846, 882-883: "Under California law, an employer may require preemployment drug tests and take illegal drug use into consideration in making employment decisions." The plaintiff has correctly conceded that FEHA does not require an employer to accommodate conduct that violates state law.

Marijuana remains an illegal controlled substance under California law for purposes of employment. The Compassionate Use Act provides only a criminal defense; it does not legalize marijuana or create an exception to employer drug policies. The defendant's enforcement of its neutral, uniform drug-free workplace policy was lawful and consistent with FEHA.

The plaintiff's argument that his satisfactory job performance justifies accommodation is without merit. The law does not require employers to tolerate illegal conduct, regardless of job performance. A neutral drug policy—applied uniformly to all applicants and employees, as RagingWire's policy was—does not constitute disability-based discrimination under FEHA.

The plaintiff has conceded that FEHA does not require accommodation of illegal drug use. This concession is fatal to his FEHA claim.

---

## IV. NO PUBLIC POLICY SUPPORTS A WRONGFUL TERMINATION CLAIM

The plaintiff also alleges that the defendant wrongfully terminated his employment in violation of public policy. To establish a wrongful termination claim in violation of public policy, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the termination violates a fundamental public policy that is tethered to a constitutional or statutory provision. *Stevenson v. Superior Court* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 887; *Tameny v. Atlantic Richfield Co.* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 167, 170.

The plaintiff has correctly conceded that he cannot state a claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy. The Compassionate Use Act does not articulate a public policy protecting medical marijuana users from termination. The Act is a criminal defense statute with no language addressing employment, workplace safety, or accommodation. As this Court recognized in *Stevenson*, the public policy exception to at-will employment is narrow and cannot be manufactured from a statute that is silent on employment matters.

The plaintiff cannot identify any constitutional or statutory provision that establishes a fundamental public policy protecting his termination. The Compassionate Use Act does not create such a policy, and no other provision of California law does so. The defendant's decision to maintain a lawful drug-free workplace policy does not violate any public policy.

## V. THE DEFENDANT'S CONDUCT WAS LAWFUL AND REASONABLE

The defendant RagingWire Telecommunications, Inc. acted lawfully and reasonably in all respects. The defendant maintained a neutral, uniform drug-free workplace policy that applied to all applicants and employees. The plaintiff was informed of this policy before his employment began and was required to take a preemployment drug test as a condition of employment.

When the plaintiff tested positive for THC, the defendant gave the plaintiff an opportunity to explain his use of marijuana. The defendant's conduct demonstrates a thoughtful, deliberate process that respected the plaintiff's rights while maintaining a lawful workplace policy. The defendant did not discriminate against the plaintiff because of his disability; rather, the defendant enforced a neutral policy against illegal drug use, as California law expressly permits.

The plaintiff has failed to state a cause of action under any legal theory. The Compassionate Use Act does not create employment rights. FEHA does not require accommodation of illegal drug use. No public policy supports a wrongful termination claim. The plaintiff's own concessions during this litigation confirm that these claims fail as a matter of law.
