## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This case presents a fundamental question of tribal-state relations that requires careful balancing of tribal self-governance interests against legitimate state regulatory concerns. While the State acknowledges the tribal sovereignty principles cited by the plaintiff, the State contends that the district court erred in its application of those principles and in its interpretation of Minnesota's reciprocity statutes. The plaintiff asks this Court to extend reciprocal exemptions to tribal members, but doing so would exceed the scope of the statutory framework established by the Minnesota Legislature and would improperly expand the treaty-based rights of the Red Lake Band beyond their proper limits.

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS PERTINENT TO DEFENSE

The State of Minnesota submits the following facts for the Court's consideration:

The Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians enacted a motor vehicle registration ordinance in January 1974, which was approved by the Secretary of the Interior on March 5, 1974. The ordinance imposes a substantial annual registration fee on all motor vehicles using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake Reservation and provides for the issuance of certificates of title, maintenance of records, and establishment of procedures for recording transfers.

The critical fact overlooked by the district court is that the Red Lake Reservation constitutes a *single-entity reservation* where the Band holds fee simple title to all lands within the reservation boundaries. This unique status distinguishes the Red Lake Band from other jurisdictions with which Minnesota has extended reciprocity, including other states, territories, and foreign provinces.

The State has not refused to recognize the Band's ordinance entirely. The sole dispute concerns whether Minnesota must extend the same reciprocal exemptions to tribal members that it extends to residents of other states and foreign jurisdictions.

---

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The District Court Erred in Interpreting Minnesota's Reciprocity Statutes

The district court erroneously interpreted Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187 as requiring Minnesota to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band. These statutes authorize agreements for reciprocal recognition of vehicle registration with *other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces*. The Legislature specifically enumerated the entities with which reciprocity agreements may be made, and the Red Lake Band is not among them.

As the Supreme Court held in *Alaska v. Custom Aircraft Servs., Inc.*, 527 P.2d 713, 716 (Alaska 1974), "a statute conferring power to act upon designated subjects is construed as excluding from its operation all subjects not expressly mentioned." The Minnesota Legislature's explicit listing of permissible reciprocity partners demonstrates a deliberate legislative choice to exclude tribal entities from the reciprocity framework.

The district court's reasoning would effectively rewrite Minnesota's reciprocity statutes to include tribes, despite the Legislature's evident intent to limit such agreements to governmental entities operating under similar jurisdictional frameworks. This Court should decline to add to the statutory list what the Legislature chose to omit.

### B. The District Court Erred in Applying the "Comparable Governing Entities" Test

The district court committed reversible error in creating a novel "comparable governing entities" test that has no basis in Minnesota law. The court below held that Minnesota must extend reciprocity to the Band if doing so would "significantly dilute the effectiveness of the governmental authority exercised" and absent "considerations of a substantial character" justifying denial. This test improperly imports federal Indian law principles into a state statutory scheme that was not designed to address tribal sovereignty.

While the plaintiff correctly cites *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832), for the proposition that tribal nations are distinct political communities, this principle does not stand for the proposition that states must treat tribal governments identically to other state governments for all purposes. The Constitution grants Congress plenary power over Indian affairs, *see United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 383-84 (1886), and states act pursuant to congressional authorization when interacting with tribal entities.

The district court's reliance on *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164 (1973), and *Mescalero Apache Tribe v. Jones*, 411 U.S. 145 (1973), is misplaced. Those cases addressed state taxation of tribal members and tribal sovereignty vis-à-vis congressional action, respectively. They do not stand for the proposition that states must extend all benefits granted to other governmental entities to tribal governments absent a compelling state interest.

### C. The State's Interests Justify Differential Treatment

The district court erred in finding that no "considerations of a substantial character" justified the disparity of treatment between the Red Lake Band and other jurisdictions. To the contrary, several substantial considerations support Minnesota's position.

**First**, the single-entity status of the Red Lake Reservation creates unique jurisdictional circumstances not present in other reciprocity relationships. Unlike other states with which Minnesota enters reciprocity agreements, the Red Lake Reservation consists entirely of tribal lands held in fee simple. This creates a closed jurisdiction where state motor vehicle laws have historically not applied. Extending reciprocity to tribal members while denying it to residents of other states would create an anomalous situation where tribal members enjoy greater privileges than Minnesota residents.

**Second**, the State has a substantial interest in maintaining highway safety and ensuring that all vehicles using Minnesota's roads are properly registered and taxed. The district court acknowledged that the Band's ordinance imposes a "substantial annual registration fee," yet failed to explain why Minnesota should exempt tribal members from state registration requirements when those members' vehicles use state highways outside the reservation.

**Third**, the policy rationale underlying Minnesota's reciprocity statutes—promoting "the fullest possible use of its highway system"—does not require extension to the Red Lake Band. The reciprocity framework exists to avoid double taxation and to facilitate interstate commerce. The Red Lake Band is not a foreign jurisdiction engaging in interstate commerce with Minnesota; it is a tribal government whose members reside within Minnesota's geographic boundaries.

### D. The District Court Improperly Expanded Tribal Sovereignty

The district court's order effectively grants the Red Lake Band a privilege not extended to any other governmental entity within Minnesota—the exemption from state motor vehicle registration requirements. This expansion of tribal sovereignty finds no support in federal Indian law or Minnesota's statutory framework.

In *Fisher v. United States*, 426 U.S. 443, 448-49 (1976), the Supreme Court recognized that "tribal sovereignty is dependent on, and subordinate to, the federal government." The states' authority over tribal matters is similarly limited, but not eliminated. The district court failed to recognize that Minnesota retains authority to regulate motor vehicle use on state highways by tribal members traveling outside the reservation.

The plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that tribal sovereignty includes the right to demand reciprocal treatment from states under state regulatory statutes. Such a rule would eviscerate state authority over matters of traditional state concern, including highway safety and taxation.

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the State of Minnesota respectfully requests that this Honorable Court reverse the district court's order granting declaratory judgment and permanent injunction in favor of the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians. The district court erred in interpreting Minnesota's reciprocity statutes to require extension of exemptions to the Band, in applying an improper "comparable governing entities" test, in failing to recognize substantial state interests justifying differential treatment, and in improperly expanding tribal sovereignty beyond its proper limits.

The State of Minnesota remains committed to working cooperatively with the Red Lake Band on matters of mutual concern, including highway safety and economic development. However, such cooperation must occur within the framework established by the Minnesota Legislature, not through judicial decree that rewrites state law to accommodate tribal demands.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s order granting declaratory judgment and permanent injunction in favor of the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians. The central issue is whether the State of Minnesota’s refusal to extend motor vehicle registration reciprocity to the Red Lake Band constitutes an unlawful interference with the Band’s sovereign authority to govern its internal affairs. After reviewing the arguments presented by both the State and the Band, we hold that the district court’s order must be affirmed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Principles of Tribal Sovereignty Govern This Dispute**

The State of Minnesota attempts to frame this case as a simple matter of statutory interpretation, arguing that the text of Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187 limits reciprocity to the entities expressly listed. However, this analysis ignores the foundational principles of federal Indian law that limit state authority over tribal nations. As the Supreme Court long ago established in *Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832), Indian tribes are "distinct political communities, having territorial boundaries, within which their authority is exclusive." This principle of exclusivity prohibits states from enacting laws that govern the internal relations of tribal life or that significantly dilute the effectiveness of tribal self-government.

The State’s reliance on *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 383-84 (1886), and *Fisher v. United States*, 426 U.S. 443, 448-49 (1976), to assert that tribal sovereignty is subordinate and therefore does not compel state compliance is misplaced. While it is true that tribal powers are dependent on the federal government, this dependence does not grant the state license to treat a tribal government as inferior to a foreign province or another state when the tribal government is exercising an otherwise valid sovereign function. The State cites no authority suggesting that a state may arbitrarily refuse to recognize tribal governmental acts—such as a valid motor vehicle registration ordinance—while recognizing the acts of foreign sovereigns.

**B. The State’s Statutory Argument Cannot Override Federal Preemption**

The State argues that the district court erred by reading the Red Lake Band into the reciprocity statutes, citing *Alaska v. Custom Aircraft Servs., Inc.*, 527 P.2d 713, 716 (Alaska 1974), for the proposition that a statute listing specific entities excludes those not mentioned. While this is a valid principle of statutory construction generally, it cannot be applied here to achieve a result that is preempted by federal law and the principle of tribal sovereignty.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that state laws which interfere with tribal self-government are invalid unless Congress has expressly authorized such interference. *Mescalero Apache Tribe v. Jones*, 411 U.S. 145, 148-49 (1973). In *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Comm'n*, 411 U.S. 164, 174 (1973), the Court held that a state’s power to tax tribal members is "severely limited" by federal law. The imposition of double registration fees—effectively a tax—on tribal members who are already in compliance with the Band’s comprehensive ordinance constitutes a similar burden on tribal self-government.

If the State were permitted to strictly construe its reciprocity statutes to exclude the Band, it would effectively nullify the Band’s sovereign power to regulate its own internal affairs. The State’s construction would require Band members to pay "more than one full registration fee per annum," a burden not placed on residents of Wisconsin, the District of Columbia, or the Province of Ontario. Such a disparity "significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the governmental authority exercised" by the Tribal Council, a result that federal law prohibits.

**C. The "Comparable Governing Entities" Test is Supported by the Record**

The State contends that the district court created a novel "comparable governing entities" test. However, this test is a necessary application of the equal protection principles inherent in the federal-tribal relationship. The Band’s ordinance, approved by the Secretary of the Interior, imposes substantial fees, issues titles, and maintains records—functions identical to those performed by the states and foreign provinces listed in the Minnesota statutes.

The State argues that the Red Lake Reservation’s "single-entity" status distinguishes it from other jurisdictions. We find this distinction unpersuasive. The fact that the Band holds fee simple title to its lands does not diminish its status as a governing entity; rather, it underscores the Band's comprehensive authority over its territory. The State’s argument that recognizing the Band would create an "anomalous situation" ignores the reality that the State already grants exemptions to foreign entities with far less connection to Minnesota than the Red Lake Band possesses.

**D. No Substantial State Interests Justify the Disparity**

Finally, the State argues that highway safety and taxation justify the denial of reciprocity. The district court, however, found that "the record in this case fails to establish the existence of conditions of a substantial character which would justify or require denial of such equal treatment." The State has not demonstrated how recognizing the Band’s plates—which meet the same safety and recording standards as other jurisdictions—would compromise highway safety. Furthermore, the State’s interest in revenue collection does not justify the discriminatory treatment of tribal members, particularly when the Band’s ordinance already imposes a "substantial annual registration fee."

**III. CONCLUSION**

The State of Minnesota is obligated to afford the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians the same privileges it grants to other comparable governing entities. To do otherwise would significantly dilute the effectiveness of the Band’s governmental authority in violation of established principles of tribal sovereignty. The district court correctly determined that the State’s refusal to extend reciprocity was unjustified. Therefore, the order of the district court is affirmed.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## In the Court of Appeals of the State of Minnesota

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF POSITION

Your Honor, the State of Minnesota respectfully submits this closing statement in defense of the Court's ruling reversing the district court's order. The plaintiff's closing statement misapplies fundamental principles of statutory construction, misunderstands the scope of tribal sovereignty, and ignores the clear legislative intent expressed in Minnesota's reciprocity statutes. The State maintains that its position is fully supported by law, and this Court should affirm the reversal of the district court's judgment.

The central question before this Court is not whether the Red Lake Band possesses sovereign authority to regulate motor vehicles within its reservation—its authority to do so is undisputed. Rather, the question is whether Minnesota is constitutionally or statutorily required to extend reciprocity benefits to tribal registrations for travel on state highways when the Minnesota Legislature has not authorized such an extension. The answer is no.

---

## II. THE PLAINTIFF'S STATUTORY INTERPRETATION ARGUMENT FAILS

### A. The Express Language of Minnesota's Reciprocity Statutes Controls

The plaintiff argues that this Court should interpret Minnesota's reciprocity statutes to include Indian tribes because doing so would further the legislature's stated purpose of promoting highway use. This argument ignores the fundamental principle that when the legislature has expressly enumerated the entities eligible for reciprocity, courts must respect that enumeration.

In *State v. Minnesota Mutual Life Insurance Co.*, 205 Minn. 138, 285 N.W. 466, 469 (1939), this Court firmly established that "the mention of one thing implies the exclusion of another." The Minnesota Legislature, in enacting Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, expressly identified the entities eligible for reciprocity: other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces. The legislature did not include Indian tribes.

The plaintiff's argument that the exclusion of tribes defeats the statute's purpose conflates two distinct concepts. The purpose of promoting highway use is achieved through reciprocal agreements with entities that can reciprocate in meaningful ways. The legislature made a deliberate policy choice to limit reciprocity to those jurisdictions with which Minnesota has entered into formal agreements. This Court in *Lund v. Hennepin County*, 276 Minn. 340, 150 N.W.2d 185, 188 (1967), emphasized that "the function of the judiciary is to interpret and apply the law, not to make law." The district court erred by creating a "comparable governing entities" test that effectively rewrote the statute to include an entity the legislature explicitly excluded.

### B. The Plaintiff's Reliance on Purpose Over Text Is Misplaced

The plaintiff argues that because the Band's ordinance functions "identically" to the regulatory schemes of Wisconsin, Ontario, and the District of Columbia, the Band should receive the same reciprocity treatment. This argument ignores the critical distinction between statutory text and legislative purpose.

The Minnesota Legislature knew how to include Indian tribes when it wished to do so. It chose not to. In *Alaska v. Custom Aircraft Servs., Inc.*, 527 P.2d 713, 716 (Alaska 1974), the Alaska Supreme Court held that a statute listing specific entities excludes those not mentioned. This principle applies with equal force here. The legislature's specific enumeration demonstrates a deliberate policy choice to extend reciprocity only to those jurisdictions with which Minnesota has entered into formal agreements.

The plaintiff's argument that excluding the Band "defeats the law's intent" proves too much. The legislature's intent was to extend reciprocity to specific entities, not to all entities that might theoretically further highway use. If the plaintiff believes the statute should include tribes, the appropriate remedy is legislative, not judicial.

---

## III. FEDERAL PREEMPTION DOES NOT REQUIRE RECIPROCITY

### A. Tribal Sovereignty Does Not Extend to State Highways

The plaintiff invokes tribal sovereignty to argue that Minnesota's refusal to grant reciprocity unconstitutionally burdens the Band's governmental authority. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the nature of tribal sovereignty and the scope of federal preemption.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that tribal sovereignty is at its peak within the reservation and diminishes as tribal members and activities move beyond reservation boundaries. In *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Comm'n*, 411 U.S. 164, 174 (1973), the Court recognized that states may impose taxes on tribal members earning income outside the reservation. Similarly, in *Mescalero Apache Tribe v. Jones*, 411 U.S. 145, 148-49 (1973), the Court held that tribal sovereignty extends to "matters of internal tribal relations" but does not preclude state regulation of activities occurring outside the reservation.

The Band's ordinance by its own terms applies to "public streets or highways of the Red Lake reservation." The Band regulates vehicles within its territory. Minnesota regulates vehicles on state highways. These are distinct regulatory spheres. The Band's sovereignty to regulate on its reservation remains intact and undiminished. Minnesota's requirement that vehicles obtain state registration for travel on state highways does not interfere with the Band's internal governance.

### B. The Plaintiff Misapplies Precedent on State Taxation of Tribal Activities

The plaintiff cites *McClanahan* and *Mescalero Apache* for the proposition that tribal sovereignty preempts state authority. These cases are inapposite. Both involved state interference with tribal activities occurring on the reservation. Here, the State is regulating vehicles operating on state highways—which are not part of the reservation.

In *Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation*, 515 U.S. 450, 458 (1995), the Supreme Court recognized that motor vehicle fees are regulatory in nature and that "the core of tribal immunity from state taxation requires congressional preemption." Congress has not exercised its power to preempt state motor vehicle registration requirements for tribal members traveling on state highways. The plaintiff cannot rely on federal preemption to invalidate state registration requirements when Congress has not acted to preempt those requirements.

The plaintiff argues that requiring Band members to pay state registration fees constitutes an impermissible tax. This argument fails. Motor vehicle registration fees are regulatory fees, not taxes, and serve the legitimate state interest in highway safety and revenue. The Supreme Court has upheld similar fees against tribal sovereignty challenges. *See Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation*, 515 U.S. at 458.

---

## IV. THE STATE'S INTERESTS JUSTIFY THE DIFFERENCE IN TREATMENT

### A. The Band's Authority Remains Undiluted

The plaintiff argues that denying reciprocity "significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the governmental authority exercised" by the Tribal Council. This argument is speculative and unsupported by the record.

The Band's authority to regulate motor vehicles on the reservation remains exclusive and undiminished. The Band may continue to enforce its ordinance, issue titles, and collect fees within its territory. The Band's registration license serves a distinct purpose: regulation within the reservation. Minnesota's requirement that Band members obtain state registration for travel on state highways does not invalidate the Band's registration or undermine the Band's sovereignty. It simply reflects the reality that vehicles traveling on state highways are subject to state jurisdiction.

The plaintiff's argument that the Band's license is made "less worthy" ignores the functional distinction between registration for on-reservation use and registration for use on state highways. The Band's license remains fully effective for its intended purpose. The State's requirement in no way diminishes the Band's authority to govern its own roads.

### B. The State Has Substantial Interests in Highway Safety and Revenue

The plaintiff dismisses the State's interests in highway safety and revenue as insufficient to justify the difference in treatment. This argument ignores the reality of highway regulation.

Minnesota has a substantial interest in ensuring that vehicles using its highways are properly registered, identifiable, and subject to safety standards. The State must be able to track vehicles, enforce traffic laws, and collect fees for highway maintenance. These interests are legitimate and substantial.

The plaintiff argues that because Minnesota grants reciprocity to Canada and Wisconsin—entities that impose no meaningful burden on safety or revenue—the State cannot justify excluding the Band. This argument confuses the analysis. The question is not whether the State could extend reciprocity to additional entities, but whether the State is required to do so. The legislature determined that reciprocity with certain entities serves the State's interests. It did not determine that reciprocity with tribes serves those interests.

The plaintiff argues that the Band's ordinance "mirrors" the systems of reciprocating jurisdictions and therefore should receive the same treatment. But the mere similarity of regulatory schemes does not create a legal requirement of reciprocity. The legislature determined which entities would receive reciprocity based on factors including the ability to enter into formal agreements and the reciprocity of treatment. The Band is not similarly situated to foreign provinces or sister states for these purposes.

---

## V. THE DEFENSE RESPONDS TO SPECIFIC PLAINTIFF ARGUMENTS

### A. On the "Comparable Governing Entities" Test

The plaintiff argues that the "comparable governing entities" test is supported by precedent. This is incorrect. The district court created this test out of whole cloth. No Minnesota statute authorizes the extension of reciprocity to comparable governing entities not expressly listed. The district court's creation of this test was precisely the "judicial legislation" that this Court correctly identified in its ruling.

### B. On the Claim of "Hierarchy Among Sovereigns"

The plaintiff argues that denying reciprocity creates an improper "hierarchy among sovereign governments." This argument is overdrawn. Minnesota has not denied the Band's sovereignty. The Band remains free to govern its internal affairs, including motor vehicle registration within the reservation. Minnesota has simply declined to extend a statutory benefit to the Band that the legislature did not authorize. This is not a diminishment of sovereignty; it is the ordinary operation of our federal system.

### C. On the Claim of "Arbitrary and Capricious" Treatment

The plaintiff argues that excluding the Band based on its tribal status is "arbitrary and capricious." This argument fails because the distinction is not based on tribal status per se, but on the legislature's determination that reciprocity should extend only to entities with which Minnesota has formal agreements. The Band is free to seek legislative action to include tribes within the reciprocity framework. The State's refusal to judicially extend the statute is not arbitrary.

---

## VI. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the State of Minnesota respectfully requests that this Court affirm its ruling reversing the district court's order. The plaintiff has failed to demonstrate that Minnesota's reciprocity statutes require the extension of benefits to the Red Lake Band, that federal preemption compels such an extension, or that the State's interests in highway safety and revenue are insufficient to justify the difference in treatment.

The Band's sovereignty within its reservation remains intact. The Band may continue to regulate motor vehicles on its roads, issue titles, and collect fees. Minnesota's requirement that vehicles obtain state registration for travel on state highways does not diminish this authority. The plaintiff's attempt to transform the denial of a statutory benefit into a constitutional violation must fail.

The judgment of the district court was properly reversed. This Court should affirm that reversal.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians (the "Band") and the State of Minnesota (the "State"). The Band appeals the district court’s order granting declaratory judgment and permanent injunction, which required the State to extend motor vehicle registration reciprocity to the Band. In our previous ruling, we reversed the district court, holding that the court had engaged in judicial legislation and that federal preemption did not require the State to recognize tribal plates on state highways. Having reviewed the parties’ rebuttal arguments, we find no reason to disturb our prior decision. The arguments presented by the Band fail to overcome the clear statutory text and the well-established limits of tribal sovereignty regarding off-reservation activities.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The District Court’s "Comparable Governing Entities" Test Constitutes Judicial Legislation**

The Band argues that the district court’s "comparable governing entities" test is a valid interpretation of Minnesota’s reciprocity statutes, Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, designed to effectuate the legislature’s goal of promoting the "fullest possible use of its highway system." The Band contends that excluding tribes while including foreign provinces and states renders the statute arbitrary.

We disagree. The principle of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*—the expression of one thing implies the exclusion of others—remains controlling. In *State v. Minnesota Mutual Life Insurance Co.*, 205 Minn. 138, 285 N.W. 466, 469 (1939), this Court held that "the mention of one thing implies the exclusion of another." The Minnesota Legislature expressly enumerated the entities eligible for reciprocity: other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces. The legislature’s decision not to include Indian tribes is a deliberate policy choice.

The Band’s reliance on the statute’s purpose to override this specific text is misplaced. While the purpose of the statute is to encourage highway use, the legislature determined that the best method to achieve this was through formal reciprocity agreements with specific categories of jurisdictions. As noted in *Alaska v. Custom Aircraft Servs., Inc.*, 527 P.2d 713, 716 (Alaska 1974), a statute listing specific entities excludes those not mentioned. To expand the statute to include tribes, as the district court did, is to usurp the legislative function. In *Lund v. Hennepin County*, 276 Minn. 340, 150 N.W.2d 185, 188 (1967), we emphasized that "the function of the judiciary is to interpret and apply the law, not to make law." The district court’s creation of a "comparable governing entities" test to include an entity the legislature chose to exclude is precisely the type of judicial legislation we cannot countenance.

**B. Tribal Sovereignty Does Not Preempt State Regulation of State Highways**

The Band argues that denying reciprocity "significantly dilutes" its governmental authority and violates principles of tribal sovereignty established in *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Comm'n*, 411 U.S. 164 (1973), and *Mescalero Apache Tribe v. Jones*, 411 U.S. 145 (1973). These cases, however, involve state interference with tribal activities occurring *on the reservation*. Here, the State is regulating vehicles operating on *state highways*.

Tribal sovereignty is inherently territorial. The Band’s ordinance applies to "public streets or highways of the Red lake reservation." The State’s registration requirements apply to vehicles traveling on state highways. These are distinct regulatory spheres. The Band’s authority to regulate vehicles on the reservation remains exclusive and undiminished. The State’s requirement that vehicles using its highways be registered with the State does not interfere with the Band’s internal governance.

The Band’s reliance on *Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation*, 515 U.S. 450 (1995), to argue that the State’s refusal is "arbitrary and capricious" is unpersuasive. In *Chickasaw Nation*, the Supreme Court recognized that motor vehicle fees are regulatory in nature and that "the core of tribal immunity from state taxation requires congressional preemption." 515 U.S. at 458. Congress has not exercised its power to preempt state motor vehicle registration requirements for tribal members traveling on state highways. Without congressional preemption, the State’s refusal to grant reciprocity does not violate federal law.

**C. The State’s Interests Justify the Difference in Treatment**

The Band argues that the State’s interests in highway safety and revenue are insufficient to justify the disparity in treatment because the Band’s ordinance "mirrors" those of other reciprocating jurisdictions. However, the question is not whether the State *could* extend reciprocity to the Band, but whether the State is *required* to do so.

The State has a substantial interest in ensuring that vehicles using its highways are properly registered, identifiable, and subject to safety standards. The legislature determined that reciprocity with certain entities serves these interests. The Band’s argument that its license is made "less worthy" ignores the functional distinction between registration for on-reservation use and registration for use on state highways. The Band’s license remains fully effective for its intended purpose within the reservation. The State’s requirement in no way diminishes the Band’s authority to govern its own roads.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Band has failed to demonstrate that the district court’s judicial expansion of Minnesota’s reciprocity statutes was permissible. The plain language of the statutes limits reciprocity to the entities expressly listed by the legislature. Furthermore, federal preemption does not require the State to exempt tribal members from motor vehicle registration requirements when traveling on state highways. The State’s substantial interests in highway safety and revenue justify the distinction drawn by the legislature.

Accordingly, we affirm our previous ruling reversing the order of the district court.
