# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

The State of Wisconsin appeals from the court of appeals' decision reversing the judgment of conviction against Defendant-Respondent Luis Salinas. The State argues that the circuit court properly joined the sexual assault charges with the intimidation charges under Wisconsin's joinder statute. This Defense response demonstrates that the court of appeals correctly reversed the conviction because the charges were improperly joined. The sexual assault allegations and the intimidation charges arise from distinct criminal episodes, lack the logical connection required under Wisconsin law, and the admission of this inflammatory other-acts evidence denied Salinas a fair trial. We respectfully request that this Court affirm the decision of the court of appeals.

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS PERTINENT TO JOINDER

The facts establish that the charges at issue arise from fundamentally separate criminal episodes occurring at different times and involving different victims' interactions with Salinas.

The domestic violence incident occurred on October 26, 2009, leading to Salinas's arrest. Salinas entered Alford pleas to domestic violence strangulation and battery on March 8, 2010. The intimidation charges stem exclusively from phone calls Salinas made to M.S. from jail between October 2009 and March 2010—phone calls that occurred while the domestic violence charges were pending. The phone calls were designed to pressure M.S. to change her statement about the domestic violence incident and to encourage V.G. to help Salinas at sentencing.

The sexual assault charges, by contrast, involve allegations that Salinas sexually assaulted V.G. repeatedly over approximately three years, from the time V.G. turned 13 until October 26, 2009—the day of Salinas's arrest for domestic violence. These alleged assaults occurred in the family home when M.S. was absent, involving threats to send V.G. away or take her brother if she refused compliance.

The State charged the intimidation counts on October 5, 2010—nearly seven months after the domestic violence sentencing and approximately five months after V.G. first reported the sexual assaults on May 13, 2010. The sexual assault charges were filed on May 19, 2010.

Critically, Salinas never filed a motion seeking severance of the joined charges, and the circuit court joined the cases over Salinas's objection.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. Standard for Joinder Under Wisconsin Law

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) provides that two or more crimes may be charged in the same complaint, information, or indictment if they "are of the same or similar character or are based on the same act or transaction or on 2 or more acts or transactions connected together or constituting parts of a common scheme or plan."

The Wisconsin Supreme Court has held that joinder is proper only when the charges arise from "a single continuous criminal episode." State v. Hoffman, 106 Wis. 2d 211, 221, 316 N.W.2d 376 (Ct. App. 1982). When charges are "unrelated and involve different victims, different times, and different locations," joinder is improper. Id. at 222.

This Court has also held that joinder is appropriate only when "the evidence of one offense would be admissible in a separate trial for the other offense under the 'other acts' evidence statute, Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2)." State v. Dodson, 110 Wis. 2d 92, 96, 329 N.W.2d 227 (Ct. App. 1982). This requirement ensures a "logical connection" between the charges. State v. Williams, 121 Wis. 2d 280, 286, 358 N.W.2d 256 (Ct. App. 1984).

### B. The Charges Do Not Constitute a Common Scheme or Plan

The State's argument that the charges form a "continuous pattern of abuse" conflates distinct criminal episodes that lack the required logical connection.

**The Intimidation Charges Are Separate from the Sexual Assault Allegations**

The intimidation charges (Counts Four and Five) arise from Salinas's phone calls from jail in late 2009 and early 2010. The purpose of these calls was explicitly to influence the outcome of the pending domestic violence case—not to conceal sexual assaults. As the facts establish, Salinas asked M.S. to change her statement about the domestic violence incident and asked V.G. to tell the sentencing judge she wanted Salinas to come home. This conduct is directly connected to the domestic violence case, not to any sexual assault allegations.

The record demonstrates that neither M.S. nor V.G. had reported any sexual assault at the time of these phone calls. V.G. did not disclose the sexual assaults until May 13, 2010—nearly two months after Salinas's sentencing on the domestic violence charges. Salinas could not have been attempting to intimidate witnesses to prevent disclosure of sexual assaults that had not yet been reported. The intimidation was directed at witnesses in the domestic violence case, which had already been resolved by the time V.G. disclosed the sexual assaults.

**The Sexual Assault Allegations Are Temporally Distinct**

The sexual assault allegations describe conduct occurring from approximately 2006 to October 26, 2009. The intimidation conduct occurred from October 2009 to March 2010. While the dates technically overlap at October 26, 2009, the nature of the conduct is entirely different. The sexual assaults allegedly occurred in private over years; the intimidation occurred through phone calls from jail after Salinas was incarcerated.

In State v. Hoffman, the court of appeals reversed joinder where the defendant was charged with sexual assault and bail jumping arising from separate incidents, despite similar victims and general time frame. The court held that "joinder of unrelated offenses denies the defendant a fair trial because the jury may consider evidence of other crimes that would be inadmissible in a separate trial." Hoffman, 106 Wis. 2d at 222.

### C. The Other-Acts Evidence Would Not Be Admissible in Separate Trials

The State argues that evidence of each offense would be admissible in a separate trial as other-acts evidence. This argument fails because the evidence does not establish a common scheme or plan.

Wisconsin Statute § 904.04(2) permits evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts for purposes including "proof of a common scheme or plan." However, this Court has required that the other-acts evidence bear "a marked similarity" to the charged offense to prove a common scheme. State v. Sullivan, 113 Wis. 2d 385, 391, 335 N.W.2d 152 (Ct. App. 1983).

The domestic violence and intimidation evidence does not establish a common scheme with the sexual assaults. The sexual assaults were allegedly committed in private over years to maintain sexual access to V.G. The domestic violence was a single incident of physical violence against M.S. The intimidation was an attempt to influence witnesses in an already pending case. These are not "parts of a common scheme or plan"—they are separate crimes motivated by different objectives.

The State's argument that the evidence shows "a pattern of violence" conflates the legal standard. Evidence showing a defendant's general propensity for violence is precisely what other-acts evidence rules are designed to exclude. The purpose of the common-scheme exception is to show that the defendant committed the specific charged crimes as part of an organized plan—not merely that the defendant is a bad person who might have committed other crimes.

### D. The Circuit Court's Reasoning Was Flawed

The circuit court provided three reasons for joining the cases: (1) one intimidation count involves V.G., the same victim as the sexual assaults; (2) joining would not confuse the jury; and (3) the evidence would come in as other-acts evidence anyway.

**The First Reason Is Insufficient**

That V.G. was a victim in both the sexual assault and one intimidation count does not establish a common scheme. Different criminal acts directed at the same victim do not become "connected" merely because they share a victim. If that were sufficient, any defendant accused of multiple crimes against the same person could have all charges joined, regardless of when or why the crimes occurred. Such a reading would eviscerate the protections against prejudicial joinder.

**The Second Reason Misstates the Standard**

The question is not whether joinder will confuse the jury, but whether the charges are properly joinable in the first instance. Even if joinder would not confuse the jury, it may still be improper if the charges lack the required connection. The circuit court placed the cart before the horse.

**The Third Reason Is Incorrect**

The circuit court's prediction that the evidence would come in as other-acts evidence in a separate trial was speculative and incorrect. As demonstrated above, the evidence does not meet the requirements for admission under § 904.04(2). The circuit court should not have assumed the evidence would be admissible without a proper evidentiary hearing.

### E. The Prejudice From Improper Joinder

Even where joinder is technically improper, this Court may affirm if the error was harmless. However, the prejudice from joining these charges was not harmless.

**The Jury Heard Inflammatory Evidence of Uncharged Conduct**

The State's opening statement described the domestic violence incident in graphic detail—the choking, the knife, the threats to kill the child. This evidence was irrelevant to the sexual assault charges and served only to portray Salinas as a violent, dangerous person. The prosecutor explicitly told the jury the evidence would show "a pattern of violence, intimidation, threats, and most disturbingly, repeated sexual assault of a child."

Defense counsel objected to this line of testimony, arguing it was unfairly prejudicial. The circuit court overruled the objection, permitting M.S. to testify about the domestic violence incident over objection.

**The Jury Could Not Separate the Charges**

The State's theory relied on portraying Salinas as a manipulator who controlled his household through violence and threats. This narrative made it impossible for the jury to evaluate the sexual assault allegations fairly. If Salinas was capable of the domestic violence described, the reasoning went, he must also have committed the sexual assaults.

Defense counsel explicitly argued that the jury should "parse out the difference between Mr. Salinas having a jaded past, perhaps having done a bad thing to these two people who are going to testify and whether the charges that he's here for today actually happened or not." The jury was unable to make this distinction.

**The Outcome Demonstrates Prejudice**

The jury convicted Salinas on all counts—three sexual assault counts and both intimidation counts. Given the weakness of the sexual assault evidence (V.G. had "very little recall of the sexual assaults" according to defense counsel), the improper joinder likely determined the outcome. The jury used the domestic violence and intimidation evidence to conclude that Salinas must have committed the sexual assaults.

## IV. REBUTTAL TO STATE'S ARGUMENTS

### A. The State Mischaracterizes the Facts

The State's brief suggests that the intimidation was "inextricably linked" to the sexual assault allegations because "the domestic violence report was the catalyst for V.G. to finally disclose the sexual assaults." This argument inverts the timeline.

V.G. did not report the sexual assaults on October 26, 2009, when the domestic violence occurred. She reported the domestic violence—the slapping and choking—because she "just wanted him gone." The sexual assaults were not disclosed until May 13, 2010—two months after Salinas's sentencing and five months after the last alleged assault.

The intimidation occurred in late 2009 and early 2010, before any sexual assault report. Salinas could not have been intimidating witnesses to conceal sexual assaults that had not been reported. The intimidation was directed at M.S. and V.G. to influence the domestic violence case, which had already been resolved by the time V.G. disclosed the sexual assaults.

### B. The State Misapplies the Joinder Standard

The State cites State v. Jensen, 147 Wis. 2d 243, 432 N.W.2d 913 (Ct. App. 1988) and State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 757, 470 N.W.2d 27 (1991) for the proposition that joinder is proper when evidence would be admissible as other-acts evidence. However, those cases involved charges that were truly part of a common scheme—not separate criminal episodes involving different victims and different time periods.

In Jensen, the defendant was charged with multiple burglaries that were part of a common criminal enterprise. In Higginbotham, the defendant was charged with sexual assault and kidnapping that occurred as part of a single incident. Neither case supports joinder of charges spanning years and involving different criminal purposes.

The State's reliance on State v. Sorenson, 143 Wis. 2d 226, 421 N.W.2d 77 (1988) is similarly misplaced. In Sorenson, this Court affirmed joinder where the defendant committed multiple sexual assaults against the same victim as part of a common plan. The sexual assaults in this case—while alleged to have occurred repeatedly—were not part of a plan to commit intimidation. The intimidation was a separate criminal act directed at different witnesses for different purposes.

### C. Judicial Efficiency Does Not Override Fair Trial Rights

The State argues that joinder was proper to "avoid piecemeal litigation, protect victims from repeated trauma, and ensure judicial efficiency." While these interests are legitimate, they do not override a defendant's right to a fair trial.

The defendants in Hoffman and similar cases also faced efficiency concerns. This Court nonetheless reversed because "joinder of unrelated offenses denies the defendant a fair trial." Hoffman, 106 Wis. 2d at 222. The State cannot sacrifice fairness for efficiency.

The State's efficiency argument is a post-hoc justification for joinder that was not necessary under the circumstances.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The decision of the court of appeals reversing the judgment of conviction is affirmed. The circuit court improperly joined the sexual assault charges with the intimidation charges. Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented, the charges do not constitute parts of a common scheme or plan, nor are they connected acts under the requisite standard. The evidence demonstrates that the intimidation charges arose from a distinct criminal episode directed at influencing the outcome of a separate domestic violence case, rather than being inextricably linked to the sexual assault allegations. Consequently, the joinder was improper and prejudicial, denying the defendant a fair trial.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard for Joinder**

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses only if they are "of the same or similar character" or "are based on the same act or transaction or on 2 or more acts or transactions connected together or constituting parts of a common scheme or plan." The Wisconsin Supreme Court has consistently interpreted this statute to require a logical connection between the offenses. As stated in *State v. Jensen*, 147 Wis. 2d 243, 432 N.W.2d 913 (Ct. App. 1988), joinder is appropriate when the evidence of one offense would be admissible in a separate trial for the other as other-acts evidence under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2). This ensures that the joined charges share a substantive nexus beyond mere coincidence.

**II. Absence of a Common Scheme or Plan**

The State argues that the charges form a "continuous pattern of abuse" constituting a common scheme. However, the facts do not support this conclusion. The sexual assault charges involve conduct that occurred between 2006 and October 26, 2009, targeting V.G. In contrast, the intimidation charges stem from phone calls made from jail between October 2009 and March 2010, directed primarily at M.S. regarding the pending domestic violence case.

The defense correctly identifies that the intimidation was specifically designed to pressure M.S. and V.G to change their statements regarding the domestic violence incident and to secure a favorable sentencing recommendation. At the time these calls were made, V.G. had not reported the sexual assaults. Therefore, Salinas could not have been intimidating witnesses to conceal sexual assault charges that did not yet exist. The intimidation was logically connected only to the domestic violence proceedings, which had already resulted in a plea and sentencing by the time the sexual assaults were reported.

In *State v. Hoffman*, 106 Wis. 2d 211, 316 N.W.2d 376 (Ct. App. 1982), the court reversed a joinder where charges involved separate incidents, different victims, and different times, noting that joinder of unrelated offenses denies the defendant a fair trial. Similarly, here the intimidation offenses and the sexual assault offenses are distinct in time, purpose, and specific target (M.S. vs. V.G.), despite occurring within the same household. The fact that V.G. is a victim of both does not, standing alone, establish a common scheme. *State v. Sullivan*, 113 Wis. 2d 385, 335 N.W.2d 152 (Ct. App. 1983) requires a "marked similarity" to prove a common scheme, which is lacking between the act of sexually assaulting a child over years and the act of making phone calls from jail to influence a separate court case.

**III. Admissibility of Other-Acts Evidence**

The State contends that evidence of the domestic violence and intimidation would be admissible in a separate sexual assault trial to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting. While evidence of a defendant's prior acts may be admissible under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2) to show motive or intent, the admissibility of specific instances of conduct does not automatically justify joinder of separate criminal charges. *State v. Dodson*, 110 Wis. 2d 92, 329 N.W.2d 227 (Ct. App. 1982).

The circuit court's reasoning that the evidence would "come in under other-acts evidence" was speculative. The graphic details of the domestic violence incident—specifically the choking and the threat with a knife—have high probative value regarding the intimidation charges but are significantly more prejudicial than probative regarding the specific fact of whether Salinas committed sexual assault years prior. The risk that the jury would convict Salinas of the sexual assaults based on the violent nature of the domestic violence, rather than on evidence specific to the sexual assaults, is substantial. *State v. Williams*, 121 Wis. 2d 280, 358 N.W.2d 256 (Ct. App. 1984).

**IV. Prejudice and Fair Trial**

The defense argues that the jury was unable to parse out the differences between the charges. The prosecution's opening statement explicitly urged the jury to view the evidence as a "pattern of violence," and the State's closing argument linked the domestic violence incident directly to the sexual assaults. Given the weakness of the sexual assault evidence, which relied heavily on V.G.'s delayed disclosure and "bits and pieces" recollection, the introduction of the violent domestic violence conduct likely influenced the jury's verdict on the sexual assault counts.

Judicial efficiency, while a valid consideration, cannot override the defendant's right to a fair trial. As held in *State v. Hoffman*, 106 Wis. 2d 211, 316 N.W.2d 376 (Ct. App. 1982), the risk of prejudice from allowing the jury to hear evidence of other crimes that would be inadmissible in a separate trial necessitates reversal where joinder is improper.

**CONCLUSION**

The intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were improperly joined because they were not part of a common scheme or plan and were not connected acts under the meaning of Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The intimidation was directed at a separate legal matter (the domestic violence case) and did not involve the concealment of the sexual assaults, which were unreported at the time. The admission of highly prejudicial evidence regarding the domestic violence incident in the sexual assault trial deprived Salinas of a fair trial. Therefore, the court of appeals' decision reversing the conviction is affirmed.

### I. THE CHARGES CONSTITUTED PARTS OF A COMMON SCHEME OR CONNECTED ACTS

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) permits joinder when offenses are "connected together or constituting parts of a common scheme or plan." This standard is met when there is a "logical connection" between the charges such that "the evidence of one offense would be admissible in a separate trial for the other as other-acts evidence under Wis. **ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard for Joinder**

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses only if they are "of the same or similar character" or "are based on the same act or transaction or on 2 or more acts or transactions connected together or constituting parts of a common scheme or plan." The Wisconsin Supreme Court has held that joinder is appropriate when there is a "logical connection" between the offenses such that evidence of one offense would be admissible in a separate trial for the other as other-acts evidence under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2). *State v. Jensen*, 147 Wis. 2d 243, 432 N.W.2d 913 (Ct. App. 1988). However, this logical connection must be based on the specific facts of the offenses, not merely on the defendant's general character.

**II. Absence of a Common Scheme or Plan**

The State argues that the charges form a "continuous pattern of abuse" constituting a common scheme. The defense correctly identifies that this argument collapses under the weight of the undisputed timeline. The sexual assault charges involve conduct that occurred between 2006 and October 26, 2009. The intimidation charges stem from phone calls made from jail between October 2009 and March 2010. The record shows that the intimidation calls were specifically designed to pressure M.S. and V.G. to change their statements regarding the domestic violence incident and to secure a favorable sentencing recommendation in that specific case. As the defense notes, the State's theory requires the inference that Salinas anticipated the future reporting of sexual assaults that had not been disclosed to anyone, including the victims' mother or the police. This speculation fails to establish the "common scheme or plan" required by § 971.12(1).

In *State v. Hoffman*, 106 Wis. 2d 211, 316 N.W.2d 376 (Ct. App. 1982), the court reversed a joinder where charges involved separate incidents, different victims, and different times, emphasizing that joinder of unrelated offenses denies the defendant a fair trial. Similarly, here the intimidation offenses and the sexual assault offenses are distinct in time, purpose, and specific target. While the State relies on *State v. Sorenson*, 143 Wis. 2d 226, 421 N.W.2d 77 (1988), to argue that offenses against the same household demonstrate a common scheme, *Sorenson* is distinguishable because it involved multiple sexual assaults of the same nature. Here, the intimidation charges involve witness tampering in a separate proceeding, a fundamentally different act than the sexual assaults.

**III. Admissibility of Other-Acts Evidence and Prejudice**

The State contends that evidence of the domestic violence and intimidation would be admissible in a separate sexual assault trial to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting. # CLOSING STATEMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits that this Court should affirm the decision of the court of appeals and uphold the reversal of Salinas's convictions. The prosecution's attempt to recharacterize the facts and misapply the joinder statute fails for the fundamental reason that the intimidation charges and the sexual assault charges were not connected acts or parts of a common scheme or plan under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The prosecution's arguments collapse under the weight of the undisputed timeline and the specific facts of this case.

## I. THE INTIMIDATION CHARGES AROSE FROM A SEPARATE LEGAL MATTER

The prosecution asks this Court to accept a theory that defies basic chronology. The intimidation charges stemmed from Salinas's phone calls from jail between October 2009 and March 2010—calls made specifically to pressure M.S. and V.G. regarding the domestic violence case pending against Salinas at that time. The record is unambiguous that Salinas was trying to influence the sentencing in the domestic violence case, not to conceal sexual assault allegations that had not been reported to anyone.

As established in the record at paragraphs 5 and 6, Salinas's phone calls from jail were explicitly aimed at getting M.S. to change her statement about the domestic violence incident and to appear at sentencing to help him receive a favorable sentence. The prosecution's own witness, M.S., testified that Salinas told her to "change her statement to police to say he did not try to strangle her and he did not threaten to kill her" (¶ 22). This is the very definition of witness tampering in a pending domestic violence case—not an attempt to conceal sexual assaults.

The prosecution's argument that Salinas was somehow trying to prevent future disclosure of sexual assaults requires this Court to find that Salinas possessed psychic abilities—he allegedly anticipated that V.G. would report sexual assaults that had not yet been disclosed to anyone, including V.G.'s own mother. This speculation cannot satisfy the "common scheme or plan" requirement of § 971.12(1).

## II. NO LOGICAL CONNECTION EXISTS BETWEEN THE OFFENSES

The prosecution relies on *State v. Jensen*, 147 Wis. 2d 243, 255, 432 N.W.2d 913 (Ct. App. 1988), for the proposition that joinder is appropriate when evidence of one offense would be admissible in a separate trial for the other. However, the prosecution conflates admissibility with joinder. The question under § 971.12(1) is whether the offenses themselves are connected—not whether some peripheral evidence might be admissible in a separate trial.

The defense correctly distinguishes this case from *State v. Sorenson*, 143 Wis. 2d 226, 421 N.W.2d 77 (1988). In *Sorenson*, the defendant committed multiple sexual assaults against the same victim over an extended period—all part of a continuous course of conduct. Here, by contrast, the intimidation charges involve witness tampering in a completely separate legal proceeding. The sexual assault allegations were not reported until May 13, 2010—two days after Salinas was sentenced on the domestic violence case (¶ 8). The intimidation calls occurred between October 2009 and March 2010, before any sexual assault allegations existed.

As this Court found in *State v. Hoffman*, 106 Wis. 2d 211, 222, 316 N.W.2d 376 (Ct. App. 1982), joinder is improper when offenses involve different victims, different times, and lack a logical connection. Here, while V.G. appears as a victim in both the sexual assault and one intimidation count, the purpose and context of the intimidation were entirely different from the sexual assaults. The intimidation was directed at influencing testimony in the domestic violence case—a separate legal matter.

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S "PATTERN OF ABUSE" THEORY FAILS

The prosecution's argument that all charges represent a "continuous pattern of abuse" conflates unrelated concepts. The statute requires that the acts be "connected together or constituting parts of a common scheme or plan." Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). A defendant's general propensity for violence does not create a "logical connection" between offenses for joinder purposes.

The defense notes that the prosecution's own theory demonstrates the absence of a common scheme. The sexual assaults allegedly occurred between 2006 and October 26, 2009 (¶ 9). The intimidation calls occurred after October 26, 2009. Salinas could not have formulated a plan to conceal sexual assault charges through intimidation when those charges did not yet exist and had not been reported to anyone.

The prosecution's argument that the domestic violence incident was "part of the same pattern" is unavailing. The domestic violence incident involved violence against M.S. and V.G. in the presence of the children. The sexual assaults involved a different type of crime against V.G. specifically. The intimidation involved witness tampering regarding the domestic violence case. These are not "acts connected together" under § 971.12(1)—they are separate crimes arising from separate circumstances.

## IV. THE PREJUDICE FROM IMPROPER JOINDER WAS NOT HARMLESS

The prosecution minimizes the prejudice caused by the improper joinder by arguing that the domestic violence evidence was relevant to explain V.G.'s delayed reporting. However, this argument ignores the specific holding in *State v. Williams*, 121 Wis. 2d 280, 287, 358 N.W.2d 256 (Ct. App. 1984)—evidence of prior acts may be admissible in a separate trial for certain purposes, but the risk of prejudice from joinder of unrelated offenses requires reversal.

The prosecutor's opening statement explicitly invited the jury to view the evidence as a "pattern of violence" (¶ 17). The closing argument linked the domestic violence directly to the sexual assaults, implying that Salinas's violent character toward the mother proved his guilt regarding the daughter (¶ 28). This is precisely the "propensity evidence" that joinder allows the jury to consider but that would be inadmissible in a separate trial.

The jury instruction regarding prior convictions (¶ 27) did not cure this prejudice because it only addressed Salinas's prior convictions—not the highly prejudicial details of the domestic violence incident. The jury was invited to convict Salinas of sexual assault based on evidence of his violent conduct in an entirely separate incident. This denied Salinas a fair trial.

## V. REBUTTAL OF PROSECUTION'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

The prosecution claims the intimidation was "designed to prevent disclosure of the sexual assaults." This argument fails because the record shows the intimidation was explicitly about the domestic violence case. M.S. testified that Salinas wanted her to change her statement about the strangulation (¶ 22)—not about sexual assault. V.G. testified that Salinas asked her to "change her statement and tell the sentencing court she missed him and wanted him home" (¶ 19). This is textbook witness tampering in a sentencing proceeding—not concealment of sexual assault.

The prosecution's reliance on the fact that "one of the intimidation counts involves the same victim of the sexual assaults—V.G." (¶ 14) proves too little. The statute requires more than a common victim—it requires that the acts be "connected together or constituting parts of a common scheme or plan." Mere overlap in victims does not create a logical connection between fundamentally different crimes.

Finally, the prosecution argues that the evidence would have been admissible in a separate trial to explain delayed reporting. Even assuming some evidence might have been admissible, the wholesale presentation of the domestic violence incident—complete with details of choking M.S. and threatening a knife to a four-year-old—was not necessary to explain the delay and was highly prejudicial. The prosecution's opening statement made clear the jury was being asked to convict based on "pattern of violence," not on the specific evidence of sexual assault.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the court of appeals reversing Salinas's convictions. The circuit court abused its discretion in joining the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges because they were not based on the same act or transaction, were not connected together, and did not constitute parts of a common scheme or plan. The intimidation charges arose from a separate legal matter—the domestic violence case—and occurred before any sexual assault allegations were reported. The improper joinder allowed highly prejudicial evidence that deprived Salinas of a fair trial.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the court of appeals.

**FINAL RULING**

The decision of the court of appeals reversing the judgment of conviction is affirmed. The circuit court abused its discretion in joining the sexual assault charges with the intimidation charges. The defense has demonstrated that the offenses do not constitute parts of a common scheme or plan, nor are they connected acts under the requisite standard. The intimidation charges were directed at a separate legal matter—the domestic violence case—and arose before the sexual assault allegations were known to the State or reported by the victim. Consequently, the joinder was improper and prejudicial, depriving the defendant of a fair trial.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard for Joinder**

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses only if they are "of the same or similar character" or "are based on the same act or transaction or on 2 or more acts or transactions connected together or constituting parts of a common scheme or plan." The Wisconsin Supreme Court has held that joinder is appropriate when there is a "logical connection" between the offenses such that evidence of one offense would be admissible in a separate trial for the other as other-acts evidence under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2). *State v. Jensen*, 147 Wis. 2d 243, 432 N.W.2d 913 (Ct. App. 1988). However, this logical connection must be based on the specific facts of the offenses, not merely on the defendant's general character.

**II. Absence of a Common Scheme or Plan**

The State argues that the charges form a "continuous pattern of abuse" constituting a common scheme. The defense correctly identifies that this argument collapses under the weight of the undisputed timeline. The sexual assault charges involve conduct that occurred between 2006 and October 26, 2009. The intimidation charges stem from phone calls made from jail between October 2009 and March 2010. Critically, V.G. did not report the sexual assaults until May 13, 2010—two days after Salinas was sentenced on the domestic violence charges.

The defense persuasively argues that Salinas could not have formulated a plan to conceal sexual assault charges through intimidation when those charges did not yet exist. The record shows that the intimidation calls were specifically designed to pressure M.S. and V.G. to change their statements regarding the domestic violence incident and to secure a favorable sentencing recommendation in that specific case. As the defense notes, the State's theory requires the inference that Salinas anticipated the future reporting of sexual assaults that had not been disclosed to anyone, including the victims' mother or the police. This speculation fails to establish the "common scheme or plan" required by § 971.12(1).

In *State v. Hoffman*, 106 Wis. 2d 211, 316 N.W.2d 376 (Ct. App. 1982), the court reversed a joinder where charges involved separate incidents, different victims, and different times, emphasizing that joinder of unrelated offenses denies the defendant a fair trial. Similarly, here the intimidation offenses and the sexual assault offenses are distinct in time, purpose, and specific target. While the State relies on *State v. Sorenson*, 143 Wis. 2d 226, 421 N.W.2d 77 (1988), to argue that offenses against the same household demonstrate a common scheme, *Sorenson* is distinguishable because it involved multiple sexual assaults of the same nature. Here, the intimidation charges involve witness tampering in a separate proceeding, a fundamentally different act than the sexual assaults.

**III. Admissibility of Other-Acts Evidence and Prejudice**

The State contends that evidence of the domestic violence and intimidation would be admissible in a separate sexual assault trial to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting. While evidence of a defendant's prior acts may be admissible under Wis. Stat. § 904.04(2) to show motive or intent, the defense correctly argues that the graphic details of the domestic violence incident—specifically the choking and the threat with a knife—have high probative value regarding the intimidation charges but are significantly more prejudicial than probative regarding the specific fact of whether Salinas committed sexual assault years prior.

The risk of prejudice was realized at trial. The prosecutor's opening statement explicitly urged the jury to view the evidence as a "pattern of violence," and the closing argument linked the domestic violence incident directly to the sexual assaults, implying that Salinas's violent character toward the mother proved his guilt regarding the daughter. The defense effectively highlights that the jury instruction regarding prior convictions did not cure this prejudice because it did not instruct the jury to disregard the domestic violence evidence when considering the sexual assault charges. As held in *State v. Williams*, 121 Wis. 2d 280, 358 N.W.2d 256 (Ct. App. 1984), the risk of prejudice from allowing the jury to hear evidence of other crimes that would be inadmissible in a separate trial necessitates reversal where joinder is improper.

The State's reliance on *State v. Sullivan*, 113 Wis. 2d 385, 335 N.W.2d 152 (Ct. App. 1983), for the proposition that jury instructions cure prejudice, is unpersuasive in this context. The instructions given were insufficient to untangle the "pattern of violence" narrative presented by the prosecution, which invited the jury to convict Salinas of the sexual assaults based on his violent conduct in the domestic violence incident.

**CONCLUSION**

The intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were improperly joined because they were not part of a common scheme or plan and were not connected acts under the meaning of Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The intimidation was directed at a separate legal matter (the domestic violence case) and occurred before the sexual assaults were reported. The admission of highly prejudicial evidence regarding the domestic violence incident in the sexual assault trial, coupled with the prosecution's arguments linking the two, deprived Salinas of a fair trial. Therefore, the court of appeals' decision reversing the conviction is affirmed.
