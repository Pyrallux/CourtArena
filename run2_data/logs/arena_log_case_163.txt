**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen v. PACT Charter School**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School. The issue presented is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, creates a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. Appellants argue that the statute, read in context and in light of legislative intent, supports a "proximity preference." Respondent counters that the statute’s plain language is silent regarding residency, and thus, no such preference exists. For the reasons set forth below, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The interpretation of a statute is a question of law reviewed de novo. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 400, 404 (Minn. 2009). Similarly, this Court reviews a district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo. *Boulevard Woods, LLC v. Kmart Corp.*, 790 N.W.2d 873, 877 (Minn. 2010). Summary judgment is appropriate when there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Plain Language of the Statute Controls**

The primary object of statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate the intent of the legislature. *State v. Lie*, 815 N.W.2d 19, 22 (Minn. 2012). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must enforce the statute as written and may not disregard the plain meaning of the statute to pursue the spirit of the law. *City of Roseville v. State*, 831 N.W.2d 746, 750 (Minn. 2013).

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, governs charter school admissions. As argued by Respondent, the statute establishes a lottery system for oversubscribed schools and contains no language explicitly granting a preference to residents of the city where the school is located. The statute mandates that charter schools admit students without regard to geographic location when applications exceed capacity. Because the statutory text is clear and unambiguous regarding the use of a lottery system and the absence of geographic restrictions, the court must apply the statute as written.

Appellants ask this Court to read a "proximity preference" into the statute. However, it is a well-established principle of statutory construction that courts may not add words or provisions that are not present in the statute. *State v. Thiel*, 644 N.W.2d 452, 456 (Minn. 2002). To adopt Appellants’ interpretation would require this Court to insert a geographic preference that the legislature chose to omit. This Court has consistently held that statutes must be applied as written, and courts should not create exceptions not found in the law. *In re Welfare of T.R.*, 567 N.W.2d 47, 50 (Minn. 1997). Therefore, the district court correctly concluded that the plain language of the statute does not support a proximity preference.

**B. The Statutory Scheme and Legislative History Do Not Override the Plain Text**

Appellants argue that the statute must be read in context with the broader statutory scheme and legislative history to reveal a legislative intent to favor local students. While this Court acknowledges that words and phrases must be interpreted in light of their context and the statutory scheme as a whole, *State v. Brooks*, 604 N.W.2d 345, 349 (Minn. 2000), this principle does not permit the court to override clear statutory language.

The statutory scheme cited by Appellants establishes the general purpose of charter schools but does not specifically authorize a residency preference in subdivision 9. Where the legislature has specifically detailed the admissions process in subdivision 9 without mentioning a proximity preference, the court cannot infer such a preference from the general purposes of the act. The specific controls the general. Furthermore, while legislative history may provide persuasive evidence of intent, it cannot be used to contradict the statute's plain meaning. *State v. Lie*, 815 N.W.2d at 22. The district court did not err in disregarding legislative history that sought to import a restriction absent from the statutory text.

**C. The Doctrine of Expressio Unius and Absurd Results**

Appellants contend that the doctrine of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* does not apply because the legislature could not have contemplated the specific circumstances, and that interpreting the statute to exclude a local preference leads to absurd results.

Regarding the doctrine of *expressio unius*, the absence of a provision for city residency in a statute that otherwise comprehensively details the admissions process supports the conclusion that the legislature did not intend to create such a preference. The argument that the legislature failed to contemplate the need for local preferences is speculative. The legislature is presumed to know the law and the consequences of its enactments.

Appellants also rely on the rule that courts should avoid a construction that leads to absurd results. *Wegley v. City of Rochester*, 184 Minn. 138, 142, 238 N.W. 347, 349 (1931). Appellants posit that it is absurd for a charter school to be unable to prioritize local residents. However, the result of a pure lottery system—where admission is based on chance rather than geography—is not absurd as a matter of law; it is a direct consequence of the legislature’s mandate to provide equal access to charter school enrollment regardless of location. The policy arguments advanced by Appellants regarding community support and local priority are matters for legislative consideration, not judicial rewriting. The plain meaning of the statute does not produce an absurd result; it produces a result that Appellants simply find undesirable as a matter of policy.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court correctly determined that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where a charter school is located. The plain language of the statute is unambiguous, and this Court may not judicially insert a proximity preference where the legislature has not seen fit to include one.

Accordingly, the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School is affirmed.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School. The issue presented is whether Minn. **II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The interpretation of a statute is a question of law reviewed de novo. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 400, 404 (Minn. 2009). Similarly, this Court reviews a district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo. *Boulevard Woods, LLC v. Kmart Corp.*, 790 N.W.2d 873, 877 (Minn. 2010). Summary judgment is appropriate when there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Plain Language of the Statute Controls**

The primary object of statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate the intent of the legislature. *State v. Lie*, 815 N.W.2d 19, 22 (Minn. 2012). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must enforce the statute as written and may not disregard the plain meaning of the statute to pursue the spirit of the law. *City of Roseville v. State*, 831 N.W.2d 746, 750 (Minn. 2013).

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, governs charter school admissions. The statute explicitly mandates that if the number of applications exceeds capacity, the school "must conduct a lottery." The statute contains no language explicitly granting a preference to residents of the city where the school is located. Appellants argue that the statute is ambiguous because it is silent regarding residency, citing *A.W. v. Northwest Rabbinical Yeshiva*, 681 N.W.2d 15, 20 (Minn. 2004), for the proposition that silence creates ambiguity. However, the statute’s silence on a specific residency preference does not render the mandatory lottery language ambiguous. Rather, the specific mandate to use a lottery controls the admissions process.

Appellants ask this Court to read a "proximity preference" into the statute. However, it is a well-established principle of statutory construction that courts may not add words or provisions that are not present in the statute. *State v. Thiel*, 644 N.W.2d 452, 456 (Minn. 2002). To adopt Appellants’ interpretation would require this Court to insert a geographic preference that the legislature chose to omit. This Court has consistently held that statutes must be applied as written, and courts should not create exceptions not found in the law. *In re Welfare of T.R.*, 567 N.W.2d 47, 50 (Minn. 1997). Therefore, the district court correctly concluded that the plain language of the statute does not support a proximity preference.

**B. The Statutory Scheme Does Not Override the Plain Text**

Appellants argue that the statute must be read in context with the broader statutory scheme to reveal a legislative intent to favor local students. While this Court acknowledges that words and phrases must be interpreted in light of their context and the statutory scheme as a whole, *Moffett v. Life Ins. Co. of North America*, 720 N.W.2d 820, 824 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), this principle does not permit the court to override clear statutory language.

Appellants rely on *St. Otto's Church v. Minnesota Department of Revenue*, 325 N.W.2d 615, 618 (Minn. 1982), to argue that the statute must be interpreted to give effect to all provisions and avoid internal inconsistency. However, the general purpose provisions cited by Appellants—stating that charter schools are intended to increase learning opportunities and accountability—do not conflict with the specific lottery mandate in subdivision 9. A lottery system is a method of ensuring accountability and equal access. Where the legislature has specifically detailed the admissions process in subdivision 9 without mentioning a proximity preference, the court cannot infer such a preference from the general purposes of the act. The specific controls the general.

**C. Legislative History Cannot Create a Preference the Statute Lacks**

Appellants argue that because the statute is ambiguous, legislative history must be consulted to determine intent, citing *Bjorklund v. County of Chippewa*, 582 N.W.2d 593, 597 (Minn. Ct. App. 1998). However, because the Court finds the statutory language to be clear and unambiguous regarding the use of a lottery, legislative history is irrelevant. Furthermore, even if the statute were deemed ambiguous, the legislative history cited by Appellants—general statements about serving communities—does not specifically mandate a residency preference in the admissions process. Legislative history may not be used to contradict the statute's plain meaning. *State v. Lie*, 815 N.W.2d at 22.

Appellants contend that the doctrine of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* does not apply because the legislature did not affirmatively exclude residency preferences. They rely on *Merrill Lynch v. First Nat’l Bank*, 772 F.2d 158, 162 (5th Cir. 1985), for the proposition that the doctrine applies only when the legislature intends one method to be exclusive. However, the comprehensive detailing of the lottery process in subdivision 9 indicates the legislature's intent to make the lottery the exclusive method for resolving oversubscription. **E. The District Court’s Interpretation Does Not Produce Absurd or Unconstitutional Results**

Appellants argue that the district court’s ruling leads to "absurd results" under *Wegley v. City of Rochester*, 184 Minn. 138, 142, 238 N.W. 347, 349 (1931), and "unreasonable results" under *State v. Ness*, 174 Minn. 143, 148, 218 N.W. 555, 558 (1928). They posit that it is irrational for a charter school to be unable to prioritize local residents who fund the school. However, the result of a pure lottery system—where admission is based on chance rather than geography—is not absurd or unreasonable as a matter of law; it is a direct consequence of the legislature’s mandate to provide equal access to charter school enrollment regardless of location.

Appellants further argue that the district court’s interpretation raises constitutional concerns under the uniformity clause of the Minnesota Constitution, citing *Anderson v. City of Minneapolis*, 289 Minn. 366, 371, 184 N.W.2d 833, 837 (1971). This argument is unpersuasive. The legislature has the authority to establish charter schools as distinct entities with specific enrollment procedures. The uniformity clause does not require identical admissions criteria for all schools, nor does it compel the Court to rewrite a statute to avoid speculative constitutional doubts. The policy arguments advanced by Appellants regarding community support and local priority are matters for legislative consideration, not judicial rewriting.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Appellants claim the statute is ambiguous because it is "silent" on residency preferences. But silence on a specific issue does not create ambiguity when the statute expressly addresses the matter at hand. It says nothing about residency preferences. That is not ambiguity—it is deliberate legislative choice.  

Appellants rely on *A.W. v. Northwest Rabbinical Yeshiva*, 681 N.W.2d 15, 20 (Minn. 2004), for the proposition that silence creates ambiguity. But *A.W.* does not stand for that proposition. That case involved a statute with contradictory language, not a clear mandate that omits a specific exception. Here, the statute’s lottery requirement is precise and unqualified. As *State v. To read a residency preference into this text would violate the fundamental rule that "courts may not add words or provisions that are not present in the statute." *State v. Thiel*, 644 N.W.2d 452, 456 (Minn. 2002).  

### II. Context and Legislative History Cannot Override Plain Language  
Appellants argue that the statutory scheme and legislative history reveal a "proximity preference." But this Court has consistently held that context cannot override clear statutory text. *Moffett v. General purpose provisions—such as "increasing learning opportunities" (subd. 1) or "establishing accountability" (subd. 2)—do not conflict with subdivision 9’s lottery mandate. In fact, a lottery system *is* a method of ensuring equal access and accountability. Where the legislature has specifically detailed the admissions process in subdivision 9 without mentioning residency preferences, this Court cannot infer such a preference from general purposes. As *In re Welfare of T.R.*, 567 N.W.2d 47, 50 (Minn. 1997), teaches, "courts should not create exceptions not found in the law."

The legislature could intend charter schools to serve communities *and* admit students via lottery—these goals are complementary, not contradictory. *St. Otto’s* does not permit courts to rewrite statutes to harmonize unrelated provisions. Appellants’ argument that *expressio unius* does not apply because the statute did not "affirmatively exclude" preferences is circular. **FINAL RULING**

**Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen v. PACT Charter School**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School. Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen challenge the district court’s interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, arguing that the statute creates a “proximity preference” for residents of the city where the charter school is located. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both parties, the Court finds that Respondent’s interpretation of the statute is correct. The plain language of the statute mandates a lottery system without geographic exceptions, and Appellants’ arguments regarding ambiguity, legislative intent, and absurd results are insufficient to overcome this clear statutory command. Accordingly, the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The interpretation of a statute is a question of law reviewed de novo. *Am. Family Mut. Ins. Co. v. Ryan*, 762 N.W.2d 400, 404 (Minn. 2009). Similarly, this Court reviews a district court’s grant of summary judgment de novo. *Boulevard Woods, LLC v. Kmart Corp.*, 790 N.W.2d 873, 877 (Minn. 2010). Summary judgment is appropriate when there are no genuine issues of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Statute’s Plain Language Is Unambiguous**

The primary object of statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate the intent of the legislature. *State v. Lie*, 815 N.W.2d 19, 22 (Minn. 2012). When the language of a statute is clear and unambiguous, the court must enforce the statute as written. *City of Roseville v. State*, 831 N.W.2d 746, 750 (Minn. 2013).

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, provides that if the number of applications exceeds capacity, the school “must conduct a lottery.” Appellants argue that the statute is ambiguous because it is silent regarding residency preferences, relying on *A.W. v. Northwest Rabbinical Yeshiva*, 681 N.W.2d 15, 20 (Minn. 2004), for the proposition that silence creates ambiguity. However, as Respondent correctly notes, *A.W.* involved contradictory statutory language, not mere silence. Here, the statute’s mandate to conduct a lottery is precise and unqualified. Silence on a specific residency preference does not render the explicit lottery requirement ambiguous; rather, it indicates that the legislature did not intend to create such a preference. To read a “proximity preference” into the statute would require this Court to add words that the legislature chose to omit, a violation of the principle that courts may not add provisions to a statute. *State v. Thiel*, 644 N.W.2d 452, 456 (Minn. 2002).

**B. The Statutory Scheme Does Not Support a Proximity Preference**

Appellants contend that the broader statutory scheme, which states that charter schools are intended to increase learning opportunities and accountability, demonstrates a legislative intent to serve local communities. While this Court acknowledges that statutes must be interpreted in context, *Moffett v. Life Ins. Co. of North America*, 720 N.W.2d 820, 824 (Minn. Ct. App. 2006), general purpose provisions cannot override specific statutory mandates. *In re Welfare of T.R.*, 567 N.W.2d 47, 50 (Minn. 1997). The specific requirement in subdivision 9 that oversubscription be resolved by a lottery controls over the general goals of the charter school act. A lottery system is a neutral method of ensuring equal access and accountability, and it is not inconsistent with the legislature’s general goals. Appellants’ reliance on *St. Otto’s Church v. Minnesota Department of Revenue*, 325 N.W.2d 615, 618 (Minn. 1982), is misplaced because there is no internal inconsistency to resolve; the lottery mandate simply does not contain the geographic preference Appellants seek.

**C. Legislative History Is Irrelevant and Does Not Create a Preference**

Because the Court finds the statutory language to be clear and unambiguous, legislative history need not be consulted. *State v. Lie*, 815 N.W.2d at 22. Even if the Court were to find the statute ambiguous and consider the legislative history cited by Appellants, *Bjorklund v. County of Chippewa*, 582 N.W.2d 593, 597 (Minn. Ct. App. 1998), the history provides no specific mandate for a residency preference. General statements about “serving communities” are insufficient to override the specific lottery requirement or to imply a geographic exclusion that is absent from the text.

**D. The Doctrine of *Expressio Unius* Confirms the Lottery’s Exclusivity**

Appellants argue that the doctrine of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* does not apply because the legislature did not affirmatively exclude residency preferences. The Court disagrees. When a statute “comprehensively details” a process, omissions reflect intentional exclusions. *Thiel*, 644 N.W.2d at 456. Subdivision 9 comprehensively details the method for resolving oversubscription: a lottery. By specifying this exclusive method, the legislature excluded all other methods, including geographic preferences. *Merrill Lynch v. First Nat’l Bank*, 772 F.2d 158, 162 (5th Cir. 1985). Appellants’ assertion that the legislature did not “affirmatively exclude” preferences ignores the fact that the mandate to use a lottery is itself an affirmative exclusion of alternative selection criteria.

**E. The District Court’s Interpretation Does Not Produce Absurd or Unconstitutional Results**

Appellants argue that the district court’s ruling leads to “absurd results” because it prevents charter schools from prioritizing local residents who fund the school. *Wegley v. City of Rochester*, 184 Minn. 138, 142, 238 N.W. 347, 349 (1931). However, the result of a pure lottery system—where admission is based on chance rather than geography—is not absurd or unreasonable as a matter of law; it is a valid policy choice by the legislature to ensure equal access. *State v. Ness*, 174 Minn. 143, 148, 218 N.W. 555, 558 (1928). The policy arguments advanced by Appellants regarding community support and local priority are matters for legislative consideration, not judicial rewriting.

Furthermore, Appellants’ argument that the ruling violates the uniformity clause of the Minnesota Constitution, citing *Anderson v. City of Minneapolis*, 289 Minn. 366, 371, 184 N.W.2d 833, 837 (1971), is unpersuasive. The uniformity clause does not require identical admissions criteria for all schools, nor does it compel the Court to insert a geographic preference into the statute. The lottery system is a uniform method of selection that treats all applicants equally, regardless of their residence.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the district court correctly determined that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where a charter school is located. The plain language of the statute is unambiguous, and the Court may not judicially insert a proximity preference where the legislature has not seen fit to include one. The statutory scheme, legislative history, and doctrines of *expressio unius* and absurd results do not alter this conclusion.

Accordingly, the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School is affirmed.
