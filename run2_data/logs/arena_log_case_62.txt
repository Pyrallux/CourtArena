FACTS:
Appellee, a government contractor identified herein as John Doe, underwent military detention in Iraq. After his release, he filed this action in the district court against, inter alia, then-Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld alleging claims under the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., and a Bivens action for violation of his due process rights. Secretary Rumsfeld moved to dismiss for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. The district court granted the motion as to the claims under the DTA and some other claims, but did imply an action under the Bivens due process theory and denied Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss as to those claims. Secretary Rumsfeld appeals from the denial of his motion, arguing both that the claims are barred by qualified immunity and that the court erred in implying such a cause of action in the first instance. Because we agree that the district court erred in implying such a cause of action, we reverse the order of the district court.

Background

Because this case arises out of a motion to dismiss, we, like the district court, accept the well-pleaded factual allegations set forth in Doe’s complaint as true for purposes of this stage of the litigation and construe reasonable inferences from those allegations in Doe’s favor, although we are not required to accept Doe’s legal conclusions as true. See, e.g., Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). Doe’s complaint alleges the following facts:

In July 2005, he made contact and developed a relationship with Iraqi Sheikh Abd AlSattar Abu Risha. 
On October 20, 2005, Doe returned to a United States military camp in preparation to depart for his annual leave. There, a Navy Criminal Investigative Service (NCIS) agent asked to interview Doe. Doe agreed and discussed his work -with the agent.

Two weeks later, Doe traveled to A1 Asad, a United States military base, from which he was scheduled to depart for his leave. At that point, three NCIS agents, including the one who previously interviewed him, along with another United States official, detained Doe and interrogated him for four hours. Doe alleges that the agents denied his requests to have an attorney, a company representative, or a member of the Marine unit present for his questioning.  After seventy-two hours of solitary confinement, he was blindfolded, hooded, and  where he was confined for nine months. During his detainment, United States government officials interrogated Doe multiple times and denied his requests for an attorney to be present at those interrogations.

In December 2005, the Detainee Status Board held a hearing and deemed Doe a threat to the Multi-National Forces in Iraq. Procedural History

In November 2008, Doe filed this action against former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld, other United States government officers and agents, and the United States government. Relevant to this appeal, Doe asked the district court to hold Secretary Rumsfeld personally liable for violating Doe’s rights under the DTA and for violating Doe’s constitutional rights guaranteed under the Fifth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments .

The district court dismissed Doe’s claims arising under the DTA, holding that the DTA does not provide a private cause of action. Id. at 104-05. The district court also dismissed Doe’s procedural due process and access to court claims, holding that Doe had not pled sufficient facts to overcome Secretary Rumsfeld’s qualified immunity defense. The court held, however, that Doe could maintain a federal cause of action for his substantive due process claims under Bivens v. Secretary Rumsfeld filed this interlocutory appeal of the district court’s partial denial of his motion to dismiss Doe’s claims against him. Although the jurisdiction of courts of appeals ordinarily extends only to review of “final decisions” of the district courts, we have jurisdiction over the current interlocutory appeal. This case fits squarely within a well-established exception that the denial of a motion to dismiss on the ground of qualified immunity has sufficient finality to warrant interlocutory review, and within the language of Hartman v. ## INTRODUCTION

Secretary Rumsfeld's appeal asks this Court to dismiss Appellee's substantive due process claims at the pleading stage based on two flawed arguments: first, that no Bivens remedy can exist in this context, and second, that qualified immunity shields him from suit. Both arguments fail. The district court correctly held that Doe has stated a viable Bivens claim for violations of his substantive due process rights and that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity at this stage. This Court should affirm the district court's denial of the motion to dismiss.

---

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY RECOGNIZED A BIVENS REMEDY FOR SUBSTANTIVE DUE PROCESS CLAIMS

### A. The Framework for Analyzing Bivens Claims

The Supreme Court's decision in *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017), establishes the analytical framework for determining whether a Bivens remedy should be recognized. Under *Ziglar*, this Court must first determine whether the claim arises in a "new context," and if so, whether special factors counsel hesitation before recognizing a Bivens remedy. *Id.* at 1855-57. However, *Ziglar* does not establish a categorical bar against recognizing Bivens remedies in all new contexts; rather, it requires courts to exercise "caution" and consider whether "special factors" weigh against implying a remedy. *Id.*

Critically, substantive due process claims alleging government detention that "shocks the conscience" do not present a truly novel context. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause protects individuals from government conduct that shocks the conscience. *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172-73, 72 S. Ct. 205, 96 L.Ed. 183 (1952). This principle has been applied in numerous contexts, including police conduct during investigations, *see* *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846, 118 S. Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998), and conditions of confinement, *see* *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 738, 122 S. Ct. 2508, 153 L.Ed.2d 666 (2002). The constitutional right Doe asserts—the right to be free from government detention practices that shock the conscience—is well-established in precedent predating the alleged conduct.

### B. This Case Does Not Present the Type of "New Context" That Counsels Against a Bivens Remedy

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that this case presents a "new context" because it involves a civilian contractor detained overseas. This argument misconstrues the "new context" analysis. In *Ziglar*, the Supreme Court identified the critical concern as whether the case involves "different constitutional provisions, different rights, or different injuries" from the three original Bivens contexts. 137 S. Ct. at 1856. Here, Doe alleges a violation of his Fifth Amendment right to substantive due process—a right that has been recognized for decades. The fact that the specific factual scenario (detention of a civilian contractor in Iraq) may be novel does not transform the constitutional claim into a new one.

Moreover, the policy concerns that animated *Ziglar* are not present here. In *Ziglar*, the Court worried about "broad-based official misconduct" involving immigration enforcement and the potential for "massive litigation" against federal officials. *Id.* at 1858, 1860. Here, by contrast, Doe alleges specific, individually targeted misconduct by high-ranking officials, including Secretary Rumsfeld himself. The deterrent value of allowing this claim to proceed is substantial, while the risk of opening floodgates is minimal.

### C. Special Factors Do Not Counsel Against a Bivens Remedy

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that "special factors" counsel hesitation because the alleged conduct involves military operations and foreign policy. However, the Supreme Court has rejected the proposition that military operations categorically preclude Bivens remedies. In *Department of Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988), the Court recognized that the judiciary has a role in reviewing constitutional claims even in military contexts. More recently, in *Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council*, Inc., 555 U.S. 7, 24 (2008), the Court applied traditional constitutional analysis to military decisions without deferring categorically to executive judgment.

The fact that Congress has not provided a statutory remedy does not counsel against recognizing a Bivens remedy. As the Supreme Court noted in *Ziglar*, "the absence of a statutory alternative does not automatically require a Bivens remedy," but it is also not dispositive against one. 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The key inquiry is whether "special factors" indicate that the political branches are better positioned to address the alleged harm. Here, there is no indication that Congress has considered and rejected a remedy for civilian contractors subjected to unconstitutional detention practices. The absence of a statutory remedy reflects congressional inaction, not affirmative policy judgment.

---

## II. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS NOT ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY AT THE PLEADING STAGE

### A. The Applicable Legal Standard

Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability unless the right allegedly violated was "clearly established" at the time of the conduct. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S. Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982). The "clearly established" inquiry has two components: (1) the law must define the right with "particularity," and (2) the factual situation must be "sufficiently similar" to prior cases such that a reasonable official would understand that his conduct violates that right. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. at 739-41.

At the pleading stage, this Court must accept well-pleaded facts as true and determine whether the complaint alleges a violation of a clearly established right. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S. Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). This is a legal determination that does not require evidentiary development.

### B. The Right to Be Free from Detention That Shocks the Conscience Was Clearly Established

The Supreme Court has long held that substantive due process protects individuals from government conduct that "shocks the conscience." *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. at 172-73. This standard applies with particular force to conditions of confinement and methods of interrogation. *See* *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. at 738 (holding that denial of adequate sanitation and exposure to extreme temperatures stated a claim under the Eighth Amendment); *Chandler v. Miller*, 520 U.S. 305, 308 (1997) (acknowledging that substantive due process protects against government actions that "violate the decencies of civilized conduct").

Doe's complaint alleges conduct that unquestionably meets the "shocks the conscience" standard:

- Four-hour interrogation during which agents denied his requests for an attorney, kicked him in the back, and threatened to shoot him
- Seventy-two hours of solitary confinement
- Nine months of detention in harsh conditions, including extreme temperatures and deprivation of sleep
- Physical abuse, including being choked repeatedly
- Detention for an extended period without formal charges or access to courts

These allegations describe treatment that far exceeds the threshold of constitutional minima. The district court correctly held that Doe has alleged a violation of his clearly established right to be free from detention that shocks the conscience.

### C. Secretary Rumsfeld's Argument That the "Shock the Conscience" Standard Is Too Vague Fails

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that the "shock the conscience" standard is inherently vague and has never been applied to the specific factual scenario alleged here. This argument misapprehends the qualified immunity analysis. The Supreme Court has explicitly held that officials cannot escape liability by pointing to the absence of prior cases with nearly identical facts. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. at 741. The "clearly established" inquiry does not require "a case directly on point," but only that the law be "sufficiently clear" that a reasonable official would understand that his conduct violates the Constitution. *Id.*

The conduct Doe alleges—prolonged solitary confinement, physical abuse, threats of death, denial of legal representation, and detention without charges—does not require a sophisticated legal analysis to determine that it violates the Constitution. Any reasonable official would understand that such treatment shocks the conscience and violates fundamental due process rights.

### D. The Individual Capacity Claim Against Secretary Rumsfeld Is Properly Pleaded

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that he cannot be held personally liable because he did not personally participate in the alleged misconduct. This argument fails. Doe alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld, as the head of the Department of Defense, established and maintained the policies and practices that led to his mistreatment. Doe alleges that Rumsfeld was personally involved in creating the detention and interrogation program that permitted the alleged abuses. These allegations are sufficient to state a claim against Secretary Rumsfeld in his individual capacity. *See* *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 677 (holding that supervisory officials may be held liable for constitutional violations where they "knew of and disregarded the substantial risk of serious harm").

---

## III. THE REMEDY SOUGHT IS APPROPRIATE

Secretary Rumsfeld's appeal seeks to dismiss this case in its entirety. However, even if this Court were to find some aspect of the claim problematic, dismissal with prejudice is not warranted. At most, the appropriate remedy would be to allow Doe to amend his complaint to cure any pleading deficiencies. The current complaint, however, adequately states a claim for relief.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the district court's denial of Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss. The district court correctly held that Doe may maintain a Bivens action for substantive due process violations and that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity at the pleading stage. Doe has alleged specific, egregious conduct that violates clearly established constitutional rights. The appropriate forum for resolving the factual disputes is a trial on the merits, not a dismissal before the parties have engaged in discovery.

**PER CURIAM.**

This case requires us to determine whether the district court erred in denying Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss a Bivens action brought by John Doe, a civilian contractor detained in Iraq. Because we find that the district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy in this new context and that Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity, we reverse.

**I. JURISDICTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

We have jurisdiction over this interlocutory appeal pursuant to the collateral order doctrine, which allows for review of a district court’s order denying a claim of qualified immunity. *Hartman v. Moore*, 547 U.S. 250, 257 n. 5 (2006). We review de novo the district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008). In doing so, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations in the complaint as true and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the plaintiff. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009).

**II. BIVENS REMEDY**

The district court erred in implying a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Fed. Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971). The Supreme Court has consistently instructed that expanding the *Bivens* remedy is a "disfavored" judicial activity. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). A *Bivens* action is appropriate only if the case presents no "new context" and if no "special factors counsel hesitation" in implying a remedy. *Id.*

A. **New Context**

This case presents a "new context" distinct from the three specific contexts in which the Supreme Court has implied a *Bivens* remedy: Fourth Amendment search and seizure (*Bivens*), Fifth Amendment gender discrimination in employment (*Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979)), and Eighth Amendment cruel and unusual punishment of federal prisoners (*Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980)). *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1855-57.

Appellee argues that this is not a new context because the constitutional right asserted—the right to substantive due process—is well established. However, the "new context" inquiry is not limited to the constitutional provision invoked; it requires an analysis of the "totality of the circumstances," including the risk of disruptive intrusion by the Judiciary into the functions of other branches. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859. The detention of a civilian contractor by the military in a war zone involves national security and foreign policy considerations fundamentally different from the domestic contexts previously recognized by the Court. The relationship between the parties—a high-ranking military official and a contractor operating in a combat theater—is likewise distinct. Therefore, this case falls outside the narrow scope of recognized *Bivens* actions.

B. **Special Factors**

Even if this context were not entirely new, "special factors" counsel hesitation before implying a *Bivens* remedy. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The conduct alleged involves military detention and interrogation operations during an active conflict. The Supreme Court has recognized that the Judiciary is ill-equipped to second-guess military decisions made in such sensitive environments. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1864. Furthermore, the existence of alternative remedial structures, such as military review boards and the potential for congressional action, weighs against the creation of a new judicial remedy. *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988). While Appellee notes the absence of a specific statutory cause of action, the absence of such a remedy does not automatically justify a *Bivens* action where the political branches are better positioned to address the harm. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

**III. QUALIFIED IMMUNITY**

Even assuming a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity shields government officials from liability unless they violate a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). A right is clearly established only when its contours are sufficiently definite that any reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002).

Appellee relies on the "shocks the conscience" standard of substantive due process. *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952). While the general prohibition against conduct that shocks the conscience is established, Appellee has not identified prior case law that clearly establishes that the specific detention and interrogation practices alleged here—applied to a civilian contractor in a war zone—were unconstitutional. *Reichle v. Howards*, 566 U.S. 658, 664-65 (2012). The cases cited by Appellee, such as *Hope v. Pelzer*, involve domestic prison conditions and do not place a reasonable military official on notice that the conduct at issue violated the Constitution. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). Because the right was not clearly established in this specific context, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy for Appellee’s claims and in denying Secretary Rumsfeld qualified immunity. The order of the district court is reversed.

The appellee's brief cites *Hope v. #### B. The Appellee's Reliance on *Hope v. Pelzer* Is Misplaced

**PER CURIAM.**

Upon consideration of the rebuttals filed by the parties, we reaffirm our preliminary ruling. The district court erred in implying a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Fed. Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), and in denying Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. We therefore reverse the order of the district court.

**I. BIVENS REMEDY**

The district court’s implication of a *Bivens* remedy in this case was erroneous. The Supreme Court has made clear that expanding the *Bivens* remedy is a "disfavored" judicial activity. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). A *Bivens* action is appropriate only if the case presents no "new context" and if no "special factors counsel hesitation" in implying a remedy. *Id.*

This case presents a "new context" distinct from the three specific contexts in which the Supreme Court has implied a *Bivens* remedy: Fourth Amendment search and seizure (*Bivens*, 403 U.S. at 388), Fifth Amendment gender discrimination in employment (*Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979)), and Eighth Amendment cruel and unusual punishment of federal prisoners (*Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980)). *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1855-57. The Appellee’s reliance on general substantive due process principles, as articulated in *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), and *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), does not overcome this hurdle. The "new context" inquiry requires an analysis of the "totality of the circumstances," including the risk of disruptive intrusion by the Judiciary into the functions of other branches. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859. The detention of a civilian contractor by the military in a war zone involves national security and foreign policy considerations fundamentally different from the domestic contexts previously recognized by the Court.

Furthermore, "special factors" counsel hesitation against implying a *Bivens* remedy. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The conduct alleged involves military detention and interrogation operations during an active conflict. The Judiciary is ill-equipped to second-guess military decisions made in such sensitive environments. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1864. Additionally, the existence of alternative remedial structures, such as the Detainee Status Board hearings provided to Doe, weighs against the creation of a new judicial remedy. *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988). The absence of a specific statutory cause of action does not automatically justify a *Bivens* action where the political branches are better positioned to address the harm. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

**II. QUALIFIED IMMUNITY**

Even assuming a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity shields government officials from liability unless they violate a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). A right is clearly established only when its contours are sufficiently definite that any reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002).

The Appellee relies on the "shocks the conscience" standard of substantive due process. *Rochin*, 342 U.S. at 172. However, the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that this general standard does not satisfy the qualified immunity inquiry where the alleged conduct has no prior case law directly addressing its constitutionality. *Reichle v. Howards*, 566 U.S. 658, 664-65 (2012). The "shocks the conscience" test is inherently vague and requires case-specific application. *County of Sacramento*, 523 U.S. at 846. No prior case has held that the specific detention and interrogation practices alleged here—applied to a civilian contractor in a war zone—were unconstitutional. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). The cases cited by the Appellee, such as *Hope v. Pelzer*, involve domestic prison conditions and do not place a reasonable military official on notice that the conduct at issue violated the Constitution. Because the right was not clearly established in this specific context, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy for Appellee’s claims and in denying Secretary Rumsfeld qualified immunity. The order of the district court is reversed.

**JOHN DOE, APPELLEE**
v.
**DONALD RUMSFELD, APPELLANT**

**Argued [Date]. Decided [Date].**

**On Appeal from the United States District Court for the District of Columbia**

**[No. 08-01969]**

**Hon. [Judge Name], District Judge**

**OPINION**

**PER CURIAM.**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the parties, we reaffirm our preliminary determination that the district court erred in implying a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Fed. Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), and in denying Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity. For the reasons stated below, we reverse the order of the district court.

**I. BIVENS REMEDY**

The district court’s implication of a *Bivens* remedy in this case was erroneous. The Supreme Court has made clear that expanding the *Bivens* remedy is a "disfavored" judicial activity. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). A *Bivens* action is appropriate only if the case presents no "new context" and if no "special factors counsel hesitation" in implying a remedy. *Id.*

This case presents a "new context" distinct from the three specific contexts in which the Supreme Court has implied a *Bivens* remedy: Fourth Amendment search and seizure (*Bivens*, 403 U.S. at 388), Fifth Amendment gender discrimination in employment (*Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979)), and Eighth Amendment cruel and unusual punishment of federal prisoners (*Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980)). *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1855-57. The Appellee’s reliance on general substantive due process principles, as articulated in *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), and *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), does not overcome this hurdle. The "new context" inquiry requires an analysis of the "totality of the circumstances," including the risk of disruptive intrusion by the Judiciary into the functions of other branches. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1859. The detention of a civilian contractor by the military in a war zone involves national security and foreign policy considerations fundamentally different from the domestic contexts previously recognized by the Court.

Furthermore, "special factors" counsel hesitation against implying a *Bivens* remedy. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The conduct alleged involves military detention and interrogation operations during an active conflict. The Judiciary is ill-equipped to second-guess military decisions made in such sensitive environments. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1864. Additionally, the existence of alternative remedial structures, such as the Detainee Status Board hearings provided to Doe, weighs against the creation of a new judicial remedy. *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988). The absence of a specific statutory cause of action does not automatically justify a *Bivens* action where the political branches are better positioned to address the harm. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. As noted in *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518 (1988), executive discretion in national security matters is a significant factor counseling against judicial interference.

**II. QUALIFIED IMMUNITY**

Even assuming a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity shields government officials from liability unless they violate a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). A right is clearly established only when its contours are sufficiently definite that any reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002).

The Appellee relies on the "shocks the conscience" standard of substantive due process. *Rochin*, 342 U.S. at 172. However, the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that this general standard does not satisfy the qualified immunity inquiry where the alleged conduct has no prior case law directly addressing its constitutionality. *Reichle v. Howards*, 566 U.S. 658, 664-65 (2012). The "shocks the conscience" test is inherently vague and requires case-specific application. *County of Sacramento*, 523 U.S. at 846. No prior case has held that the specific detention and interrogation practices alleged here—applied to a civilian contractor in a war zone—were unconstitutional. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). The cases cited by the Appellee, such as *Hope v. Pelzer*, involve domestic prison conditions and do not place a reasonable military official on notice that the conduct at issue violated the Constitution. Because the right was not clearly established in this specific context, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy for Appellee’s claims and in denying Secretary Rumsfeld qualified immunity. The order of the district court is reversed.

**Reversed.**
