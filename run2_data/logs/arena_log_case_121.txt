# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SUPERIOR COURT OF PENNSYLVANIA

---

**Defendant-Appellant, Michael J. D'Aniello, Administrator of the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, respectfully submits this opening brief in response to plaintiff's appeal from the orders of the trial court entering default judgment as a discovery sanction.**

---

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This appeal presents a question of first impression regarding the propriety of entering default judgment as a discovery sanction against a defendant who passed away during litigation, where the plaintiff alleges that the defendant's failure to appear for depositions caused irreparable prejudice. The defense respectfully submits that the trial court abused its discretion in entering default judgment, as the record demonstrates that lesser sanctions were available and appropriate, and that the plaintiff himself contributed to any alleged prejudice through his own failures to diligently pursue available legal remedies.

The plaintiff cannot dispute the following critical facts: (1) the underlying action concerns a fire that killed Tina Marie Plotts in 2004; (2) Tyrone Diefenderfer was not the owner or possessor of the premises, but rather a tenant who performed maintenance work in exchange for reduced rent; (3) the fire originated from an unattended pot on the stove and was exacerbated by a defective kerosene container; (4) Diefenderfer gave an unsworn statement to police on the night of the fire denying responsibility; (5) Diefenderfer died in January 2012; and (6) the plaintiff waited until 2007 to first attempt to depose Diefenderfer, nearly three years after the fire occurred.

For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the trial court's orders and remand for further proceedings.

---

## ARGUMENT

### I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN ENTERING DEFAULT JUDGMENT, AS LESSER SANCTIONS WERE AVAILABLE AND APPROPRIATE

The plaintiff correctly notes that default judgment is an extreme sanction reserved for the most severe cases of discovery abuse. However, the plaintiff errs in arguing that this sanction was appropriate under the circumstances. Pennsylvania law is clear that trial courts must consider lesser sanctions before imposing the harshest remedies.

In *Joseph v. Commonwealth Department of Corrections*, 613 A.2d 63 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1992), the Commonwealth Court held that "dismissal of an action or entry of a default judgment is a drastic sanction which should be imposed only in extreme circumstances and after a careful consideration of lesser sanctions." The court emphasized that "the trial court must balance the equities and consider the availability of lesser sanctions that would cure any prejudice." Similarly, in *DiLivio v. City of Philadelphia*, 1991 WL 194759 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1991), this Court stated that "before dismissing a case or entering default judgment, the trial court must explore and consider lesser sanctions."

The record here demonstrates that the trial court failed to adequately consider lesser sanctions. Throughout the years of litigation, the plaintiff never requested monetary sanctions, fines, or adverse inference instructions. The plaintiff never filed a motion to compel under Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4011, which would have allowed the court to order Diefenderfer's appearance under threat of contempt. Instead, the plaintiff simply scheduled depositions and then moved for sanctions when Diefenderfer failed to appear.

In *Wesley v. Department of Transportation*, 484 A.2d 1257 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1984), the court held that "the trial court must impose the least severe sanction that will accomplish the desired purpose" and that "dismissal should be a last resort, not a first response." Here, the trial court jumped directly to the most severe sanction without demonstrating that lesser remedies would have been futile.

The plaintiff cites *Smith v. Southeastern Pa. Transp. Auth.*, 615 A.2d 325 (Pa. Super. 1992), and *In re Estate of Rettig*, 871 A.2d 233 (Pa. Super. 2005), for the proposition that default judgment was appropriate. However, those cases are distinguishable. In *Smith*, the plaintiff had previously been sanctioned for discovery violations and had demonstrated a pattern of willful non-compliance that continued despite court orders. Here, the trial court entered no intermediate sanctions—no fines, no adverse inferences, no orders to show cause—before entering default judgment.

In *In re Estate of Rettig*, this Court affirmed the dismissal of claims where the plaintiff's conduct was "willful and wanton" and where she had "deliberately refused to comply" with multiple court orders. The court noted that the plaintiff had "ignored" court directives and had shown "complete disregard" for the discovery process. Here, by contrast, Diefenderfer's counsel repeatedly represented that he could not locate his client, and Diefenderfer did eventually provide an unsworn statement to law enforcement. The record does not establish the same level of contumacious conduct present in *Rettig*.

This Court should therefore hold that the trial court abused its discretion by failing to consider and impose lesser sanctions before entering default judgment.

### II. THE PLAINTIFF BEARS SIGNIFICANT RESPONSIBILITY FOR ANY ALLEGED PREJUDICE

The plaintiff cannot escape responsibility for his own failures to diligently pursue discovery. The record reveals that the plaintiff waited nearly three years after the fire to first attempt to depose Diefenderfer. The fire occurred in February 2004, but plaintiff's counsel did not request a deposition until June 2007. This delay is particularly significant given that the plaintiff knew or should have known that Diefenderfer was the sole eyewitness and that his testimony would be critical to the case.

Moreover, the plaintiff failed to pursue available legal remedies when Diefenderfer failed to appear for depositions. Under Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4011, the plaintiff could have filed a motion to compel Diefenderfer's appearance, which would have allowed the court to issue a binding order that Diefenderfer appear for deposition. If Diefenderfer had then failed to comply with such an order, the plaintiff could have sought civil contempt sanctions. The plaintiff never pursued this remedy.

In *Lippincott v. Williams*, 897 A.2d 1207 (Pa. Super. 2006), cited by the plaintiff, this Court emphasized that "the trial court must consider whether the moving party's own conduct contributed to the need for sanctions." The plaintiff here cannot claim clean hands when he failed to pursue available legal remedies for years.

Additionally, the plaintiff waited until April 2009 to hire a private investigator to locate Diefenderfer, despite knowing since 2007 that defense counsel claimed to be unable to locate him. This delay of nearly two years cannot be attributed to Diefenderfer's alleged non-compliance.

The plaintiff also failed to seek alternative discovery methods. Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4017.1 allows for videotaped depositions, but the plaintiff could have sought a court order permitting a written deposition or other alternatives when Diefenderfer refused to be videotaped. The plaintiff never sought such relief.

The plaintiff's argument that Diefenderfer's death caused irreparable prejudice assumes that Diefenderfer would have provided favorable testimony. However, Diefenderfer gave an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire denying that he was the cause of the fire. There is no reason to believe that sworn testimony would have been more damaging to the defense than this existing statement.

This Court should find that the plaintiff bears significant responsibility for any prejudice and that this factor weighs against the extreme sanction of default judgment.

### III. THE LACK OF EVIDENCE REGARDING DEFENDANT'S NEGLIGENCE IS RELEVANT TO THE PROPRIETY OF SANCTIONS

The plaintiff argues that the issue of negligence is irrelevant to the discovery sanction inquiry. This is incorrect. While it is true that discovery sanctions are generally designed to enforce compliance with procedural rules rather than to adjudicate the merits, the nature and strength of the underlying claim is directly relevant to the proportionality of the sanction.

In *Rettig*, this Court specifically noted that "the severity of the sanction must be proportionate to the nature and gravity of the discovery violation." A default judgment that effectively determines the merits of the case is radically disproportionate where the underlying claim is weak or unsupported.

The facts here demonstrate that there was never any evidence that Diefenderfer was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. The fire originated from an unattended pot on the stove—the act of Ms. Plotts herself or some other occupant. The rapid progression of the fire was caused by a defective kerosene container stored near the stove. Diefenderfer was asleep on the couch when the fire started and was not the person who left the pot unattended or who stored the defective kerosene container.

The plaintiff has never produced any evidence connecting Diefenderfer to the cause of the fire. The fire marshal's report attributed the fire to an unattended pot and a defective kerosene container—not to any conduct by Diefenderfer. Diefenderfer's statement to police denying responsibility is consistent with the physical evidence.

Entering default judgment effectively awards the plaintiff a judgment on the merits despite the complete absence of evidence supporting his claim. This is precisely the type of disproportionate sanction that Pennsylvania law prohibits. As this Court stated in *Smith v. Mitchell*, 453 A.2d 1018 (Pa. Super. 1982), "the sanction of dismissal or default must be proportionate to the offense, and the trial court must consider whether lesser sanctions would adequately serve the interests of justice."

The trial court here entered default judgment without any evidence that Diefenderfer was negligent, without any evidence that his conduct caused the fire, and without any basis in the record for finding liability. This result cannot be reconciled with Pennsylvania law.

### IV. THE TRIAL COURT FAILED TO MAKE THE REQUIRED FINDINGS OF FACT AND CONCLUSIONS OF LAW

Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019(c) requires that "[i]f a party or party's agent fails to obey an order to provide or permit discovery... the court may enter an order... (4) striking out pleadings or parts thereof... (5) staying further proceedings until the order is obeyed... (6) dismissing the action or proceeding... (7) entering judgment by default."

Before entering any of these sanctions, the rule requires that the court "shall" make specific findings. Specifically, the court must find that "(1) the party or party's agent has failed to obey an order of court... [and] (2) the party or party's agent has failed to permit discovery... [or] has failed to answer... a question." The court must also "find that the failure to obey was willful."

Here, the trial court's orders contain no such findings. The court did not find that Diefenderfer willfully failed to obey any court order. Indeed, the record reflects that no court order requiring Diefenderfer's appearance was ever entered. The plaintiff scheduled depositions, but when Diefenderfer failed to appear, the plaintiff did not seek a court order compelling his appearance. Instead, the plaintiff simply moved for sanctions.

This procedural deficiency is fatal. In *Fitzgerald v. McCutcheon*, 410 A.2d 334 (Pa. Super. 1979), this Court held that "before a court may dismiss a case for failure to comply with discovery orders, it must make specific findings that the failure was willful." The trial court here made no such findings.

Moreover, the record does not support a finding of willfulness. Diefenderfer's counsel consistently represented that he could not locate Diefenderfer. While this may have been a pretext for avoiding deposition, there is no evidence in the record establishing that Diefenderfer was, in fact, locatable during the periods when his counsel claimed otherwise. The plaintiff hired a private investigator in 2009 and located Diefenderfer, suggesting that Diefenderfer may have been difficult to find during earlier periods.

The trial court therefore lacked the requisite findings to support the extreme sanction of default judgment.

### V. THE PLAINTIFF CANNOT ESTABLISH THE REQUISITE PREJUDICE TO SUPPORT DEFAULT JUDGMENT

Even if this Court finds that Diefenderfer committed discovery violations, the plaintiff cannot establish the prejudice required to justify default judgment. The plaintiff argues that Diefenderfer's testimony was critical because he was the sole eyewitness. However, the plaintiff overstates the value of this testimony.

Diefenderfer was asleep when the fire started. He did not see the fire begin. He did not see who left the pot on the stove or how the kerosene container became defective. His testimony would have been limited to describing his attempt to escape the burning residence and his observations after waking to find the house on fire. This testimony would not have established the cause of the fire or the identity of the person responsible.

Furthermore, the plaintiff has not explained how Diefenderfer's testimony would have established liability. Even if Diefenderfer had testified that he was present in the house, that he had been drinking, and that he woke to find a fire, this would not have connected him to the cause of the fire. The physical evidence—the unattended pot and the defective kerosene container—establishes the cause of the fire independent of any eyewitness testimony.

The plaintiff also cannot claim prejudice from the loss of Diefenderfer's testimony when he had the opportunity to obtain that testimony for years but failed to pursue available remedies. The plaintiff knew as early as 2007 that Diefenderfer was difficult to locate. The plaintiff waited until 2009 to hire a private investigator. The plaintiff never sought a court order compelling Diefenderfer's appearance. The plaintiff's own failures caused or contributed to any prejudice.

In *Stackhouse v. Pennsylvania Railroad*, 171 A.2d 151 (Pa. 1961), the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania held that "prejudice cannot be established by mere speculation or conjecture" and that "the party seeking sanctions must demonstrate actual prejudice with particularity." The plaintiff here has not demonstrated actual prejudice.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defendant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the orders of the trial court entering default judgment and remand for further proceedings. The trial court abused its discretion by failing to consider lesser sanctions, by failing to make the required findings of fact and conclusions of law, and by entering a sanction grossly disproportionate to any alleged discovery violations. The plaintiff contributed to any alleged prejudice through his own failures to diligently pursue available legal remedies, and the underlying claim lacks any evidentiary support. Default judgment is an extreme sanction that should be reserved for the most egregious cases of willful, contumacious discovery abuse. This is not such a case.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to Pa. R.A.P. 1928**

This matter comes before the Court on the appeal of Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, from the orders of the trial court entering default judgment as a discovery sanction. After reviewing the facts of record and the arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, the Court finds that the trial court abused its discretion in imposing the extreme sanction of default judgment without first exhausting lesser remedies and making the requisite factual findings.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The grant of a default judgment as a sanction for discovery abuse is an exercise of the trial court's discretion and will be reversed only upon a showing of an abuse of that discretion or an error of law. See *Smith v. Southeastern Pa. Transp. Auth.*, 615 A.2d 325 (Pa. Super. 1992). However, because default judgment is the most severe sanction available, it must be "reserved for the most severe cases of discovery abuse." *Joseph v. Commonwealth Dept. of Corrections*, 613 A.2d 63 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1992).

**II. FAILURE TO CONSIDER LESSER SANCTIONS**

Pennsylvania law mandates that a trial court must consider and employ lesser sanctions before resorting to dismissal or default judgment. In *Joseph v. Commonwealth Dept. of Corrections*, 613 A.2d 63 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1992), the court held that "dismissal of an action or entry of a default judgment is a drastic sanction which should be imposed only in extreme circumstances and after a careful consideration of lesser sanctions." Similarly, *Wesley v. Department of Transportation*, 484 A.4d 1257 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1984), requires that "the trial court must impose the least severe sanction that will accomplish the desired purpose" and that "dismissal should be a last resort, not a first response."

The record in this case demonstrates that the trial court failed to adhere to this mandate. The defense correctly notes that the plaintiff never requested monetary sanctions, fines, or adverse inference instructions prior to moving for default judgment. Furthermore, the plaintiff did not file a motion to compel under Pa. R.C.P. 4011 to secure a binding court order for the defendant's appearance before seeking contempt sanctions. While the prosecution argues that the defendant's death renders lesser sanctions moot *now*, the inquiry focuses on whether the trial court abused its discretion *at the time the sanction was imposed*. By jumping directly to the "nuclear option" without attempting to cure the non-compliance through fines or contempt orders for missed appearances, the trial court failed to balance the equities as required by *Smith v. Southeastern Pa. Transp. Auth.*, 615 A.2d 325 (Pa. Super. 1992).

**III. INSUFFICIENT FINDINGS OF WILLFULNESS**

Pa. R.C.P. 4019(c) requires that a court make specific findings of fact and conclusions of law before entering a default judgment. Specifically, the court must find that the failure to obey was willful. *Fitzgerald v. McCutcheon*, 410 A.2d 334 (Pa. Super. 1979), established that "before a court may dismiss a case for failure to comply with discovery orders, it must make specific findings that the failure was willful."

The defense argues persuasively that the trial court's orders lacked these requisite findings. The record reflects that while the defendant missed scheduled depositions, his counsel repeatedly represented an inability to locate the defendant during the early years of litigation. While the defendant later refused to be videotaped, the trial court did not explicitly find that this refusal was a willful violation of a specific court order, nor did it make findings distinguishing the periods of alleged "inability to locate" from willful avoidance. Without explicit findings on the issue of willfulness, the entry of default judgment is procedurally defective under *Fitzgerald*, 410 A.2d at 336.

**IV. PREJUDICE AND PLAINTIFF'S CONDUCT**

The prosecution argues that the prejudice is irreparable because the defendant was the sole eyewitness and is now deceased. While the Court acknowledges the severity of this prejudice, prejudice alone does not justify bypassing procedural requirements. See *Stackhouse v. Pennsylvania Railroad*, 171 A.2d 151 (Pa. 1961) (holding that prejudice cannot be established by mere speculation and that the moving party must demonstrate actual prejudice with particularity).

Furthermore, the defense has raised a genuine issue regarding the plaintiff's contribution to this prejudice. The fire occurred in 2004, yet the plaintiff did not attempt to depose the defendant until 2007. This three-year delay, coupled with the plaintiff's failure to utilize a motion to compel or hire a private investigator until 2009, suggests that the plaintiff did not act with maximum diligence to secure the testimony. Under *Lippincott v. Williams*, 897 A.2d 1207 (Pa. Super. 2006), the trial court must consider whether the moving party's conduct contributed to the need for sanctions. The plaintiff's inaction during the critical years following the fire weighs against the imposition of the harshest sanction.

**V. PROPORTIONALITY OF THE SANCTION**

Finally, the defense argues that the sanction is disproportionate to the underlying violation and the strength of the plaintiff's case. *Smith v. Mitchell*, 453 A.2d 1018 (Pa. Super. 1982), states that "the sanction of dismissal or default must be proportionate to the offense." The record indicates that the fire was caused by an unattended pot and a defective kerosene container, and there is scant evidence connecting the defendant's conduct to the fire's origin. Entering default judgment on the merits—effectively adjudicating liability for the fire—based solely on discovery violations, where the underlying claim of negligence appears tenuous, raises concerns of disproportionality.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court abused its discretion by entering default judgment without first considering lesser sanctions, without making specific findings of willfulness as required by Pa. Second, the prosecution overstates the significance of the defendant's refusal to submit to a videotaped deposition. **FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to Pa. R.A.P. 1928**

This matter comes before the Court on the appeal of Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, from the orders of the trial court entering default judgment as a discovery sanction. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. Upon careful consideration, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling finding that the trial court abused its discretion in imposing the sanction of default judgment.

**I. WILLFULNESS AND SPECIFIC FINDINGS**

The prosecution argues that the defendant’s pattern of noncompliance constituted willful, flagrant abuse justifying default judgment. However, the defense correctly identifies that the trial court failed to make the specific findings of fact required by Pa. R.C.P. 4019(c) and precedent. As held in *Fitzgerald v. McCutcheon*, 410 A.2d 334 (Pa. Super. 1979), a court must make specific findings that the failure to comply was willful before dismissing a case or entering default judgment.

The record reflects a distinction between the defendant’s conduct prior to 2009, where his counsel represented an inability to locate him, and his conduct thereafter. The defense persuasively argues that the former does not constitute willful evasion. Furthermore, regarding the defendant’s refusal to be videotaped, the defense notes that Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4017.1 permits but does not mandate videotaped depositions. The prosecution failed to obtain a specific court order compelling the defendant to submit to a videotaped deposition over his objection. Without a specific court order directing the defendant to appear and be videotaped, the refusal to do so cannot be characterized as a willful violation of a court order sufficient to justify the extreme sanction of default judgment. The trial court’s failure to distinguish between these periods or to find a willful violation of a specific order requires reversal.

**II. FAILURE TO CONSIDER LESSER SANCTIONS**

The prosecution contends that lesser sanctions would have been futile. The Court disagrees. Pennsylvania law is clear that default judgment is a sanction of last resort. In *Joseph v. Commonwealth Dept. of Corrections*, 613 A.2d 63 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1992), the court stated that "dismissal of an action or entry of a default judgment is a drastic sanction which should be imposed only in extreme circumstances and after a careful consideration of lesser sanctions." Similarly, *Wesley v. Department of Transportation*, 484 A.4d 1257 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1984), requires that the trial court impose the least severe sanction that will accomplish the desired purpose.

The defense correctly points out that the prosecution never requested monetary sanctions, fines, or adverse inference instructions prior to moving for default judgment. While the prosecution filed contempt motions, these do not equate to the court imposing or considering a graduated series of sanctions to compel compliance. The defense further notes that the prosecution never filed a motion to compel under Pa. R.C.P. 4011 to secure a binding court order for the defendant's appearance. The trial court’s leap directly to default judgment, without first attempting lesser measures to cure the non-compliance, was an abuse of discretion.

**III. PREJUDICE AND PLAINTIFF'S CONDUCT**

The prosecution argues that the prejudice is irreparable because the defendant was the sole eyewitness. While the loss of testimony is unfortunate, the defense correctly identifies that the prosecution failed to demonstrate prejudice with the particularity required by *Stackhouse v. Pennsylvania Railroad*, 171 A.2d 151 (Pa. 1961). The defense highlights that the physical evidence—an unattended pot and a defective kerosene container—suggests the fire was accidental or caused by product defect, rather than the defendant's negligence while he was asleep on the couch. Thus, the value of the defendant’s testimony to the plaintiff’s case is speculative.

Crucially, the defense points to the plaintiff’s own delay in pursuing discovery. The fire occurred in 2004, yet the plaintiff did not request the defendant’s deposition until 2007. Under *Lippincott v. Williams*, 897 A.2d 1207 (Pa. Super. 2006), the trial court must consider whether the moving party's conduct contributed to the need for sanctions. The plaintiff’s three-year delay in initiating discovery and failure to hire a private investigator until 2009 significantly contributed to the inability to secure the testimony before the defendant’s death. The trial court failed to adequately weigh this factor.

**IV. PROPORTIONALITY OF THE SANCTION**

Finally, the defense argues that the sanction of default judgment is disproportionate to the offense. *Smith v. Mitchell*, 453 A.2d 1018 (Pa. Super. 1982), states that "the sanction of dismissal or default must be proportionate to the offense." The sanction imposed here effectively adjudicates the defendant liable for the fire and the plaintiff’s damages. Given the tenuous connection between the defendant’s conduct (sleeping on the couch) and the cause of the fire (unattended pot/defective container), imposing liability based solely on discovery violations is grossly disproportionate. The prosecution’s reliance on *In re Estate of Rettig*, 871 A.2d 233 (Pa. Super. 2005), is misplaced, as that case involved a situation where liability was otherwise established, whereas here, default judgment determines the merits of a negligence claim that is not supported by the physical evidence.

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court abused its discretion by entering default judgment without making specific findings of willfulness, without considering lesser sanctions, and by failing to account for the plaintiff's contribution to the delay. The defense’s rebuttal successfully demonstrates that the procedural prerequisites for such an extreme sanction were not met.

**FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to Pa. R.A.P. 1928**

This matter comes before the Court on the appeal of Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, from the orders of the trial court entering default judgment as a discovery sanction. The Court has carefully reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. Upon consideration of the record, the applicable law, and the arguments presented, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling finding that the trial court abused its discretion in imposing the sanction of default judgment.

**I. SPECIFIC FINDINGS OF WILLFULNESS**

The prosecution argues that the trial court’s findings of willfulness were implicit in its order and that the defendant’s post-2009 conduct constituted deliberate obstruction. The Court is not persuaded. Pennsylvania law is clear that before a court may dismiss an action or enter default judgment as a sanction for noncompliance, it must make specific findings of fact that the failure to comply was willful. As held in *Fitzgerald v. McCutcheon*, 410 A.2d 334 (Pa. Super. 1979), the requirement of specific findings is not a mere formality; it ensures that the drastic sanction of default judgment is applied only where the record demonstrates a deliberate disregard of court orders.

Here, the trial court failed to make the requisite specific findings. The prosecution relies on the defendant’s refusal to appear for a videotaped deposition. However, as the defense correctly notes, Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4017.1 permits but does not mandate videotaped depositions. The defendant’s refusal to be videotaped, while arguably uncooperative, does not constitute a willful violation of a specific court order compelling such a procedure. The prosecution never obtained a court order overriding the defendant’s objection via a motion to compel under Pa. R.C.P. 4011. Without a specific order directing the defendant to submit to a videotaped deposition, the refusal cannot serve as the basis for a finding of willful contempt sufficient to justify default judgment. The trial court’s failure to distinguish between the periods where the defendant was unable to be located and the period where he objected to the form of the deposition, and its failure to make explicit findings of willfulness regarding a specific order, constitutes legal error.

**II. FAILURE TO CONSIDER LESSER SANCTIONS**

The prosecution contends that the filing of contempt motions in 2009 and 2010 satisfied the requirement to consider lesser sanctions. The Court disagrees. The imposition of default judgment is a sanction of last resort, to be utilized only after a careful consideration of lesser sanctions. In *Joseph v. Commonwealth Dept. of Corrections*, 613 A.2d 63 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1992), the court stated that "dismissal of an action or entry of a default judgment is a drastic sanction which should be imposed only in extreme circumstances and after a careful consideration of lesser sanctions." Similarly, *Wesley v. Department of Transportation*, 484 A.2d 1257 (Pa. Cmwlth. 1984), requires that the trial court impose the least severe sanction that will accomplish the desired purpose.

The record reflects that the prosecution never requested monetary sanctions, fines, or adverse inference instructions prior to moving for default judgment. The contempt motions cited by the prosecution were filed in pursuit of the ultimate sanction, not as a graduated attempt to compel compliance. The trial court failed to actively consider whether monetary penalties, preclusion of evidence, or adverse inferences might have cured the prejudice. The prosecution’s bare assertion that lesser sanctions would have been futile is unsupported by the record, as the defendant had no prior history of sanctions being imposed against him. The leap directly to default judgment violated the mandates of *Joseph* and *Wesley*.

**III. PREJUDICE AND PLAINTIFF'S CONDUCT**

The prosecution argues that the loss of the defendant’s testimony as the sole eyewitness constitutes irreparable prejudice. While the loss of evidence is unfortunate, the prosecution failed to demonstrate prejudice with the particularity required by *Stackhouse v. Pennsylvania Railroad*, 171 A.2d 151 (Pa. 1961). The defense correctly points out that the physical evidence—an unattended pot and a defective kerosene container—suggests the fire was accidental or caused by product defect. The defendant’s testimony, given that he was asleep on the couch at the time of the fire, would likely have been limited to confirming his lack of knowledge regarding the fire's origin. Thus, the prosecution’s claim that the testimony was essential to proving negligence is speculative.

Furthermore, the trial court failed to account for the plaintiff’s contribution to the delay. Under *Lippincott v. Williams*, 897 A.2d 1207 (Pa. Super. 2006), the court must consider whether the moving party's conduct contributed to the need for sanctions. The fire occurred in 2004, yet the plaintiff did not request the defendant’s deposition until 2007. This three-year delay, followed by the failure to hire a private investigator until 2009, significantly contributed to the inability to secure the testimony before the defendant’s death. The prosecution cannot now wholly blame the defendant for a loss of evidence that its own procrastination helped precipitate.

**IV. PROPORTIONALITY OF THE SANCTION**

Finally, the defense argues that the sanction of default judgment is disproportionate to the offense. *Smith v. Mitchell*, 453 A.2d 1018 (Pa. Super. 1982), states that "the sanction of dismissal or default must be proportionate to the offense." The sanction imposed here effectively adjudicates the defendant liable for the fire and the plaintiff’s damages. Given the tenuous connection between the defendant’s conduct (sleeping on the couch) and the cause of the fire (unattended pot/defective container), imposing liability based solely on discovery violations is grossly disproportionate. The prosecution’s reliance on *In re Estate of Rettig*, 871 A.2d 233 (Pa. Super. 2005), is misplaced, as that case involved a situation where liability was otherwise established, whereas here, default judgment determines the merits of a negligence claim that is not supported by the physical evidence.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court abused its discretion by entering default judgment without making specific findings of willfulness, without considering lesser sanctions, and by failing to account for the plaintiff's contribution to the delay. The defense’s rebuttal successfully demonstrates that the procedural prerequisites for such an extreme sanction were not met.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the orders of the trial court entering default judgment are **VACATED**, and the matter is **REMANDED** for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
