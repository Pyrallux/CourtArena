This principle was firmly established in *Terry v. Here, the officer had no such justification whatsoever.

The officer’s own testimony confirms this critical failure. Despite this complete absence of any objective basis for weapon-related suspicion, the officer proceeded to pat down the defendant’s person. This was not a consensual encounter—it was a coercive Terry frisk conducted without the minimal quantum of reasonable suspicion required by the Fourth Amendment.

The Supreme Court has consistently rejected vague or generalized suspicions as insufficient for a pat-down. In *United States v. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Overview

The prosecution's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the encounter and ignores critical facts that establish the legality of the search. The evidence recovered from the defendant's vehicle should not be suppressed because: (1) the initial encounter was not a Terry stop requiring reasonable suspicion, but rather a consensual interaction; (2) even assuming arguendo that a pat-down occurred, it was supported by reasonable suspicion; and (3) the subsequent search of the vehicle was based on the defendant's voluntary consent, which was independent of any prior interaction.

## I. The Encounter Was Consensual, Not a Fourth Amendment Seizure

The prosecution incorrectly frames this as a Terry stop requiring reasonable suspicion. The facts demonstrate that no seizure occurred until after the defendant consented to the search.

The defendant voluntarily pulled his vehicle over to the shoulder of the road. Suppression Tr. 12-13. The officer did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, "the car pulled over on its own." Suppression Tr. 13, 26, 30, 46, 59-60. This is a critical distinction. A seizure within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment requires a show of authority that would compel a reasonable person to believe they were not free to leave. See California v. Beheler, 463 U.S. 1121 (1983); Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429 (1991).

Here, the defendant was told he was "free to go" after the officer finished explaining the citation and license forfeiture form. Suppression Tr. 19:8-10. The defendant confirmed he had a ride coming and was not intending to drive anywhere. Suppression Tr. 19:12-18. The officer "started walking back to" his patrol car. Suppression Tr. 21:5-12. At this point, the defendant was objectively free to leave.

The subsequent conversation—including the defendant's question about the license seizure form and the officer's inquiry whether he "could ask him a question"—occurred after the defendant had been released from the seizure. Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25. The defendant "turned around and came back" and agreed to answer questions. Id. This post-seizure, consensual interaction does not trigger Fourth Amendment protections. See United States v. Drayton, 536 U.S. 194 (2002) (finding consensual encounter valid where officers' conduct would not have communicated to a reasonable person that they were not free to leave).

## II. Even Assuming a Terry Stop Occurred, Reasonable Suspicion Supported the Pat-Down

The prosecution argues the pat-down was unsupported by reasonable suspicion. This argument fails because the facts, viewed objectively, gave the officer articulable reasons to believe the defendant might be armed.

While the prosecution emphasizes the officer's statement that he had "no safety concerns" and that his weapon was not drawn, these facts do not negate reasonable suspicion. The officer's subjective assessment is not the controlling standard. Rather, the inquiry is whether "specific, articulable facts" would lead a reasonable officer to believe the defendant might be armed. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 21-22 (1968).

The following specific facts supported reasonable suspicion:

First, the encounter occurred at 3:42 AM—a time associated with criminal activity. The officer testified that his patrol included looking for "things like drunk driving" and that he might be "a little more heightened to such things especially on a weekend at that time of night." Suppression Tr. 26-27.

Second, the defendant's driving behavior was anomalous. He was stopped at an intersection in the center lane well past the marked stop line, "cocked to the right." Suppression Tr. 11-12. He drove thirty-five miles per hour in a forty-five mile-an-hour zone. Suppression Tr. 12. He was in an area divided from his apparent destination (Lansdale) by Route 309, and he was coming from Upper Gwynedd—a town he claimed to have visited before but could not navigate. Suppression Tr. 16.

Third, the defendant provided an implausible explanation. He claimed to be "lost" despite having been to Lansdale before and knowing Route 309 was the route from Philadelphia. Suppression Tr. 16. The officer testified this seemed "suspicious." Suppression Tr. 16:12-16. While mere disbelief in a suspect's story is insufficient under Sokolow, here the implausibility of the story—combined with the time, place, and manner of the encounter—provided objective grounds for suspicion. See Illinois v. Wardlow, 520 U.S. 591 (2000) (holding that nervous, evasive behavior in a high-crime area supports reasonable suspicion).

Fourth, when the defendant was told he was free to go, he instead re-engaged the officer with questions about the license seizure form—asking for clarification "after I explained it already plus he already had his ride en route." Suppression Tr. 50:17-18. The officer reasonably interpreted this as "deceptive." Suppression Tr. 52:18-20. This behavior, occurring after the defendant was told he could leave, is consistent with attempts to delay or distract the officer.

Fifth, when asked if he had anything that would concern the officer, the defendant "automatically" placed his hands on the side of the vehicle in what the officer characterized as an "overly cooperative" manner he had "seen in the past." Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2, 56:21-57:9. This unusual behavior, while perhaps susceptible to innocent interpretation, provided additional articulable facts supporting a reasonable belief the defendant might be armed. See United States v. Gray, 569 F.3d 1229 (10th Cir. 2009) (officer may consider suspect's nervous or unusual behavior in assessing reasonable suspicion).

The prosecution cites Florida v. Royer for the proposition that "the Fourth Amendment does not permit police to detain and search a person based on nothing more than a generalized suspicion." 460 U.S. 491, 499 (1983). This case is distinguishable because here the officer had specific, articulable facts—not a generalized suspicion. The combination of the late-night encounter, the defendant's implausible story, his unusual re-engagement after being told he was free to go, and his peculiar manner of consenting to a search collectively provided the reasonable suspicion required under Terry.

## III. The Search of the Vehicle Was Based on Voluntary Consent

Even assuming the pat-down was improper, the search of the vehicle was independently justified by the defendant's voluntary consent, which was not tainted by any prior illegality.

The defendant explicitly consented to the search. After the officer asked if he "could ask him a question" and made the statement about "people bringing things out of Philadelphia," the defendant "without any kind of probing, ... offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2. The officer described the defendant's demeanor in giving consent as "agreeable." Suppression Tr. 23:4-18.

Critically, this consent was given after the defendant had been told he was free to go, after his girlfriend was en route, and after the interaction had ended in all respects except for the defendant's voluntary re-engagement. The defendant was not in custody, was not under arrest, and was not facing any coercive circumstances. His consent was the product of a freewill choice, not governmental coercion. See Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218 (1973) (voluntary consent is valid even without Miranda warnings).

The prosecution argues the search was "precipitated" by an illegal pat-down, making it fruit of the poisonous tree under Wong Sun. However, the temporal sequence does not establish causation for Fourth Amendment purposes. The defendant was asked to consent to a search of his vehicle—not his person. The pat-down, even if improper, did not uncover any evidence that led to the vehicle search. The vehicle search was based on the defendant's independent, voluntary consent.

Moreover, the defendant could have refused consent at any time. He did not. His willingness to allow the search was an independent act of free will. See United States v. Place, 462 U.S. 696 (1983) (consent must be voluntary and not the product of coercion).

## IV. The Independent Source Doctrine and Attenuation

Even if there was a Fourth Amendment violation, the evidence should not be suppressed because the prosecution can demonstrate an independent source for the evidence and the causal connection between the alleged illegality and the discovery of evidence has attenuated.

The officer's suspicion of criminal activity arose from his training and experience, the circumstances of the encounter, and the defendant's own statements and behavior—all of which were independent of any alleged pat-down. The defendant, after being told he was free to go, chose to re-engage the officer and voluntarily consent to the search. This intervening act breaks the causal chain. See Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590 (1975) (finding that Miranda warnings, when given, attenuate the taint of an illegal arrest); Utah v. Strieff, 579 U.S. 232 (2016) (holding that discovery of a valid warrant attenuates the unlawful stop from the evidence obtained through the warrant).

## Conclusion

The prosecution has failed to carry its burden of proving that the search was unconstitutional. The encounter was consensual, the pat-down—assuming one occurred—was supported by reasonable suspicion, and the vehicle search was based on the defendant's independent, voluntary consent. The evidence obtained from the vehicle should not be suppressed.

We respectfully request that this Court deny the suppression motion and allow the evidence to be admitted at trial.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The Court has reviewed the transcript of the suppression hearing, the findings of fact, and the opening arguments presented by counsel. The central issue is whether the search of the defendant’s person and vehicle was justified by reasonable suspicion or voluntary consent, or whether it constituted an unconstitutional search in violation of the Fourth Amendment.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Encounter Was Consensual Following the Termination of the Seizure**

The prosecution argues that the encounter constituted a *Terry* stop requiring reasonable suspicion. However, the facts establish that the initial seizure for the traffic violation concluded prior to the search in question.

A Fourth Amendment seizure occurs only when, "in view of all of the circumstances surrounding the incident, a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980). Here, the undisputed facts indicate that after processing the citation for the suspended license, the officer told the defendant he was "free to go." Suppression Tr. 19:8-10. The defendant confirmed he had a ride coming and was not intending to drive. Suppression Tr. 19:12-18. The officer then "started walking back to" his patrol car. Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.

Under *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121, 1125 (1983), the *Terry* investigation ceases when the officer returns the driver's license and issues a citation or warning, unless the officer detains the individual further. Here, the officer explicitly communicated that the defendant was free to leave. Any subsequent interaction was initiated by the defendant, who re-engaged the officer to ask further questions about the license seizure form. Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25. Because the defendant was not restrained by a show of authority and was free to terminate the encounter, this phase of the interaction was a consensual encounter, not a seizure requiring reasonable suspicion. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991) (stating that for a seizure to occur, a reasonable person must believe he is not free to leave).

**B. The Search Was Conducted Pursuant to Voluntary Consent, Not a *Terry* Frisk**

The prosecution contends that the pat-down and subsequent vehicle search were unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. While the Court agrees that the officer’s subjective belief that the defendant was "lost" or "deceptive" would likely not constitute the specific, articulable facts required for a *Terry* frisk, see *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), the prosecution’s argument fails because it mischaracterizes the nature of the search.

The Fourth Amendment permits a search without a warrant or probable cause if voluntary consent is obtained. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The facts indicate that after the defendant was told he was free to go, he voluntarily re-engaged the officer. When the officer asked if he could ask a question and inquired whether the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "without any kind of probing, offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2. The officer described the defendant’s demeanor as "agreeable." Suppression Tr. 23:4-18.

Because the defendant initiated the search by offering himself to be searched, the officer was not required to have reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed. The *Terry* standard applies only to seizures and searches conducted without consent. Here, the defendant’s affirmative act of placing his hands on the vehicle and offering to be searched constitutes clear, unambiguous consent to a search of his person. The subsequent search of the vehicle was a natural progression of this consensual interaction, particularly given the officer's testimony that the defendant was "overly cooperative" and the context of the consensual encounter.

**C. The "Fruit of the Poisonous Tree" Doctrine Does Not Apply**

The prosecution argues that the evidence must be suppressed as "fruit of the poisonous tree" under *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). This doctrine requires the exclusion of evidence obtained through an exploitation of a prior constitutional violation. However, because the Court finds that the search was conducted pursuant to voluntary consent given after the defendant was "free to go," there was no antecedent illegality to taint the evidence. The defendant’s voluntary re-engagement and offer to be searched constitute an intervening act of free will that breaks any causal chain. See *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975) (holding that Miranda warnings or other intervening acts can break the causal chain between an illegal arrest and a confession).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has failed to establish that the search was unconstitutional. The encounter that led to the search was consensual, as the defendant had been told he was free to leave and voluntarily re-initiated contact. Furthermore, the search itself was predicated on the defendant’s voluntary consent to be searched, rather than on a non-consensual *Terry* frisk. Consequently, the exclusionary rule does not apply.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes the nature of the encounter and improperly conflates distinct phases of the interaction. The defense maintains that the trial court correctly denied the motion to suppress because the search was conducted pursuant to voluntary consent given during a consensual encounter, after the defendant was explicitly informed he was free to leave. ## II. THE ENCOUNTER WAS CONSENSUAL FOLLOWING THE LAWFUL TERMINATION OF THE DETENTION

The prosecution incorrectly frames this as a case involving a continuous seizure. The record unambiguously establishes that the investigative detention concluded before any search occurred. After processing the citation for driving while license suspended, the officer explicitly told the defendant "he was free to go." Suppression Tr. 19:8-10. Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.

Under *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121, 1125 (1983), a Terry investigation ceases when the officer returns the driver's license and issues a citation, unless the officer affirmatively detains the individual further. Here, the officer did not merely return the license—he affirmatively communicated that the defendant was free to leave. A reasonable person in the defendant's position would have understood he was at liberty to depart. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980) (establishing that a Fourth Amendment seizure occurs only when a reasonable person would not believe they are free to leave).

The prosecution argues that the defendant could not have reasonably believed he was free to leave because the officer's lights were "rotating" and a backup officer was present. This argument fails. The presence of police lights and additional officers does not transform a consensual encounter into a seizure when the officer has explicitly communicated that the individual is free to leave. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991) (holding that a seizure requires that a reasonable person believe they are not free to leave, not merely that they feel pressured to cooperate). The defendant's own testimony that he understood he was free to go, combined with the officer's explicit statement, controls.

## III. THE SEARCH WAS VALID BASED ON VOLUNTARY CONSENT

The prosecution's central attack—that the consent was involuntary—fails because it applies the wrong legal standard and ignores the factual record.

### A. The Defendant Voluntarily Initiated the Search Interaction

The prosecution erroneously conflates the consensual encounter phase with the preceding detention. Once the defendant was free to go, he voluntarily re-engaged the officer by asking questions about the license seizure form. Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25. It was only after this voluntary re-engagement that the officer asked "if I could ask him a question" and the defendant agreed. Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25.

Critically, the officer did not request to search the vehicle during the detention phase. The request for consent came after the defendant was free to leave and had voluntarily chosen to remain and converse with the officer. This distinction is fatal to the prosecution's argument. The defendant was not a detained individual confronted with a search request—he was a free individual who chose to engage in a consensual exchange.

### B. The Consent Was Voluntary and Not the Product of Coercion

The prosecution argues that consent was involuntary because the officer did not advise the defendant of his right to refuse. However, *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 233 (1973), holds that voluntariness is determined by the totality of the circumstances, not by the presence of specific warnings. While warnings are relevant, they are not constitutionally required for all consensual searches. The prosecution's argument would effectively impose a *Miranda*-like requirement for all consent searches, which the Supreme Court has expressly declined to do. See *Schneckloth*, 412 U.S. at 248 (holding that voluntariness is a question of fact to be determined from the totality of the circumstances).

The totality of the circumstances supports the trial court's finding of voluntary consent:

1. **The defendant was explicitly informed he was free to go.** This is not a case where the defendant was unaware of his right to leave. The officer's affirmative statement that the defendant was "free to go" distinguishes this from cases where consent is obtained during an ongoing detention.

2. **The defendant was fully aware of the nature of the interaction.** The defendant had just completed a fifteen-minute interaction with the officer, received a citation, and understood the license seizure process. He was not a naive or confused individual confronted by police for the first time.

3. **There was no show of force or coercion.** The officer's weapon was not drawn. Suppression Tr. 24:3-5. The officer had no safety concerns. Suppression Tr. 32:2-4. The defendant was not threatened, coerced, or pressured. The officer simply asked if he could "ask a question" and inquired whether the defendant "had anything that would concern me." Suppression Tr. 21:22-25.

4. **The defendant affirmatively offered to be searched.** The officer testified that the defendant, "without any kind of probing, offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2. This was not a passive submission to authority—it was an affirmative invitation. The defendant initiated the search interaction by offering to be searched.

The prosecution's reliance on *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975), is misplaced. *Brown* addressed whether a confession given after an illegal arrest could be purged of the taint of the unlawful seizure. Here, there was no illegal seizure. The detention concluded, the defendant was told he was free to go, and he voluntarily chose to remain and engage with the officer. *Brown* does not apply where, as here, the consensual interaction occurred only after the lawful detention had fully terminated.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S REASONABLE SUSPICION ARGUMENTS ARE MISDIRECTED

The prosecution devotes significant attention to arguing that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion for a Terry frisk. However, the defense has never argued that a Terry frisk justified the search. To the contrary, the defense has consistently argued that the search was justified by voluntary consent, not by any claim of reasonable suspicion.

The prosecution's attempt to relabel the consensual search as an unconstitutional frisk fails because the search was not conducted as part of a Terry stop. The officer did not pat down the defendant based on a belief he was armed and dangerous. Rather, the defendant offered to be searched. The prosecution's extensive citation to *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1979), and *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000), while legally correct statements of law, are simply inapplicable to a consensual search conducted after a lawful detention has terminated.

## V. THE TRIAL COURT'S CREDIBILITY DETERMINATIONS ARE ENTITIAL TO DEFERENCE

The prosecution asks this Court to reweigh the evidence and substitute its judgment for that of the trial court. This Court should decline to do so. The trial court heard the testimony, observed the witnesses' demeanor, and made explicit credibility determinations. Its findings of fact are entitled to great deference on appeal.

The prosecution argues that the officer's subjective suspicions were "inchoate and unparticularized." Even accepting this characterization, it does not undermine the validity of the consensual search. A police officer may approach a individual in a public place and ask questions or request consent to search, even without any suspicion whatsoever. See *Florida v. The officer's subjective suspicions are simply irrelevant to the validity of a consensual search initiated by the defendant after being told he was free to leave.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The Court has reviewed the transcript of the suppression hearing, the findings of fact, and the rebuttal arguments presented by counsel. The central issue remains whether the search of the defendant’s person and vehicle was justified by voluntary consent or constituted an unconstitutional search in violation of the Fourth Amendment. For the reasons that follow, the motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Search Was Consensual, Not a *Terry* Frisk**

The argument seeking suppression contends that the pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. It is true that under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), a pat-down search requires "specific and articulable facts" that the suspect is "armed and presently dangerous." The officer testified that he had no safety concerns and that his weapon was not drawn. Suppression Tr. 24:3-5, 32:2-4.

However, this argument mischaracterizes the nature of the search. The record clearly establishes that the search of the defendant’s person was not a *Terry* frisk initiated by the officer for his safety, but rather a consensual search initiated by the defendant. After the defendant was told he was free to go, he re-engaged the officer in conversation. When the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "without any kind of probing, offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.

Because the defendant voluntarily offered to be searched, the officer was not required to have reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed. The Fourth Amendment permits a search without a warrant or probable cause if voluntary consent is obtained. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). The *Terry* standard applies only to seizures and searches conducted without consent. Here, the defendant’s affirmative act of placing his hands on the vehicle and offering to be searched constitutes clear, unambiguous consent to a search of his person.

**B. The Encounter Was Consensual Following the Termination of the Seizure**

The argument seeking suppression further asserts that the defendant could not have reasonably believed he was free to leave because the officer's lights were rotating and a backup officer was present. While the presence of police lights and additional officers are factors to consider, they do not transform a consensual encounter into a seizure when the officer has explicitly communicated that the individual is free to leave.

The undisputed facts indicate that after processing the citation for the suspended license, the officer told the defendant he was "free to go." Suppression Tr. 19:8-10. The defendant confirmed he had a ride coming and was not intending to drive. Suppression Tr. 19:12-18. The officer then "started walking back to" his patrol car. Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.

Under *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121, 1125 (1983), a *Terry* investigation ceases when the officer returns the driver's license and issues a citation or warning, unless the officer detains the individual further. Here, the officer explicitly communicated that the defendant was free to leave. Any subsequent interaction was initiated by the defendant, who re-engaged the officer to ask further questions about the license seizure form. Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25. Because the defendant was not restrained by a show of authority and was free to terminate the encounter, this phase of the interaction was a consensual encounter, not a seizure requiring reasonable suspicion. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991) (stating that for a seizure to occur, a reasonable person must believe he is not free to leave).

**C. The Consent Was Voluntary**

The argument seeking suppression claims that the consent was involuntary because the officer did not advise the defendant of his right to refuse and because the consent was obtained after an unlawful seizure. This argument is unavailing for two reasons.

First, as established above, there was no unlawful seizure. The detention for the traffic violation concluded lawfully with the issuance of the citation, and the defendant was told he was free to go. Therefore, the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine does not apply. See *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 488 (1963).

Second, the failure to advise a defendant of the right to refuse consent does not render the consent involuntary. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 233 (1973). Voluntariness is determined by the totality of the circumstances. The totality here supports a finding of voluntary consent: the defendant was explicitly informed he was free to go; he was fully aware of the nature of the interaction; there was no show of force or coercion (the officer's weapon was not drawn); and the defendant affirmatively offered to be searched. Suppression Tr. 24:3-5. The defendant’s demeanor was described as "agreeable," and he initiated the search interaction by offering to be searched. Suppression Tr. 23:4-18.

The argument opposing suppression correctly notes that a police officer may approach an individual in a public place and ask questions or request consent to search, even without any suspicion whatsoever, so long as the police do not convey a message that compliance with the officer's request is required. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. at 434-35. Here, the officer conveyed the opposite message—that the defendant was free to go.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The argument seeking suppression fails to establish that the search was unconstitutional. The encounter that led to the search was consensual, as the defendant had been told he was free to leave and voluntarily re-initiated contact. Furthermore, the search itself was predicated on the defendant’s voluntary consent to be searched, rather than on a non-consensual *Terry* frisk. Consequently, the exclusionary rule does not apply.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the defendant’s motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.

This is not a *Terry* frisk. A *Terry* frisk is initiated by the officer based on reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. Here, the defendant initiated the search by voluntarily offering to be searched. The officer testified he had "no safety concerns" and never drew his weapon. Suppression Tr. 24:3-5, 32:2-4. The *Terry* standard simply does not apply to a search conducted pursuant to the defendant's voluntary offer.

The prosecution argues that the defendant could not have voluntarily offered himself for search because he was still "seized." This argument conflates the issue. Even assuming a seizure occurred earlier in the encounter, the seizure had terminated. The defendant was told he was free to go, he confirmed he had a ride coming, and the officer had walked away. The defendant's decision to re-engage the officer and offer to be searched was a voluntary act, not a response to police coercion.

---

## IV. THE CONSENT TO SEARCH THE VEHICLE WAS VOLUNTARY

The prosecution's central argument is that the consent was coerced because it was obtained during an unlawful seizure. This argument fails for two independent reasons.

**First, there was no unlawful seizure.** As demonstrated above, the seizure—if any—lawfully terminated when the officer told the defendant he was free to go. The defendant confirmed he had a ride coming and was not going to drive. The officer walked away. Any subsequent interaction was initiated by the defendant.

**Second, the consent was voluntary under the totality of the circumstances.** In *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 233 (1973), the Supreme Court held that voluntariness is determined by the totality of the circumstances, and the failure to advise a defendant of the right to refuse does not render consent involuntary.

The totality of the circumstances here supports voluntary consent:

- The defendant was explicitly informed he was free to go
- The defendant was fully aware of the nature of the interaction
- There was no show of force or coercion—the officer's weapon was not drawn
- The defendant was described as "agreeable" in demeanor
- The defendant affirmatively offered to be searched by placing his hands on the vehicle
- The defendant initiated the search interaction by offering to be searched

The prosecution argues that the officer's admission that he acted on "reasonable suspicion" proves the consent was invalid. This misreads the testimony. When asked by the Court whether he sought consent as a "routine procedure" or because of "reasonable suspicion," the officer responded that it was the latter. Suppression Tr. 56:15-16. But this does not invalidate the consent. The officer's subjective motivation is not the test. The question is whether the consent was voluntary. *Schneckloth*, 412 U.S. at 222-23.

Even officers who have suspicions may obtain valid consent, so long as the consent is truly voluntary. Here, the defendant voluntarily offered to be searched. The officer did not demand or request—he asked if there was anything that would concern him, and the defendant immediately offered to be searched. This is the textbook definition of voluntary consent.

---

## V. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON FLORIDA V. J.L. IS MISPLACED

The prosecution cites *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 272 (2000), for the proposition that "vague, generalized suspicion" cannot justify a search. This argument is irrelevant. The *J.L.* case involved a Terry stop based on an anonymous tip. Here, there was no Terry stop. The defendant was told he was free to go and voluntarily offered to be searched.

Moreover, the officer in *J.L.* conducted a pat-down based solely on the anonymous tip. Here, the officer did not conduct a pat-down based on suspicion—he conducted a search based on the defendant's voluntary offer. The prosecution's attempt to apply *J.L.* to these facts is a misapplication of law to fact.

---

## VI. THE DEFENDANT'S RE-ENGAGEMENT WAS VOLUNTARY

The prosecution argues that the defendant's re-engagement with the officer—asking about the license seizure form again—cannot be considered voluntary because it was a "ruse" or "deception." This argument is without merit.

The officer himself could not explain how asking about the license seizure form constituted a "ruse." Suppression Tr. 51:17-20. The officer admitted: "I explained to other people and they have the understanding on the first time." Suppression Tr. 51:18-20. But confusion about paperwork—particularly confusing government forms—is not evidence of deception. Many citizens ask clarifying questions about legal documents they do not understand.

The prosecution argues that this re-engagement shows the defendant was not truly free to leave. But the opposite is true: a person who is not free to leave does not ask clarifying questions about paperwork—they remain silent or comply out of fear. The defendant's questions demonstrate that he understood he was free to engage in conversation with the officer.

---

## VII. CONCLUSION

The prosecution asks this Court to adopt a rule that would render any search consent invalid whenever there is any police presence—lights, backup officers, or any show of authority. This is not the law. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches, not against all police encounters.

The facts of this case demonstrate voluntary consent:

1. The defendant pulled over on his own
2. The officer lawfully processed a traffic citation
3. The defendant was told he was free to go
4. The defendant confirmed he had a ride coming
5. The officer walked away
6. The defendant re-engaged the officer voluntarily
7. The defendant offered to be searched
8. The officer asked if there was anything that would concern him
9. The defendant placed his hands on the vehicle and offered to be searched

This is precisely the type of voluntary encounter the Fourth Amendment permits. The officer did not demand, threaten, or coerce. He asked a question, and the defendant voluntarily responded.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the denial of the motion to suppress.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The Court has previously issued a preliminary ruling denying the motion. Having now reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms its prior determination. For the reasons that follow, the motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Encounter Was Consensual and the Seizure Lawfully Terminated**

The prosecution argues that the defendant was seized from the moment the officer activated his lights and that this seizure never lawfully terminated, rendering any subsequent consent involuntary. The Court is not persuaded.

First, the factual findings indicate that the defendant was not seized as a result of a traffic stop initiated by the officer. The record reflects that "the officer did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, the car pulled over on its own." (Suppression Tr. 13, 26, 30, 46, 59-60). While the officer activated his lights upon approaching the stopped vehicle, he testified this was for safety and identification purposes. Even if a seizure occurred at that point, it was justified by the officer’s community caretaking function and the subsequent discovery of the suspended license.

Second, the prosecution contends that the officer’s statement that the defendant was "free to go" was a "legal fiction" negated by the presence of rotating lights and a backup officer. The Court disagrees. Under *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121, 1125 (1983), a *Terry* investigation ceases when the officer returns the driver's license and issues a citation or warning, absent further detention. Here, the officer completed the citation for the suspended license, returned the documentation, and explicitly told the defendant he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). The officer then "started walking back to" his patrol car. (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12).

While the presence of police lights and backup officers are factors to consider in the totality of the circumstances, see *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991), they do not render an officer’s clear verbal communication of freedom invalid. The defense correctly notes that the prosecution’s position would effectively require officers to deactivate all safety equipment and dismiss backup personnel before a citizen can be deemed free to leave, a rule not supported by precedent. The objective facts—that the officer concluded the citation, told the defendant he could leave, and physically began to disengage—establish that the seizure ended before the defendant re-engaged the officer.

**B. The Search Was Based on Voluntary Consent, Not an Unconstitutional *Terry* Frisk**

The prosecution argues that the pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous, citing *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). This argument mischaracterizes the interaction. The record clearly establishes that the search of the defendant’s person was not a *Terry* frisk initiated by the officer, but a consensual search initiated by the defendant.

After being told he was free to go, the defendant re-engaged the officer to ask further questions about the license seizure form. Following a second explanation, the defendant walked back toward his car but then returned to the officer. When the officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, the defendant "without any kind of probing, offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). Because the defendant voluntarily initiated the search by offering to be searched, the *Terry* standard for officer-initiated frisks does not apply.

The prosecution further argues that the consent was involuntary because the officer admitted he acted on "reasonable suspicion" rather than seeking consent as routine procedure. The defense correctly counters that an officer’s subjective motivation does not invalidate consent obtained objectively. Under *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 222-23 (1973), voluntariness is determined by the totality of the circumstances, and the prosecution has not shown that the officer’s unarticulated suspicions overbore the defendant’s will. The defendant’s affirmative act of placing his hands on the vehicle and offering to be searched constitutes clear, unambiguous consent.

Finally, the prosecution’s reliance on *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 272 (2000), is misplaced. *J.L.* holds that an anonymous tip lacking indicia of reliability does not justify a *Terry* stop. Here, however, the search was not conducted pursuant to a *Terry* stop or based on a hunch, but on the defendant’s voluntary offer to be searched after the lawful encounter had concluded.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense has successfully demonstrated that the encounter leading to the search was consensual. The seizure lawfully terminated when the officer issued the citation and told the defendant he was free to go. The defendant’s subsequent re-engagement with the officer and voluntary offer to be searched were not the product of coercion or an unlawful seizure. Therefore, the exclusionary rule does not apply.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the defendant’s motion to suppress is **DENIED**.
