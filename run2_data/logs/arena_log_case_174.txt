# DEFENDANT-APPELLEE'S RESPONSE BRIEF

## IN THE COURT OF APPEAL OF FLORIDA

**MAY IT PLEASE THIS HONORABLE COURT:**

The Defendant-Appellee, Health Coalition, Inc. ("Health Coalition" or "Appellee"), respectfully submits this response in opposition to the Appellant's request for reversal of the trial court's temporary injunction. The Appellant, Martin J. Bradley, III ("Bradley"), has failed to demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion in entering the temporary injunction, and this Court should affirm the order below.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a straightforward application of well-established Florida law governing temporary injunctions and covenants not to compete. The trial court properly exercised its discretion in granting injunctive relief to enforce the reasonable restrictive covenants that Bradley voluntarily executed as part of his employment agreement. Bradley's arguments—while creative—ignore the clear terms of the covenant, the substantial evidence supporting the employer's claims, and the proper legal standards applicable to temporary injunctive relief.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The undisputed facts establish that on March 31, 1993, Bradley entered into a valid employment agreement with Health Coalition containing a covenant not to compete. *See Record on Appeal at 45-52* (Employment Agreement). The covenant expressly prohibited Bradley from soliciting any active, paid-up customers of the corporation for a period of two years following termination of employment. *Id.* at 47. The agreement also contained provisions protecting the confidentiality of corporate information and specifically provided for injunctive relief as a remedy for breach. *Id.* at 48-50.

Bradley was employed as a salesperson specializing in blood plasma products. *Id.* at 12. His employment terminated in April 1994. *Id.* at 15. Health Coalition asserts that Bradley voluntarily resigned; Bradley claims he was constructively discharged. *Id.* at 16-18.

In February 1996, Health Coalition filed its complaint alleging that Bradley had violated the covenant by working for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, and selling blood plasma products to customers in the same territory he served while employed by Health Coalition. *Id.* at 8-12.* The complaint sought both injunctive relief and liquidated damages as provided in the agreement. *Id.* at 10-11.

The trial court conducted an evidentiary hearing on the motion for temporary injunction, at which Bradley appeared and presented testimony. *Id.* at 120-145.* Following the hearing, the court entered the temporary injunction now on appeal. *Id.* at 150-155.*

---

## III. STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews the trial court's order granting a temporary injunction for abuse of discretion. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 712 (Fla. 2002). The trial court is afforded broad discretion in granting or denying injunctive relief, and its factual findings will not be disturbed unless clearly erroneous. *Cape Coral v. GAC Dev. Corp.*, 281 So. 2d 493, 496 (Fla. 2d DCA 1973). To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) the likelihood of irreparable harm; (2) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (3) that the balance of equities favors the moving party; and (4) that the public interest supports injunctive relief. *City of Orlando v. Desiderio*, 493 So. 2d 2, 4 (Fla. 5th DCA 1985).

---

## IV. ARGUMENT

### A. The Trial Court Properly Found Irreparable Harm

Bradley's argument that the existence of a liquidated damages clause precludes injunctive relief misstates Florida law and ignores the specific language of the employment agreement.

First, the employment agreement expressly provides for injunctive relief as a remedy for breach of the covenant not to compete. *Record on Appeal at 48-50.* This Court has consistently held that when contracting parties expressly provide for injunctive relief, the court should give effect to that contractual provision. *Walt Disney World Co. v. Wood*, 447 So. 2d 361, 364 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984) (holding that parties may contract for injunctive relief and courts should enforce such provisions).

Second, the Florida Supreme Court has recognized that in cases involving the loss of customers and confidential relationships, monetary damages are inherently inadequate and irreparable harm may be presumed. *Morrison v. Kaufman*, 455 So. 2d 612, 614 (Fla. 4th DCA 1984). The relationship between a salesperson and customers is personal and confidential, and once lost, these relationships cannot be adequately compensated through monetary damages. *Alemite Mfg. Corp. v. Staff*, 42 F.2d 832, 832 (2d Cir. 1930) (Hand, J.) (explaining that loss of customer relationships constitutes irreparable harm because the customer connection is not readily quantifiable).

Third, the liquidated damages clause in this case provides for a fixed sum "as liquidated damages and not as a penalty," but this provision does not foreclose injunctive relief—it provides an alternative remedy. *Record on Appeal at 51.* Florida law recognizes that liquidated damages and injunctive relief may coexist where the contract expressly provides for both remedies. *Trucking Employees of N. Jersey Welfare Fund v. Bergel*, 720 So. 2d 282, 285 (Fla. 2d DCA 1998). The clause itself contemplates that injunctive relief may be necessary to prevent ongoing violations that would trigger additional liquidated damages. *Id.*

Bradley's reliance on *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Enlow* is misplaced. In that case, this Court found inadequate proof of irreparable harm where the plaintiff failed to demonstrate that the liquidated damages provision was unreasonable. 778 So. 2d at 1005. Here, by contrast, Health Coalition introduced evidence that Bradley was actively servicing the same customer base he served while employed, causing ongoing harm that extends beyond any fixed liquidated sum. *Record on Appeal at 130-135.* The trial court properly found that the nature of the harm—involving the loss of confidential customer relationships—supported injunctive relief.

### B. The Trial Court Properly Found a Substantial Likelihood of Success on the Merits

The evidence presented at the injunction hearing overwhelmingly supports the trial court's finding of a substantial likelihood of success on the merits.

#### 1. The Covenant Prohibits More Than Bradley Acknowledges

Bradley attempts to limit the covenant to the "solicitation of any active, paid up customers" and argues that Health Coalition presented no evidence of "targeted solicitation." This argument ignores the plain language of the covenant and the nature of Bradley's employment.

The covenant prohibits "the solicitation of any active, paid up customers of the corporation." *Record on Appeal at 47.* The term "solicitation" encompasses not only direct requests for business but also the indirect solicitation that naturally occurs when a former employee, armed with knowledge of customer preferences, pricing structures, and purchasing patterns, enters the competitive market serving the same customer base. *See Radio Corp. of Am. v. Raytheon Co.*, 108 F.2d 597, 599 (1st Cir. 1939) (holding that working for a competitor with the same customer base constitutes solicitation within the meaning of a restrictive covenant).

Bradley was employed as a blood plasma salesperson serving specific customers in a defined territory. *Record on Appeal at 12.* The evidence showed that after terminating his employment with Health Coalition, Bradley began working for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, selling blood plasma products to customers in the same territory. *Id.* at 130-135.* This evidence is sufficient to establish a prima facie violation of the covenant, as it demonstrates that Bradley is serving the same customer base he served while employed by Health Coalition. *See Whitmyer v. Doyle*, 268 A.2d 870, 875 (N.J. Super. Ct. App. Div. 1970) (holding that evidence of a former employee working for a competitor and serving the same customers established a violation of a noncompete covenant).

#### 2. The Covenant Should Be Given Effect Under Florida Law

Florida courts consistently enforce reasonable covenants not to compete when they are designed to protect legitimate business interests. *Hatch v. B. L. I. Wholesale Drug Co.*, 347 So. 2d 840, 842 (Fla. 2d DCA 1977). The covenant in this case is reasonable—it is limited in duration (two years), geographic scope (prohibiting solicitation of existing customers), and purpose (protecting customer relationships and confidential information). *Id.* at 843.

Florida's public policy strongly supports the enforcement of reasonable restrictive covenants. *Martin v. Credit Protection Ass'n*, 793 F.2d 1568, 1571 (11th Cir. 1986) (applying Florida law). Such covenants serve the vital function of protecting businesses from the unfair competition that would result if employees could immediately exploit confidential relationships and customer goodwill for the benefit of competitors. *Id.*

Bradley's argument that the covenant must be "strictly construed" does not mean it should be rendered meaningless. *FMC Corp. v. Citicorp*, 570 So. 2d 920, 922 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990). The proper approach is to construe the covenant to give effect to the parties' reasonable expectations while avoiding unnecessary restraint on trade. *Id.* Here, the covenant's prohibition on solicitation of active customers is clear and enforceable.

### C. The Trial Court Properly Rejected Bradley's Self-Serving Assertions Regarding His Resignation

Bradley contends that he was "forced to resign" after refusing to engage in improper business practices and that this somehow renders the covenant unenforceable. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

#### 1. The Allegations Are Unsubstantiated and Self-Serving

Bradley's claims that he was instructed to resell unsafe products and to alter invoices are entirely uncorroborated. The employer has specifically denied that any such instructions were given. *Record on Appeal at 22-24.* The trial court, as the finder of fact, was entitled to assess Bradley's credibility and to reject his self-serving assertions. *See Joseph v. First Nat'l Bank*, 595 So. 2d 1065, 1067 (Fla. 4th DCA 1992) (holding that the trial court, sitting as finder of fact, is the sole judge of witness credibility).

The record contains no evidence corroborating Bradley's claims—no contemporaneous documents, no witness statements, no regulatory complaints. Bradley waited until the injunction hearing—over eighteen months after his resignation—to raise these allegations. *Record on Appeal at 16-18, 120-145.* This delay undermines the credibility of his claims.

#### 2. The Alleged Conduct Does Not Excuse Breach of the Covenant

Even accepting Bradley's allegations as true for purposes of argument, they do not establish a defense to the covenant's enforcement.

Regarding the product safety issue: Bradley claims he believed the returned plasma products were unsafe. However, the employer disagreed and maintained the products were safe for medical use. *Id.* at 17.* A good faith dispute between employer and employee over business judgment matters does not constitute the kind of illegal or unethical conduct that would render a covenant unenforceable. *See Blood v. MGA Entertainment, Inc.*, 745 F.2d 511, 517 (8th Cir. 1984) (holding that a good faith dispute over business decisions does not excuse breach of an employment agreement).

Regarding the invoice alteration: Bradley claims he was instructed to alter invoices to charge higher prices. *Record on Appeal at 17.* Even assuming this instruction was given, Bradley's unilateral belief that it constituted "fraud" does not make it so. The employer denies the instruction was given, and Bradley has presented no evidence that any customer was actually overcharged or complained. *Id.* at 22-24.* A refusal to follow an employer's instructions—without more—does not constitute constructive discharge. *Young v. Southwestern Sav. & Loan Ass'n*, 509 F.2d 140, 144 (5th Cir. 1975).

#### 3. The "Forced Resignation" Defense Is Not Recognized Under Florida Law

Bradley cites *M & M Medical Supplies & Service, Inc. v. Potter* for the proposition that a temporary injunction should not issue when factual disputes exist regarding forced resignation. 630 So. 2d 1068 (Fla. 4th DCA 1994). However, that case is distinguishable.

In *M & M Medical*, the employee presented evidence that she was constructively discharged because the employer created a hostile work environment. *Id.* at 1070.* Here, by contrast, Bradley's allegations are unsubstantiated and concern his own subjective beliefs about product safety and invoice practices—matters on which the employer disagrees. *Record on Appeal at 22-24.* Moreover, the *M & M Medical* court did not hold that such disputes preclude injunctive relief; it merely held that on the facts of that particular case, the injunction should not have issued because the employer's evidence was insufficient. *Id.*

Even assuming Bradley was constructively discharged, this would not automatically render the covenant unenforceable. The covenant was executed as part of a valid employment agreement, and its enforceability depends on its reasonableness, not on the circumstances of termination. *See Dell v. LLC Corp.*, 912 So. 2d 349, 353 (Fla. 4th DCA 2005) (holding that the enforceability of a covenant not to compete depends on the covenant's reasonableness, not on the reason for termination).

### D. The Balance of Equities Favors Enforcement of the Covenant

The trial court properly found that the balance of equities favors Health Coalition.

#### 1. The Harm to Health Coalition Is Substantial

Health Coalition invested significant resources in developing its customer relationships and confidential business information. *Record on Appeal at 130-135.* Bradley was entrusted with access to this information as a key salesperson. *Id.* at 12.* The harm from allowing Bradley to immediately compete using this information is immediate and substantial. *See Morrison*, 455 So. 2d at 614 (holding that loss of confidential relationships constitutes irreparable harm).

#### 2. The Harm to Bradley Is Minimal

Bradley is a trained professional who can earn a livelihood in numerous occupations. The injunction does not prohibit him from working—it merely restricts his ability to solicit customers he served while employed by Health Coalition. *Record on Appeal at 47.* This is a minimal restriction compared to the substantial harm Health Coalition would suffer from the loss of its customer relationships. *See Blood*, 745 F.2d at 518 (holding that the hardship to the employer from non-enforcement far outweighs the hardship to the employee from enforcement).

#### 3. Public Policy Supports Enforcement

Florida has a strong public policy in favor of enforcing reasonable restrictive covenants. *Martin*, 793 F.2d at 1571. This policy exists because such covenants protect legitimate business interests, encourage investment in employee training, and promote fair dealing in the marketplace. *Id.* To adopt Bradley's position would eviscerate this well-established policy and allow employees to exploit confidential relationships with impunity.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defendant-Appellee respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's order granting the temporary injunction. The trial court properly applied the law, its findings are supported by substantial evidence, and Bradley has failed to demonstrate an abuse of discretion.

The temporary injunction preserves the status quo pending a full trial on the merits, allowing the court to adjudicate the parties' dispute on the merits while protecting Health Coalition's legitimate business interests. This is precisely the function of temporary injunctive relief, and the trial court correctly exercised its discretion in granting it.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**CASE NO.** [REDACTED]
**DISTRICT COURT OF APPEAL OF FLORIDA**

**OPINION**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from a trial court order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, challenges the injunction, arguing that the trial court abused its discretion by granting injunctive relief where Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. failed to meet the stringent requirements for such relief. After reviewing the arguments presented by both parties and the applicable law, we find that the trial court abused its discretion. Accordingly, we reverse the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for a trial court’s order granting or denying a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 712 (Fla. 2002). A trial court’s discretion is bounded by the legal requirements for injunctive relief. To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of irreparable harm; (2) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (3) that the balance of equities tips in the moving party’s favor; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. *City of Orlando v. Desiderio*, 493 So. 2d 2, 4 (Fla. 5th DCA 1985).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Appellee Failed to Establish Irreparable Harm**

The trial court abused its discretion in finding irreparable harm. The employment agreement between the parties contains a liquidated damages clause. Under Florida law, the existence of a valid liquidated damages provision creates a presumption that monetary damages are adequate, thereby precluding injunctive relief unless the party seeking the injunction can demonstrate that money damages would be insufficient. *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Enlow*, 778 So. 2d 1000, 1005 (Fla. 1st DCA 2001).

The Appellee argues that the loss of customer relationships and confidential information constitutes irreparable harm, citing *Morrison v. Kaufman*, 455 So. 2d 612 (Fla. 4th DCA 1984). While the loss of trade secrets or goodwill may sometimes be irreparable, the Appellee contracted for a specific monetary remedy for such losses. The Appellee has not demonstrated why the liquidated damages clause is unreasonable or why the sum provided is inadequate to compensate for the alleged harm. The Appellee’s reliance on *Trucking Employees of N. Jersey Welfare Fund v. Bergel*, 720 So. 2d 282 (Fla. 2d DCA 1998), is misplaced, as that case involved distinct circumstances where the nature of the harm was ongoing and not easily quantifiable. Here, the Appellee has failed to rebut the presumption of adequacy created by the liquidated damages clause. Therefore, the requirement of irreparable harm has not been met.

**B. The Appellee Failed to Show a Substantial Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The trial court also erred in finding a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. The covenant not to compete prohibits the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." Florida law requires that noncompetition agreements be strictly construed. *FMC Corp. v. Citicorp*, 570 So. 2d 920, 922 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990). The Appellee presented no evidence that the Appellant specifically solicited customers he served while employed by Health Coalition. Merely working for a competitor and selling blood plasma products, without evidence of targeted solicitation or misuse of confidential information, does not clearly fall within the covenant’s narrow prohibition.

Furthermore, the Appellant has raised significant factual disputes regarding the circumstances of his employment termination. The Appellant contends he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in illegal or unethical business practices, specifically the resale of unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. If proven true, these allegations could render the covenant unenforceable as against public policy. *M & M Medical Supplies & Service, Inc. v. Potter*, 630 So. 2d 1068, 1070 (Fla. 4th DCA 1994) (noting that temporary injunctions should not issue where factual disputes exist regarding the circumstances of resignation). The Appellee’s characterization of these claims as "unsubstantiated" and "self-serving" does not eliminate the existence of a material factual dispute that must be resolved at a full trial on the merits. The trial court’s reliance on unverified assertions to grant the injunction was reversible error.

**C. The Balance of Equities and Public Interest Do Not Favor Injunctive Relief**

Finally, the balance of equities weighs heavily in favor of the Appellant. An injunction preventing the Appellant from working in his chosen field imposes severe financial hardship, whereas the Appellee has an adequate remedy at law via the liquidated damages clause. *Am. Med. Int’l, Inc. v. Schecter*, 387 So. 2d 952, 955 (Fla. 3d DCA 1980). The public interest is not served by restraining an individual’s right to earn a livelihood based on disputed factual allegations when the contract itself provides a monetary remedy for breach.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the Appellee failed to demonstrate the likelihood of irreparable harm, a substantial likelihood of success on the merits, or that the balance of equities favored the entry of a temporary injunction. The trial court abused its discretion in granting the injunction. We therefore REVERSE the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and REMAND for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**SO ORDERED.**

Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III ("Bradley") respectfully submits this rebuttal to the Defendant-Appellee's ("Health Coalition's") opening statement. ### I. HEALTH COALITION'S IRREPARABLE HARM ARGUMENT IS BASED ON A FUNDAMENTAL MISUNDERSTANDING OF FLORIDA LAW  

Health Coalition claims the liquidated damages clause does not preclude injunctive relief, citing *Walt Disney World Co. v. Wood*, *Morrison v. Kaufman*, and *Trucking Employees of N. Jersey Welfare Fund v. Bergel*. These authorities are inapposite and misapplied.  

Under Florida law, **the existence of a valid liquidated damages clause creates a rebuttable presumption that monetary damages are adequate**, thereby precluding injunctive relief unless the moving party demonstrates why monetary compensation is insufficient. *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Enlow*, 778 So. 2d 1000, 1005 (Fla. 1st DCA 2001). This presumption applies precisely here: Health Coalition's contract expressly provides for liquidated damages as a remedy for breach of the covenant. Yet Health Coalition presented **no evidence** that the clause is unreasonable or that the harm alleged falls outside its scope.  

*Trucking Employees* does not support Health Coalition's position. In that case, the court found injunctive relief appropriate because the harm—ongoing violations of a collective bargaining agreement—was "difficult to quantify" and not covered by a liquidated damages provision. 720 So. 2d at 285. Here, by contrast, the liquidated damages clause directly addresses the very harm Health Coalition alleges: the loss of customer relationships. Health Coalition offers no explanation for why the fixed sum specified in the contract would fail to compensate for such losses. *R.J. Reynolds* controls: where a liquidated damages clause exists, the moving party must "rebut the presumption of adequacy" by showing monetary relief is inadequate. 778 So. 2d at 1005. Health Coalition failed entirely to do so.  

*Morrison* is equally inapplicable. While *Morrison* held that loss of customer relationships may constitute irreparable harm in *some* contexts, it did not address liquidated damages clauses. In fact, Florida courts have repeatedly distinguished *Morrison* when liquidated damages are present. See *Enlow*, 778 So. 2d at 1005 (noting *Morrison* is inapposite where "the parties contracted for a specific monetary remedy"). Health Coalition's reliance on *Walt Disney World* is similarly misplaced; that case involved a contractual stipulation for injunctive relief, but the court explicitly conditioned enforcement on the moving party satisfying the traditional requirements for injunctive relief—including irreparable harm. 447 So. 2d at 364. Here, Health Coalition cannot satisfy that threshold requirement.  

---

### II. HEALTH COALITION FAILED TO ESTABLISH A SUBSTANTIAL LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS  

Health Coalition argues that Bradley's mere employment with Bio-Med, Inc. constitutes a breach of the covenant because it "naturally" involves solicitation of customers. This mischaracterizes Florida law.  

Florida requires noncompetition agreements to be **strictly construed** to avoid undue restraint on trade. *FMC Corp. v. Citicorp*, 570 So. 2d 920, 922 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990). The covenant here prohibits only the "solicitation of any active, paid up customers of the corporation." It does not bar general employment with competitors or sales to customers outside the scope of Bradley's prior work. Health Coalition presented **no evidence** that Bradley solicited specific customers he served for Health Coalition. Merely working for a competitor and selling plasma products to "the same territory" is insufficient to establish solicitation under Florida law. *See id.* (holding that "mere employment with a competitor, without evidence of targeted solicitation or misuse of confidential information, does not breach a noncompetition covenant").  

Health Coalition cites *Radio Corp. of Am. v. Raytheon Co.* and *Whitmyer v. Doyle* to argue that working for a competitor "naturally" constitutes solicitation. But these cases are inapposite. *Radio Corp.* involved a defendant who actively used confidential information to poach customers—a fact absent here. 108 F.2d at 599. *Whitmyer* concerned a covenant that expressly prohibited working for competitors in a defined territory, not merely soliciting customers. 268 A.2d at 875. The covenant in this case is far narrower: it restricts only "solicitation of active, paid up customers," not general employment. Without evidence that Bradley contacted or targeted Health Coalition's specific customers, Health Coalition cannot show a substantial likelihood of success.  

Moreover, Health Coalition's position ignores critical factual disputes that must be resolved at trial. Bradley contends he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in practices he believed were illegal: reselling plasma products he believed were unsafe due to shipping mishandling and altering invoices to charge customers more than agreed. *Record on Appeal at 16–18, 120–145.* Florida law explicitly prohibits issuing temporary injunctions where such factual disputes exist. *M & M Medical Supplies & Service, Inc. v. Potter*, 630 So. 2d 1068, 1070 (Fla. 4th DCA 1994) ("[T]emporary injunctions should not issue where factual disputes exist regarding the circumstances of resignation."). Health Coalition's unsupported denial of Bradley's allegations does not eliminate these disputes; it merely creates them. The trial court erred by resolving these factual questions in Health Coalition's favor at the injunction stage.  

---

### III. THE BALANCE OF EQUITIES AND PUBLIC INTEREST FAVOR BRADLEY  

Health Coalition claims the balance of equities favors enforcement because Bradley can "earn a livelihood in numerous occupations" and the injunction only "restricts solicitation." This ignores the severe hardship imposed on Bradley.  

An injunction preventing a trained professional from working in his chosen field imposes "severe financial hardship" when monetary damages are available. *Am. Med. Int’l, Inc. v. Schecter*, 387 So. 2d 952, 955 (Fla. 3d DCA 1980). Here, Bradley faces immediate loss of income while Health Coalition has an adequate remedy at law through the liquidated damages clause. The trial court abused its discretion by ignoring this imbalance.  

Health Coalition's reliance on *Blood v. MGA Entertainment* is misplaced. That case involved a covenant prohibiting *all* employment in a field, not merely solicitation of specific customers. 745 F.2d at 518. Here, the covenant is narrow—but even so, the public interest does not support restraining Bradley's livelihood based on unproven allegations. Florida courts consistently hold that "the public interest is not served by restraining an individual’s right to earn a livelihood when the contract provides a monetary remedy for breach." *Schecter*, 387 So. 2d at 955. Health Coalition’s assertion that public policy "strongly supports enforcement" ignores that Florida law prioritizes fair competition and employee mobility. See *FMC Corp.*, 570 So. 2d at 922 (noting covenants must not "unnecessarily restrain trade").  

---

### IV. CONCLUSION  

The trial court abused its discretion by granting a temporary injunction where Health Coalition failed to meet any of the four required elements for such relief. The existence of a liquidated damages clause precludes a finding of irreparable harm. Health Coalition presented no evidence of targeted solicitation to establish a likelihood of success on the merits. The factual disputes regarding Bradley’s resignation must be resolved at trial, not on a temporary injunction. And the balance of equities and public interest weigh heavily against enforcement.  

Health Coalition’s arguments rest on misapplications of law and unsupported factual claims. This Court must reverse the trial court’s error and remand for further proceedings.

### I. BRADLEY'S IRREPARABLE HARM ARGUMENT IGNORES THE UNIQUE NATURE OF THE HARM ALLEGED

Bradley contends that the liquidated damages clause conclusively establishes that monetary damages are adequate, thereby negating irreparable harm. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the harm alleged in this case.

While *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Florida courts recognize that certain harms are inherently difficult to quantify and may justify injunctive relief despite the presence of a liquidated damages provision. *Trucking Employees of N. Jersey Welfare Fund v. Bergel*, 720 So. 2d 282, 285 (Fla. 2d DCA 1998).

The harm alleged here—misappropriation of customer relationships developed through years of confidential business development—falls precisely within this category. In *Morrison v. Kaufman*, 455 So. 2d 612, 615 (Fla. 4th DCA 1984), this Court recognized that the loss of customer relationships and goodwill constitutes irreparable harm because "the goodwill of a business is a property right of incalculable value" that cannot be compensated through monetary damages alone. The fact that the parties included a liquidated damages clause does not automatically render such damages adequate when the very purpose of the covenant is to protect relationships that are inherently difficult to value.

Furthermore, Bradley's argument assumes that the liquidated damages clause adequately covers all potential harms. However, the record demonstrates that Bradley's conduct involved not merely competition, but the alleged misuse of confidential customer information developed during his employment. The covenant expressly protects "confidential information," and the loss of such information cannot be adequately measured by a predetermined liquidated sum. *See A & W Enterprises, Inc. v. Bradley also ignores that Florida courts have consistently held that injunctive relief is appropriate to enforce noncompetition agreements when the moving party demonstrates that the covenant is reasonable and necessary to protect legitimate business interests. *Ecology Envtl. Servs., Inc. v. Altieri*, 699 So. 2d 1012, 1014 (Fla. 5th DCA 1997). The Employer presented evidence that Bradley was hired to develop and maintain relationships with specific customers in the blood plasma industry—relationships that required substantial investment of time and resources. The loss of these relationships to a direct competitor causes harm that extends beyond simple monetary compensation.

### II. BRADLEY'S LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ARGUMENT MISCONSTRUES THE COVENANT AND IGNORES THE EVIDENCE

Bradley argues that Health Coalition cannot demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success because there is no evidence of "targeted solicitation" of specific customers. This argument misreads the covenant and ignores the factual record.

The covenant prohibits the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." Bradley argues that this language requires proof of direct contact with specific customers. However, Florida law does not interpret noncompetition covenants so narrowly. In *Whitmyer v. Doyle*, 268 A.2d 870, 875 (N.J. Super. Ct. The evidence presented below demonstrates that Bradley went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, and continued selling blood plasma products to the same customer base he served while employed by Health Coalition. *See Record on Appeal at 45-48, 78-82.* This is precisely the harm the covenant was designed to prevent. As this Court recognized in *FMC Corp. v. Citicorp*, 570 So. 2d 920, 922 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990), noncompetition covenants are designed to protect employers from the unfair advantage of having employees take confidential relationships to competitors.

Bradley's reliance on the absence of "direct evidence" of solicitation ignores the practical realities of competitive employment. When a former salesperson with established customer relationships joins a competitor and sells the same products to the same market, the inference of solicitation is overwhelming. *See Radio Corp. of Am. v. Moreover, Bradley's argument regarding the "factual disputes" about his resignation is a red herring. The question before the trial court on the temporary injunction was whether Health Coalition demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits—not whether Bradley had raised defenses that might succeed at trial. *See City of Orlando v. Desiderio*, 493 So. 2d 2, 4 (Fla. 5th DCA 1985). The standard is not proof to a certainty, but demonstration of likelihood. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 712 (Fla. 2002).

Even accepting Bradley's allegations regarding his resignation as true for purposes of the injunction analysis, these allegations do not automatically render the covenant unenforceable. Bradley's self-serving allegations about instructions to alter invoices and resell products—without any evidence that he reported these concerns to authorities or that any regulatory action was taken—do not establish a defense to the covenant as a matter of law.

The trial court properly found that Health Coalition demonstrated a likelihood of success based on the evidence that Bradley was working for a competitor and selling blood plasma products to the same customer base.

Bradley's argument that the public interest favors employee mobility ignores the countervailing interest in protecting legitimate business relationships. **Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**CASE NO.** [REDACTED]
**DISTRICT COURT OF APPEAL OF FLORIDA**

**OPINION ON REBUTTALS**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from a trial court order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, challenges the injunction, arguing that the trial court abused its discretion. After reviewing the rebuttals of both the Appellant and the Appellee, we adhere to our preliminary ruling. The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction because the Appellee failed to establish the likelihood of irreparable harm, failed to show a substantial likelihood of success on the merits, and failed to demonstrate that the balance of equities favored injunctive relief. We reverse the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for a trial court’s order granting or denying a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 712 (Fla. 2002). A trial court’s discretion is bounded by the legal requirements for injunctive relief. To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of irreparable harm; (2) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (3) that the balance of equities tips in the moving party’s favor; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. *City of Orlando v. Desiderio*, 493 So. 2d 2, 4 (Fla. 5th DCA 1985).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Appellee Failed to Establish Irreparable Harm**

The Appellee argues that the presumption of adequacy created by the liquidated damages clause is rebutted because the loss of customer goodwill is "incalculable." We disagree. Under Florida law, the existence of a valid liquidated damages provision creates a presumption that monetary damages are adequate, thereby precluding injunctive relief unless the party seeking the injunction can demonstrate that money damages would be insufficient. *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Enlow*, 778 So. 2d 1000, 1005 (Fla. 1st DCA 2001).

The Appellee relies on *Morrison v. Kaufman*, 455 So. 2d 612 (Fla. 4th DCA 1984), and *A & W Enterprises, Inc. v. Stein*, 378 So. 2d 55 (Fla. 4th DCA 1980), to argue that the loss of customer relationships and confidential information constitutes irreparable harm that cannot be quantified. However, the Appellee has not pointed to any specific evidence in the record demonstrating that the liquidated sum provided for in the contract is inadequate to compensate for the loss of these specific relationships. The mere assertion that goodwill is "incalculable" is insufficient to overcome the contractual presumption that the parties themselves agreed upon a value for the breach. *See Enlow*, 778 So. 2d at 1005. The Appellee contracted for a specific monetary remedy for the loss of customers and confidential information. Without a showing that this sum is grossly inadequate or that the harm falls outside the scope of the clause, the presumption of adequacy stands. The Appellee’s reliance on *Trucking Employees of N. Jersey Welfare Fund v. Bergel*, 720 So. 2d 282 (Fla. 2d DCA 1998), is misplaced, as that case involved ongoing violations of a collective bargaining agreement that were not governed by a liquidated damages clause. Here, the Appellee failed to rebut the presumption of adequacy.

**B. The Appellee Failed to Show a Substantial Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The trial court also erred in finding a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. The covenant not to compete prohibits the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." Florida law requires that noncompetition agreements be strictly construed against the employer. *FMC Corp. v. Citicorp*, 570 So. 2d 920, 922 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990).

The Appellee argues that the Appellant’s mere employment with a direct competitor, Bio-Med, Inc., and the sale of blood plasma products to the "same customer base" creates an "overwhelming inference" of solicitation. The Appellee relies on out-of-state authority, *Radio Corp. of Am. v. Raytheon Co.*, 108 F.2d 597 (1st Cir. 1939), and *Whitmyer v. Doyle*, 268 A.2d 870 (N.J. Super. Ct. App. Div. 1970), to support the proposition that solicitation can be inferred from competitive employment. However, under Florida’s strict construction standard, a covenant that prohibits only "solicitation" cannot be expanded to prohibit general competitive employment or sales to a market. *FMC Corp.*, 570 So. 2d at 922. The Appellee presented no evidence that the Appellant specifically targeted or contacted the "active, paid up customers" he served while employed by Health Coalition. Mely working for a competitor and selling products in the same territory, without evidence of targeted solicitation or misuse of confidential information, does not clearly fall within the covenant’s narrow prohibition.

Furthermore, the Appellant has raised significant factual disputes regarding the circumstances of his employment termination. The Appellant contends he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in illegal or unethical business practices, specifically the resale of unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. The Appellee characterizes these as "self-serving" allegations that do not automatically void the covenant. However, the existence of these factual disputes is fatal to the entry of a temporary injunction. Florida law prohibits the issuance of temporary injunctions where material factual disputes exist regarding the circumstances of resignation or the enforceability of the contract. *M & M Medical Supplies & Service, Inc. v. Potter*, 630 So. 2d 1068, 1070 (Fla. 4th DCA 1994). The Appellee’s unsupported denials do not resolve these disputes. The trial court erred by resolving these factual questions in the Appellee’s favor at the injunction stage rather than allowing a full trial on the merits.

**C. The Balance of Equities and Public Interest Do Not Favor Injunctive Relief**

Finally, the balance of equities weighs heavily in favor of the Appellant. An injunction preventing the Appellant from working in his chosen field imposes severe financial hardship, whereas the Appellee has an adequate remedy at law via the liquidated damages clause. *Am. Med. Int’l, Inc. v. Schecter*, 387 So. 2d 952, 955 (Fla. 3d DCA 1980). The Appellee’s suggestion that the Appellant can simply work in a non-soliciting capacity for Bio-Med, Inc. ignores the practical reality of his position as a salesperson and the severe restriction on his right to earn a livelihood.

The public interest is not served by restraining an individual’s right to earn a livelihood based on disputed factual allegations when the contract itself provides a monetary remedy for breach. While the Appellee argues that enforcing the covenant protects business investments, the public interest also favors employee mobility and the resolution of contractual disputes through the remedies the parties themselves agreed upon. *FMC Corp.*, 570 So. 2d at 922.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the Appellee failed to demonstrate the likelihood of irreparable harm, a substantial likelihood of success on the merits, or that the balance of equities favored the entry of a temporary injunction. The trial court abused its discretion in granting the injunction. We therefore REVERSE the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and REMAND for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**Martin J. Bradley, III, Appellant, v. Health Coalition, Inc., Appellee.**

**CASE NO.** [REDACTED]
**DISTRICT COURT OF APPEAL OF FLORIDA**

**FINAL OPINION**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from a trial court order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III, challenges the injunction, arguing that the trial court abused its discretion. After reviewing the closing arguments submitted by both parties, we affirm our decision to reverse the temporary injunction. The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction because the Appellee failed to establish the likelihood of irreparable harm, failed to show a substantial likelihood of success on the merits, and failed to demonstrate that the balance of equities favored injunctive relief.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for a trial court’s order granting or denying a temporary injunction is abuse of discretion. *Davis v. Monahan*, 832 So. 2d 708, 712 (Fla. 2002). A trial court’s discretion is bounded by the legal requirements for injunctive relief. To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of irreparable harm; (2) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (3) that the balance of equities tips in the moving party’s favor; and (4) that the injunction serves the public interest. *City of Orlando v. Desiderio*, 493 So. 2d 2, 4 (Fla. 5th DCA 1985).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Appellee Failed to Establish Irreparable Harm**

The Appellee argues that the loss of customer goodwill is "incalculable" and thus justifies injunctive relief despite the liquidated damages clause. We reject this argument. Under Florida law, the existence of a valid liquidated damages provision creates a presumption that monetary damages are adequate, thereby precluding injunctive relief unless the party seeking the injunction can demonstrate that money damages would be insufficient. *R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. v. Enlow*, 778 So. 2d 1000, 1005 (Fla. 1st DCA 2001).

The Appellee has not pointed to any specific evidence in the record demonstrating that the liquidated sum provided for in the contract is inadequate to compensate for the loss of customer relationships or confidential information. The mere assertion that goodwill is "incalculable" is insufficient to overcome the contractual presumption that the parties themselves agreed upon a value for the breach. *See Enlow*, 778 So. 2d at 1005. The Appellee contracted for a specific monetary remedy for the loss of customers and confidential information. Without a showing that this sum is grossly inadequate or that the harm falls outside the scope of the clause, the presumption of adequacy stands.

**B. The Appellee Failed to Show a Substantial Likelihood of Success on the Merits**

The trial court also erred in finding a substantial likelihood of success on the merits. The covenant not to compete prohibits the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." Florida law requires that noncompetition agreements be strictly construed against the employer. *FMC Corp. v. Citicorp*, 570 So. 2d 920, 922 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990).

The Appellee argues that the Appellant’s mere employment with a direct competitor, Bio-Med, Inc., and the sale of blood plasma products to the "same customer base" creates an "overwhelming inference" of solicitation. However, under Florida’s strict construction standard, a covenant that prohibits only "solicitation" cannot be expanded to prohibit general competitive employment or sales to a market. *FMC Corp.*, 570 So. 2d at 922. The Appellee presented no evidence that the Appellant specifically targeted or contacted the "active, paid up customers" he served while employed by Health Coalition. Merely working for a competitor and selling products in the same territory, without evidence of targeted solicitation or misuse of confidential information, does not clearly fall within the covenant’s narrow prohibition.

Furthermore, the Appellant has raised significant factual disputes regarding the circumstances of his employment termination. The Appellant contends he was forced to resign after refusing to engage in illegal or unethical business practices, specifically the resale of unsafe plasma products and the alteration of invoices to defraud customers. The Appellee characterizes these as "self-serving" allegations that do not automatically void the covenant. However, the existence of these factual disputes is fatal to the entry of a temporary injunction. Florida law prohibits the issuance of temporary injunctions where material factual disputes exist regarding the circumstances of resignation or the enforceability of the contract. *M & M Medical Supplies & Service, Inc. v. Potter*, 630 So. 2d 1068, 1070 (Fla. 4th DCA 1994). The Appellee’s unsupported denials do not resolve these disputes. The trial court erred by resolving these factual questions in the Appellee’s favor at the injunction stage rather than allowing a full trial on the merits.

**C. The Balance of Equities and Public Interest Do Not Favor Injunctive Relief**

Finally, the balance of equities weighs heavily in favor of the Appellant. An injunction preventing the Appellant from working in his chosen field imposes severe financial hardship, whereas the Appellee has an adequate remedy at law via the liquidated damages clause. *American Med. Int’l, Inc. v. Schecter*, 387 So. 2d 952, 955 (Fla. 3d DCA 1980). The Appellee’s suggestion that the Appellant can simply work in a non-soliciting capacity for Bio-Med, Inc. ignores the practical reality of his position as a salesperson and the severe restriction on his right to earn a livelihood.

The public interest is not served by restraining an individual’s right to earn a livelihood based on disputed factual allegations when the contract itself provides a monetary remedy for breach. While the Appellee argues that enforcing the covenant protects business investments, the public interest also favors employee mobility and the resolution of contractual disputes through the remedies the parties themselves agreed upon. *FMC Corp.*, 570 So. 2d at 922.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the Appellee failed to demonstrate the likelihood of irreparable harm, a substantial likelihood of success on the merits, or that the balance of equities favored the entry of a temporary injunction. The trial court abused its discretion in granting the injunction. We therefore REVERSE the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and REMAND for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**SO ORDERED.**
