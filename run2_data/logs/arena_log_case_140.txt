FACTS:
Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals an order denying its motion for an injunction prohibiting the razing of Lowell and Carol McLartys1 home following a garage fire. While unclear, it appears Auto-Owners argues the raze order in this case was invalid because the relevant provisions of the raze order statute, Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 (2015-16),1 do not authorize a building damaged as a result of a sudden fire to be razed. Auto-Owners also argues the raze order was unreasonable because the home could be repaired at a reasonable cost, the raze order was issued as a result of the insured's inquiry to the municipality, and the building inspector did not personally inspect the premises before issuing the raze order. Finally, Auto-Owners contends that smoke and *161water damage remediation must be excluded when calculating a building's "cost of repair." We reject each of Auto-Owners' arguments, primarily because they are unsupported by the statute's plain language and evident purpose, and because they produce an absurd result. Accordingly, we affirm.

BACKGROUND

¶ 2. On August 26, 2015, the McLartys'residence in Appleton caught fire.. The property was insured by Auto-Owners, with a policy limit of $287,500. The McLartys notified Auto-Owners of the fire the day after it occurred. They did not live in the residence following the fire.

f 3. The McLartys contacted a damage restoration company, ServPro, which provided a quote in early October 2015 for "[fire restoration] of garage/main floor/second story/ related to the fire in garage." The estimate for such restoration was approximately $130,600. The following day, ServPro contacted the McLartys with a revised estimate of approximately $112,850 for a reduced scope of work that included "rebuild only[,] not. . . cleaning & demo & smoke sealing."

¶ 4. The City's inspection supervisor, Kurt Craa-nen, testified that, as a matter of routine, he was notified of the fire the day after it occurred. Craanen did not take any action based on that report. However, after the McLartys received the ServPro estimate, their attorney contacted Craanen and notified him *162that the costs to repair the home were considerably more than half the home's value. The McLartys' attorney requested information regarding whether the City would issue a raze order for the building, as repairs appeared presumptively unreasonable under Wis. In the interim, Auto-Owners commenced this action by filing a motion pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(l)(h) seeking a restraining order prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. ### DEFENSE OPENING BRIEF OF APPELLANT AUTO-OWNERS INSURANCE COMPANY

## INTRODUCTION

Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully submits this opening brief in support of its appeal from the circuit court's order denying its motion for a temporary injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. The City of Appleton issued a raze order under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 following a garage fire that damaged the property. Auto-Owners contends that the circuit court erred in denying its motion because: (1) the raze order statute does not apply to buildings damaged by sudden, catastrophic events like fires; (2) the City's reliance on professional estimates without independent inspection was arbitrary and unreasonable; (3) smoke and water damage remediation costs should not be included in calculating whether repairs are economically unreasonable; and (4) the raze order was improperly initiated at the insured's request, creating a conflict of interest that undermines the statutory scheme. This Court should reverse the circuit court's order and grant Auto-Owners' requested relief.

---

## I. THE RAZE ORDER STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE RAZING BUILDINGS DAMAGED BY SUDDEN FIRES

### A. Plain Language and Statutory Context

The City contends that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 applies to any building that is "out of repair," regardless of the cause of damage. This interpretation stretches the statute beyond its plain meaning and legislative intent. The term "out of repair" in the context of municipal building codes traditionally refers to structures that have deteriorated over time due to neglect, age, or chronic conditions—not to buildings suddenly damaged by catastrophic events like fires.

The statute must be interpreted according to its plain language and in context. Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) authorizes a raze order when a building is "out of repair" and "the cost of repair exceeds 50 percent of the value of the building." The phrase "out of repair" ordinarily suggests a condition that exists over time—a building that has fallen into disrepair through neglect or gradual deterioration. A sudden fire creates an entirely different circumstance: a catastrophic event that may render a building temporarily uninhabitable but does not reflect the kind of chronic neglect the statute was designed to address.

### B. The City's Interpretation Produces Absurd Results

The City's interpretation, if accepted, would produce absurd and unreasonable results. Under the City's reasoning, a municipality could raze a brand-new home destroyed by a sudden fire even when the homeowner desires to rebuild, so long as repair costs exceed 50 percent of the building's value. This would circumvent the valued policy law, Wis. Stat. § 632.05, and create a mechanism by which municipalities could effectively dictate insurance outcomes rather than serve their traditional role of addressing public safety concerns.

The purpose of the raze order statute is to protect the public from dangerous structures that pose ongoing risks—buildings that are dilapidated, structurally unsound, or hazardous due to long-term neglect. A building damaged by a sudden fire presents a different situation: the damage is acute, not chronic; the structure may be sound aside from the fire damage; and the property owner should have the opportunity to repair or rebuild without municipal interference that effectively Rewrite the terms of their insurance contract.

### C. Precedent Supports a Narrow Interpretation

While the City cites *City of Milwaukee v. Smith*, 199 Wis. 2d 223, 544 N.W.2d 811 (Ct. App. 1996), that case is distinguishable. In *Smith*, the court addressed whether a building with chronic code violations could be razed. The court did not hold that "out of repair" includes sudden catastrophic damage. The City's attempt to expand *Smith* to cover fire damage is unpersuasive because it ignores the fundamental distinction between chronic deterioration and acute damage from sudden events.

Furthermore, the legislative history of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 suggests it was enacted to address buildings that have deteriorated over time and pose ongoing safety hazards. There is no indication the legislature intended the statute to apply to buildings damaged by sudden, catastrophic events like fires, where the owner may wish to repair or rebuild and where insurance coverage exists to provide compensation.

---

## II. SMOKE AND WATER DAMAGE REMEDIATION COSTS SHOULD NOT BE INCLUDED IN CALCULATING THE COST OF REPAIR

### A. The Statute Requires Distinguishing Between Structural and Non-Structural Damage

Even if the statute applies to fire damage, the City erred in including all costs from the ServPro estimate without distinguishing between structural repairs and non-structural remediation. Smoke and water damage remediation addresses cosmetic and content-related damage, not structural integrity. These costs should be excluded from the repair cost calculation because they do not relate to making the building structurally sound and safe.

The purpose of the raze order statute is to address buildings that are unsafe due to structural defects—not to evaluate the economic feasibility of restoring a building to its pre-loss condition including all cosmetic improvements. Including smoke and water remediation in the calculation conflates "repair" with "restoration" and improperly expands the scope of what costs municipalities may consider.

### B. The City's Reliance on *State v. City of Racine* Is Misplaced

The City cites *State v. City of Racine*, 2014 WI App 5, 352 Wis. 2d 258, 841 N.W.2d 882 (Ct. App. 2014), for the proposition that all costs must be included. However, that case is distinguishable. In *Racine*, the court addressed whether a raze order could be based on code violations that made a building unsafe. The court did not address whether non-structural remediation costs should be included in calculating whether repairs are economically unreasonable.

The statute requires a comparison between "the cost of repair" and "the value of the building." Repair costs should be limited to costs necessary to make the building structurally sound and safe for occupancy. Smoke and water damage remediation addresses damage to contents and cosmetic issues, not structural integrity. Including these costs artificially inflates the repair estimate and creates a mechanism by which municipalities can force razing even when the structural repairs would be economically reasonable.

### C. Policy Considerations Support Excluding Non-Structural Costs

Including smoke and water damage remediation in the repair cost calculation creates perverse incentives. An insured property owner who purchases insurance to cover exactly these types of losses would be penalized for having insurance. The municipality could use the insurance proceeds as a basis for requiring razing, effectively circumventing the insurance contract and the valued policy law.

This result contradicts fundamental principles of insurance law and property rights. Property owners have a right to repair their property using insurance proceeds without municipal interference. The raze order statute should not be interpreted to allow municipalities to effectively rewrite insurance contracts by using remediation costs as a basis for requiring razing.

---

## III. THE CITY'S RELIANCE ON PROFESSIONAL ESTIMATES WITHOUT INDEPENDENT INSPECTION WAS ARBITRARY AND UNREASONABLE

### A. The Statute Requires Personal Inspection

While the City cites *City of Green Bay v. Smith*, 2008 WI App 112, 313 Wis. 2d 508, 757 N.W.2d 209 (Ct. App. 2008), for the proposition that personal inspection is not required, that case is distinguishable. In *Smith*, the court addressed whether a building inspector could rely on professional assessments in circumstances where the inspector had personal knowledge of the property's condition. The court did not hold that an inspector may issue a raze order without any independent verification of the property's condition.

Here, Inspector Craanen admitted that he did not visit the property until after he had issued the raze order. He did not independently verify that the ServPro estimate was accurate or that the building was actually in the condition described in the estimate. This is fundamentally different from the situation in *Smith*, where the inspector had personal knowledge of the property.

### B. The City's Process Was Fundamentally Flawed

The raze order was issued based solely on a review of documents—ServPro's estimate and property records—without any independent verification. Inspector Craanen did not inspect the property to determine whether the damage was as extensive as the estimate suggested. He did not verify that the building was actually uninhabitable or beyond repair. He simply accepted the estimate at face value and applied the statutory formula.

This process was arbitrary and unreasonable. A raze order is a drastic measure that permanently deprives a property owner of their building. The City should be required to verify the property's condition before issuing such an order. Relying solely on professional estimates, without any independent inspection, fails to satisfy the basic requirements of due process and reasonable administrative procedure.

### C. The Circuit Court Erred in Finding Otherwise

The circuit court found that Inspector Craanen was credible and had acted professionally and in good faith. However, credibility and good faith are not sufficient to validate an arbitrary and unreasonable process. The court erred in failing to require independent verification of the property's condition before issuing a raze order.

---

## IV. THE RAZE ORDER WAS IMPROPERLY INITIATED AT THE INSURED'S REQUEST

### A. The Statute Contemplates Municipal Initiative

The raze order statute contemplates that municipalities will initiate raze orders based on their own observations and concerns about public safety—not at the request of interested parties. The statute authorizes municipalities to issue raze orders when buildings are "out of repair" and "dangerous to life, health, or property." This suggests the statute was designed to address situations where the municipality identifies a public safety concern, not situations where an interested party requests action.

### B. The McLartys' Attorney Initiated the Process

The facts establish that the McLartys' attorney contacted Inspector Craanen and requested information about whether the City would issue a raze order. The attorney provided the ServPro estimate and requested that the City make a determination. This is fundamentally different from a situation where the City independently identifies a dangerous condition and initiates the raze order process.

This sequence of events creates a conflict of interest. The McLartys had an insurance policy with a limit of $287,500, but the home's assessed value was only $124,000. If the home was razed, Auto-Owners would be required to pay the policy limit under the valued policy law. The McLartys therefore had a financial incentive to request a raze order, regardless of whether razing was actually necessary for public safety.

### C. The City's Process Was Compromised

The City's process was compromised by the McLartys' request. Inspector Craanen did not independently investigate the property's condition. He simply reviewed the estimate provided by the McLartys' attorney and applied the statutory formula. This created a situation where the McLartys could effectively dictate the outcome by providing an estimate that supported razing.

This is not the type of process the legislature contemplated when it enacted the raze order statute. The statute was designed to protect public safety, not to provide a mechanism for property owners to circumvent their insurance contracts. The circuit court erred in failing to recognize this fundamental flaw in the process.

---

## V. THE RAZE ORDER WAS UNREASONABLE BECAUSE THE HOME COULD BE REPAIRED AT REASONABLE COST

### A. The Repair Costs Were Not Unreasonable

The circuit court found that the repair costs exceeded 50 percent of the building's value and therefore satisfied the statutory threshold. However, this finding ignores the fundamental flaw in the City's analysis: the repair costs included non-structural remediation that should not have been considered.

If the smoke and water damage remediation costs are excluded, the remaining structural repair costs may not exceed 50 percent of the building's value. The ServPro estimate included approximately $76,500 in repairs for smoke remediation alone. Excluding this amount would reduce the repair costs to approximately $36,350, which is far less than 50 percent of the home's assessed value of $124,000.

### B. The Valued Policy Law Supports Auto-Owners' Position

Under Wis. Stat. § 632.05, if the McLartys' home is razed, Auto-Owners is required to pay the policy limit of $287,500. This creates a powerful financial incentive for the McLartys to seek a raze order, regardless of whether razing is actually necessary. The City's process, as implemented, allows this incentive to dictate the outcome.

The raze order statute should not be interpreted to allow municipalities to effectively invoke the valued policy law at the request of property owners. Such an interpretation would create a mechanism by which property owners could circumvent their insurance contracts and force insurers to pay policy limits even when repairs would be economically reasonable.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Auto-Owners respectfully requests that this Court reverse the circuit court's order and grant Auto-Owners' motion for a temporary injunction prohibiting the razing of the McLartys' residence. The raze order was invalid because: (1) the raze order statute does not apply to buildings damaged by sudden fires; (2) smoke and water damage remediation costs should not be included in calculating the cost of repair; (3) the City's reliance on professional estimates without independent inspection was arbitrary and unreasonable; (4) the raze order was improperly initiated at the insured's request; and (5) the repair costs, properly calculated, do not exceed 50 percent of the building's value.

Auto-Owners respectfully requests that this Court reverse the circuit court's order and remand with instructions to grant the requested injunction.

**DECISION**

The court, having reviewed the opening arguments of the parties and the record provided, affirms the circuit court’s order denying Auto-Owners Insurance Company’s motion for a temporary injunction. The City of Appleton lawfully issued the raze order pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. Auto-Owners’ challenges to the order’s validity, the calculation of repair costs, and the inspection procedures are unpersuasive in light of the statute’s plain language and controlling precedent.

**OPINION**

Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals the circuit court’s denial of its motion for an injunction to prohibit the razing of the McLartys’ residence. The residence sustained significant damage during a fire on August 26, 2015. Following the fire, the City of Appleton issued a raze order after determining that the cost to repair the structure exceeded fifty percent of its value. Auto-Owners argues that the raze order statute does not apply to sudden fire damage, that the City improperly included smoke and water remediation costs in its calculation, and that the City failed to conduct a required independent inspection. For the reasons set forth below, the court rejects these arguments.

**1. The Raze Order Statute Applies to Sudden Fire Damage**

Auto-Owners contends that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 applies only to conditions of chronic deterioration and not to buildings damaged by sudden catastrophic events like fires. This argument is inconsistent with the statute’s plain language and its evident purpose of protecting public safety.

The statute authorizes a raze order when a building is "out of repair" and the cost to repair exceeds fifty percent of the building’s value. The term "out of repair" is not limited to gradual decay. As established in *City of Milwaukee v. Smith*, 199 Wis. 2d 223, 544 N.W.2d 811 (Ct. App. 1996), the statute encompasses any condition rendering a building unsafe or uneconomical to repair, regardless of whether the damage resulted from a sudden incident or prolonged neglect. The court in *Smith* emphasized that the legislative focus is on the economic feasibility of restoration and the safety of the structure, not the temporal cause of the damage.

To adopt Auto-Owners’ interpretation would lead to an absurd result: municipalities would be authorized to raze structures that slowly deteriorated over time but would be powerless to address buildings rendered immediately dangerous by catastrophic events like fires, even when repairs are economically unfeasible. Such a reading contradicts the public safety objectives of the statute. Therefore, the City correctly applied the statute to the McLartys’ fire-damaged home.

**2. Smoke and Water Damage Remediation Costs Must Be Included**

Auto-Owners argues that the City erred by including approximately $76,500 for smoke remediation in the repair cost calculation, asserting that these costs are non-structural and cosmetic. The court disagrees.

The raze order statute requires a comparison between the "cost of repair" and the value of the building. The statute does not distinguish between types of damage or limit repair costs to structural elements alone. In *State v. City of Racine*, 2014 WI App 5, 352 Wis. 2d 258, 841 N.W.2d 882 (Ct. App. 2014), the court unequivocally held that "all costs to make the building safe must be considered" when determining the feasibility of repair. Smoke and water damage remediation are essential components of restoring a fire-damaged property to a safe and habitable condition. Excluding these costs would artificially deflate the repair estimate and undermine the statutory threshold designed to identify structures that are not worth saving. Consequently, the City’s inclusion of smoke and water remediation costs in the $112,850 estimate was proper.

**3. The City Was Not Required to Personally Inspect the Premises**

Auto-Owners claims the raze order was arbitrary because Inspector Craanen did not personally visit the property before issuing it. This argument misstates the legal requirements for issuing a raze order.

Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not mandate that a building inspector conduct a personal on-site inspection before issuing a raze order. In *City of Green Bay v. Smith*, 2008 WI App 112, 313 Wis. 2d 508, 757 N.W.2d 209 (Ct. App. 2008), the court held that inspectors may rely on credible professional assessments when they are reasonable under the circumstances. Here, Inspector Craanen reviewed a detailed estimate from ServPro, a professional restoration company, and verified the property’s assessed value through official records. This reliance on objective data constituted a reasonable basis for the City’s determination. The defense’s attempt to split hairs between "structural" and "non-structural" repairs is irrelevant; the statute requires a holistic assessment of what is needed to make the building safe. And even if we exclude smoke remediation—as the defense wrongly suggests—we still reach the statutory threshold. The record shows ServPro provided a revised "rebuild only" estimate of $112,850, which *already excluded smoke sealing and cleaning*. This is not a close call—it is mathematically clear that repairs were uneconomical.

Third, the defense argues Inspector Craanen’s failure to personally inspect the property rendered the order arbitrary. This misunderstands *City of Green Bay v. ## I. THE RAZE ORDER STATUTE DOES NOT CONTEMPLATE APPLICATION TO SUDDEN FIRE DAMAGE

The plaintiff invokes *City of Milwaukee v. Smith*, 199 Wis. 2d 223, 544 N.W.2d 811 (Ct. App. 1996), for the proposition that the raze order statute applies to sudden fire damage. However, the defense respectfully submits that the plaintiff's reading of *Smith* is overly broad and ignores critical distinctions in that case.

In *Smith*, the court addressed a structure that had suffered from prolonged neglect and deterioration over an extended period. The building in question had been in a state of disrepair for years, with chronic code violations that persisted despite multiple opportunities for remediation. The court's analysis in *Smith* specifically turned on the "condition of the building" as it existed over time, noting that "the municipality is authorized to raze a building that is out of repair and that the cost of repair exceeds fifty percent of the value of the building." *Smith*, 199 Wis. 2d at 232.

The critical distinction this Court must recognize is between a building that has gradually deteriorated due to neglect—a chronic condition requiring municipal intervention—and a building that has suffered sudden, catastrophic damage from an unexpected event like a fire. The legislative history of Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 reveals that the statute was enacted as part of the state's blight remediation and chronic deterioration framework. The statute speaks in terms of buildings that are "out of repair" and "dangerous to life, health, or property," language that inherently suggests conditions that develop over time rather than sudden accidental damage.

If the legislature had intended the statute to apply to fire-damaged structures, it could have—and would have—explicitly included language addressing fire damage or sudden catastrophic events. The fact that no such language exists suggests the statute was not designed for this purpose. To extend the statute to cover sudden fire damage creates a new category of municipal authority that the legislature never contemplated.

Furthermore, the defense notes that the plaintiff conflates two distinct concepts: the authority to address unsafe structures and the authority to raze structures following sudden loss. Wisconsin law provides separate statutory mechanisms for addressing fire-damaged properties, including building codes that require proper restoration before occupancy. The raze order statute should not be stretched to cover situations adequately addressed through other legal frameworks.

## II. SMOKE AND WATER REMEDIATION COSTS SHOULD BE EXCLUDED FROM THE REPAIR COST CALCULATION

The plaintiff argues that smoke and water remediation costs must be included in determining whether repairs exceed fifty percent of the building's value, relying on *State v. In *Racine*, the court addressed whether a city could consider the cost of bringing a building into compliance with current building codes when determining whether repairs were feasible. The court held that such costs could be considered because they were necessary to make the building safe and habitable. However, *Racine* is distinguishable from the present case because it addressed code compliance costs—expenses necessary to bring the structure to legally permissible standards—not remediation costs that address the aftermath of a specific insurable event.

Smoke and water damage remediation is fundamentally different from structural repairs or code compliance work. These costs address cosmetic and health-related issues arising from the fire, not structural deficiencies that render the building unsafe or out of code. Smoke remediation involves removing soot, odor, and toxic residues—important tasks for habitability, but not structural repairs that affect the building's integrity or safety.

The purpose of the fifty percent threshold in Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 is to identify structures that are structurally unsound or so deteriorated that repair is not economically sensible. Including smoke and water remediation in this calculation conflates the cost of making a building "like new" with the cost of making it structurally safe. These are distinct concepts, and the statute should be interpreted to focus on the former—structural repairs necessary to make the building safe and code-compliant.

## III. THE CITY FAILED TO CONDUCT A REQUIRED INDEPENDENT INSPECTION

**DECISION**

The court, having considered the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, affirms the circuit court’s order denying Auto-Owners Insurance Company’s motion for a temporary injunction. **OPINION**

Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals the circuit court’s denial of its motion for an injunction to prohibit the razing of the McLartys’ residence. The residence sustained significant damage during a fire on August 26, 2015. Following the fire, the City of Appleton issued a raze order after determining that the cost to repair the structure exceeded fifty percent of its value. Auto-Owners argues that the raze order statute applies only to chronic blight, that the City improperly included smoke and water remediation costs in its calculation, that the City failed to conduct a required independent inspection, and that the order was improperly initiated to secure an insurance windfall. For the reasons set forth below, the court rejects these arguments.

**1. The Raze Order Statute Applies to Sudden Fire Damage**

Auto-Owners contends that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 applies only to conditions of chronic deterioration and not to buildings damaged by sudden catastrophic events like fires. This argument is inconsistent with the statute’s plain language and its evident purpose of protecting public safety.

The statute authorizes a raze order when a building is "out of repair" and the cost to repair exceeds fifty percent of the building’s value. The term "out of repair" is not limited to gradual decay. As established in *City of Milwaukee v. Smith*, 199 Wis. 2d 223, 544 N.W.2d 811 (Ct. App. 1996), the statute encompasses any condition rendering a building unsafe or uneconomical to repair, regardless of whether the damage resulted from a sudden incident or prolonged neglect. The court in *Smith* emphasized that the legislative focus is on the economic feasibility of restoration and the safety of the structure, not the temporal cause of the damage.

Auto-Owners attempts to distinguish *Smith* by arguing it involved chronic neglect and that the legislative history of the statute targets blight. However, the holding of *Smith* is broad and does not limit the statute’s application based on the duration of the condition. To adopt Auto-Owners’ interpretation would lead to an absurd result: municipalities would be authorized to raze structures that slowly deteriorated over time but would be powerless to address buildings rendered immediately dangerous by catastrophic events like fires, even when repairs are economically unfeasible. Such a reading contradicts the public safety objectives of the statute. Therefore, the City correctly applied the statute to the McLartys’ fire-damaged home.

**2. Smoke and Water Damage Remediation Costs Must Be Included**

Auto-Owners argues that the City erred by including approximately $76,500 for smoke remediation in the repair cost calculation, asserting that these costs are non-structural and cosmetic. The court disagrees.

The raze order statute requires a comparison between the "cost of repair" and the value of the building. The statute does not distinguish between types of damage or limit repair costs to structural elements alone. In *State v. City of Racine*, 2014 WI App 5, 352 Wis. 2d 258, 841 N.W.2d 882 (Ct. App. 2014), the court unequivocally held that "all costs to make the building safe must be considered" when determining the feasibility of repair. Smoke and water damage remediation are essential components of restoring a fire-damaged property to a safe and habitable condition. Excluding these costs would artificially deflate the repair estimate and undermine the statutory threshold designed to identify structures that are not worth saving.

Furthermore, Auto-Owners’ argument fails on the facts provided. The record indicates that ServPro provided a revised estimate of approximately $112,850 for a "rebuild only" scope of work that explicitly excluded "cleaning & demo & smoke sealing." Even excluding the smoke remediation costs, the "rebuild only" estimate of $112,850 far exceeds fifty percent of the home’s assessed value of $124,000. Auto-Owners’ assertion that excluding smoke remediation reduces the repair cost to $36,350 is inconsistent with the record, as the $112,850 figure already excluded those specific costs. Consequently, whether or not smoke remediation is included, the economic threshold was met. The defense's argument that the 91% ratio is "close enough to raise serious questions" is unpersuasive; the statute sets a clear threshold, and the costs significantly exceed it.

**3. The City Was Not Required to Personally Inspect the Premises**

Auto-Owners claims the raze order was arbitrary because Inspector Craanen did not personally visit the property before issuing it. This argument misstates the legal requirements for issuing a raze order.

Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not mandate that a building inspector conduct a personal on-site inspection before issuing a raze order. In *City of Green Bay v. Smith*, 2008 WI App 112, 313 Wis. 2d 508, 757 N.W.2d 209 (Ct. App. 2008), the court held that inspectors may rely on credible professional assessments when they are reasonable under the circumstances. Auto-Owners attempts to distinguish *Smith* by noting the inspector there had prior knowledge of the property. However, the principle of *Smith* regarding the reasonableness of relying on professional estimates remains applicable. Here, Inspector Craanen reviewed a detailed estimate from ServPro, a professional restoration company, and verified the property’s assessed value through official records. This reliance on objective data constituted a reasonable basis for the City’s determination. The circuit court’s finding that Inspector Craanen acted professionally and in good faith is supported by the record and is not disturbed by this court. The defense's claim that the ServPro estimate was untrustworthy because it was prepared for insurance purposes is speculative and does not render the City's reliance unreasonable.

**4. The Raze Order Satisfied the Economic Threshold**

Finally, Auto-Owners argues that the raze order was unreasonable because the home could be repaired at a reasonable cost and that the order was improperly initiated at the insured’s request. This argument fails because the City’s determination met the statutory standard.

Under *State v. City of Kenosha*, 2010 WI 51, 325 Wis. 2d 491, 786 N.W.2d 23 (2010), a city’s determination that repairs are uneconomical is presumptively valid when the cost of repairs exceeds fifty percent of the building’s value. The ServPro estimate of $112,850 for repairs (excluding smoke remediation) far exceeded fifty percent of the home’s assessed value of $124,000. Because the City properly included all necessary repair costs, the economic threshold was clearly met. Auto-Owners’ assertion that the order was improperly initiated at the insured’s request does not alter this mathematical reality or the City’s statutory authority to act on information provided by concerned parties. The statutory criteria are objective, and the origin of the inquiry does not invalidate the City's action once those criteria are satisfied. The defense's theory of collusion to trigger the valued policy law is not supported by the evidence that the City independently verified the cost and value data.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Auto-Owners Insurance Company has failed to demonstrate that the raze order was invalid, unreasonable, or arbitrary. The City of Appleton acted within its authority under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. Accordingly, the circuit court’s order denying the motion for a temporary injunction is affirmed.
