Your Honor, this Court must affirm the trial court’s finding that the claimant met his burden of proving a work-related accident. The employer’s challenges to the trial court’s ruling are legally insupportable under Louisiana workers’ compensation precedent. Louisiana law is unequivocal: a claimant need not produce eyewitnesses to prove a work-related accident. As the Fourth Circuit held in *Jones v. Dillard’s*, 10-2043 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1/26/11), 58 So. 3d 1152, the testimony of the injured employee alone, if credible, may be sufficient to establish a work-related accident. Here, the claimant’s testimony—that he injured his back and shoulder while lifting heavy metal forms weighing up to 250 pounds—was credible, consistent, and uncontradicted by any competent evidence. The employer’s reliance on the absence of eyewitnesses is legally irrelevant. The trial court properly credited the claimant’s account, and this Court must uphold that credibility determination.  

The employer’s contention that reliance on the claimant’s brother’s testimony invalidates the finding is equally meritless. Raul Salazar, though not an eyewitness, confirmed the claimant was in significant pain at the end of the workday—a critical corroborating fact. Louisiana courts have repeatedly held that such testimony, when consistent with the claimant’s account, satisfies the burden of proof. In *Pelt v. City of New Orleans*, 02-1053 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/5/03), 840 So. 2d 1219, the court affirmed an award where the claimant’s brother testified that he observed the claimant in pain immediately after the alleged accident. The court explicitly rejected the employer’s argument that such testimony was insufficient, stating: “Corroboration need not come from an eyewitness; it may arise from the claimant’s own credible testimony and the observations of others regarding the claimant’s condition.” Here, Raul’s testimony that the claimant was “in pain at the end of the day” directly supports the claimant’s version of events and provides the necessary corroboration.  

Crucially, the medical evidence is uncontroverted and independently confirms the claimant’s account. The claimant sought treatment from Dr. Michael Davis on March 24, 2011—the day after the alleged accident—and reported low-back and left shoulder pain resulting from lifting at work. Dr. Davis placed him off work immediately, and subsequent treatment by Dr. F. Allen Johnston and Dr. Ward Sudderth consistently diagnosed work-related injuries. As this Court recognized in *Smith v. City of New Orleans*, 99-2373 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/21/00), 765 So. 2d 1242, “medical evidence following an alleged accident is strong proof of injury, particularly when treatment is sought promptly.” The employer offered no medical evidence to contradict these records or suggest an alternative cause for the injuries.  

The employer’s assertion that it “reasonably controverted” the claim is baseless. Under Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F), penalties and attorney’s fees are warranted when an employer fails to pay benefits without a “reasonable basis” for denial. In *Boudreaux v. Here, the employer’s insurer denied the claim on April 5, 2011, merely stating, “we have completed our investigation and find that you have not sustained a compensable work related claim,” without addressing the immediate medical treatment, the consistent testimony of the claimant and his brother, or the supervisor’s confirmation that the work involved lifting heavy forms. Finally, the parties’ stipulation to the average weekly wage is irrelevant to the factual finding of a work-related accident. The trial court correctly determined the accident occurred based on the evidence presented, and the wage stipulation pertains only to benefit calculations—not the underlying liability issue.  

Your Honor, the trial court’s ruling was fully supported by the record. The employer’s challenges ignore settled law that credible testimony and medical evidence—without eyewitnesses—suffice to prove a compensable accident. This Court should affirm the judgment in full.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE

The plaintiff claims he sustained a back and shoulder injury on March 23, 2011, while lifting heavy metal forms at the Earhart Boulevard job site. However, the evidence presented at trial fails to establish that a compensable accident occurred. The claimant did not immediately report any injury, no eyewitnesses observed the alleged accident, and the claimant's own brother—who testified on his behalf—admitted he did not witness the incident. The medical evidence, while documenting treatment, does not establish that the alleged work activity caused the claimed injuries. The trial court erred in crediting the claimant's account despite the absence of any corroborating evidence beyond the self-serving testimony of his brother, who had a clear financial interest in the outcome of this litigation. The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment below.

## II. THE PLAINTIFF FAILED TO MEET HIS BURDEN OF PROOF

### A. The Absence of Eyewitnesses Is Legally Significant

The plaintiff's opening argument correctly notes that Louisiana law does not require eyewitnesses to prove a work-related accident. However, the plaintiff mischaracterizes the defense's position and ignores the distinction between cases where some corroboration exists and cases where the claimant's account stands entirely alone.

In *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 94-1562 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/15/95), 652 So. 2d 46, this Court held that while eyewitnesses are not required, the claimant must still establish the occurrence of an accident by a preponderance of the evidence. The Court emphasized that "the testimony of the claimant alone may be sufficient to establish an accident, but only where that testimony is credible and is supported by the surrounding circumstances." Here, the surrounding circumstances do not support the claimant's account.

The plaintiff cites *Jones v. Dillard's*, 10-2043 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1/26/11), 58 So. 3d 1152, for the proposition that the claimant's testimony alone suffices. However, in *Jones*, the claimant's testimony was corroborated by immediate medical treatment and the testimony of a coworker who witnessed the accident. The plaintiff cannot rely on *Jones* for the proposition that uncorroborated testimony suffices because that case involved corroboration from multiple sources.

The defense further notes that no fewer than four witnesses who were present at the jobsite on March 23, 2011, were identified by the claimant himself as being in the vicinity of the alleged accident. None of these witnesses saw or recalled any accident. This total absence of corroboration from any non-interested party distinguishes this case from those where Louisiana courts have found sufficient proof.

### B. Raul Salazar's Testimony Does Not Constitute Corroboration

The plaintiff argues that Raul Salazar's testimony that the claimant was "in pain at the end of the day" constitutes corroboration. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, Raul Salazar is the claimant's brother and is not a disinterested witness. The defense elicited testimony that Raul Salazar had been paying the living expenses of the claimant since March 23, 2011, because the claimant had not worked. This creates an obvious financial interest in the outcome of this litigation.

Second, Raul Salazar did not witness any accident. He testified that he heard about the accident from the claimant himself. His testimony regarding the claimant's pain is merely self-serving hearsay repeated from the claimant.

Third, the case cited by the plaintiff, *Pelt v. City of New Orleans*, 02-1053 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/5/03), 840 So. 2d 1219, is distinguishable. In *Pelt*, the court found corroboration not merely from the brother's testimony about pain, but from the totality of circumstances including the claimant's immediate reporting, medical treatment, and the absence of any evidence of an alternative cause. Here, the claimant delayed reporting the alleged accident, and the defense has presented evidence suggesting the claimant may have had preexisting conditions or alternative explanations for his complaints.

The defense further notes that the *Pelt* court specifically stated that "corroboration may arise from the claimant's own credible testimony and the observations of others regarding the claimant's condition." The court did not hold that such testimony, standing alone, is always sufficient. In this case, the claimant's testimony is not "credible" when measured against the surrounding circumstances, including the failure to immediately report the accident and the absence of any independent corroboration.

## III. THE MEDICAL EVIDENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH A WORK-RELATED CAUSE

The plaintiff argues that the uncontroverted medical evidence establishes a work-related injury. This argument conflates the existence of medical treatment with the cause of the medical condition.

The medical records establish that the claimant sought treatment and reported pain. They do not establish, to a reasonable degree of medical certainty, that the alleged lifting activity on March 23, 2011, caused the claimant's injuries. The defense notes that the medical records contain no opinion establishing causation. The plaintiff bears the burden of proving that the alleged work activity caused the claimed disability, and he has failed to meet this burden.

In *Scott v. City of New Orleans*, 94-0941 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/15/94), 647 So. 2d 1354, this Court held that medical evidence establishing only that the claimant sought treatment for complaints is insufficient to establish a work-related cause. The claimant must present medical evidence establishing a causal connection between the alleged accident and the claimed disability. Here, no such evidence exists.

The defense further notes that the claimant was referred to the Industrial Medical Clinic because his family doctor "did not deal with workers' compensation." This raises questions about the reliability and completeness of the initial medical evaluation. The defense was deprived of the opportunity to have the claimant examined by its own medical experts during the critical early period following the alleged accident.

## IV. THE CLAIMANT'S FAILURE TO IMMEDIATELY REPORT THE ACCIDENT WEIGHS HEAVILY AGAINST HIS CLAIM

The facts establish that the claimant did not report the alleged accident on the day it occurred, despite working until the end of the shift. The following morning, March 24, 2011, when he arrived at the jobsite, he was met by his supervisor, Bryan Monte, who handed him a Personnel Action Form relating to an incident between the claimant and another supervisor. The claimant testified that he intended to tell Monte about the accident at that time, but did not do so.

This delay in reporting is significant. In *Brown v. City of New Orleans*, 95-2183 (La. App. 4 Cir. 4/3/96), 672 So. 2d 953, this Court held that a claimant's failure to report an accident promptly, without reasonable explanation, weighs heavily against the credibility of the claim. The claimant offered no reasonable explanation for his failure to report the accident immediately or on the morning of March 24, 2011, when he had the opportunity to speak with his supervisor.

The defense further notes that the claimant called the jobsite foreman, Byron Dupre, days after the accident to indicate that he wanted to return to work. This call was made before the claimant reported any accident. This conduct is inconsistent with a claimant who had genuinely sustained a debilitating injury requiring indefinite total disability.

## V. THE EMPLOYER REASONABLY CONTROVERTED THE CLAIM

The plaintiff argues that the employer unreasonably controverted the claim, warranting penalties and attorney's fees under Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F). This argument fails.

The employer denied the claim based on the absence of any evidence establishing that a compensable accident occurred. No eyewitnesses confirmed the alleged accident. The claimant's own witnesses could not corroborate the occurrence. The medical evidence did not establish causation. Under these circumstances, the employer's denial was reasonable.

In *Theriot v. Terrebonne Parish School Board*, 95-2345 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/28/96), 676 So. 2d 715, the court held that an employer's denial is not unreasonable when the claimant fails to present sufficient evidence to establish a compensable claim. Here, the claimant failed to meet his burden of proof at trial, demonstrating that the employer's skepticism was well-founded.

The plaintiff cites *Boudreaux v. State*, 06-1815 (La. 12/15/06), 947 So. 2d 133, for the proposition that denial without medical investigation constitutes unreasonable controversion. However, the employer did investigate the claim. The April 5, 2011 letter from the insurer states that the investigation was completed. The employer's position—that the claimant failed to establish a compensable accident—is a legitimate basis for denial, particularly where, as here, the evidence of an accident is wholly lacking.

## VI. THE TRIAL COURT'S CREDIBILITY DETERMINATIONS ARE NOT ENTITLED TO DEFERENCE

The plaintiff argues that this Court must defer to the trial court's credibility determinations under the manifest error standard. While the defense acknowledges the general principle, it submits that the manifest error standard does not apply where the trial court's factual findings are based on testimony that is internally inconsistent or contradicted by the documentary evidence.

Here, the trial court credited the claimant's account despite the absence of any corroboration from disinterested witnesses. The claimant's brother, Raul Salazar, had a financial interest in the outcome of the litigation. The claimant's own conduct—failing to report the accident immediately, calling the foreman about returning to work before reporting any injury—is inconsistent with his claim of total disability.

In *Alexander v. Pellerin Milner & Mercer*, 93-1628 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1/27/94), 631 So. 2d 486, this Court held that where a claimant's testimony is contradicted by the surrounding circumstances, the trial court may not simply credit the claimant's uncorroborated account without addressing the contradictions. The trial court failed to address the significant contradictions in this case.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and dismiss the claimant's claim for workers' compensation benefits. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on an appeal by Command Construction, LLC (Defendant) seeking to reverse the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge which found in favor of Pedro Salazar (Claimant). The Defendant contends the Claimant failed to prove a compensable accident by a preponderance of the evidence, arguing that the absence of eyewitnesses, the biased nature of the corroborating testimony, and the lack of definitive medical causation render the trial court’s findings manifestly erroneous. After a thorough review of the record and the arguments presented by both parties, the Court affirms the judgment of the trial court.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

In workers’ compensation cases, the appellate court’s review of a trial court’s factual findings is limited by the manifest error standard. A trial court’s determination of credibility and the weight of evidence should not be disturbed unless clearly wrong. As established in *Alexander v. Pellerin Milner & Mercer*, 93-1628 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1/27/94), 631 So. 2d 486, where a trial court's findings are based on reasonable evaluations of credibility and the evidence presented, the appellate court may not simply substitute its judgment for that of the trial court even if the appellate court would have weighed the evidence differently.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Sufficiency of Evidence Regarding the Accident**

The Defendant argues that the Claimant failed to meet his burden of proof because no eyewitnesses testified to the accident and the only corroboration came from a biased family member. However, Louisiana law is clear that a claimant’s uncorroborated testimony is sufficient to establish a work-related accident, provided that testimony is credible. In *Jones v. Dillard’s*, 10-2043 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1/26/11), 58 So. 3d 1152, the court held that the testimony of the injured employee alone, if credible, may be sufficient to establish a work-related accident.

Here, the Claimant provided detailed testimony regarding the specific mechanism of injury—lifting heavy metal forms weighing up to 250 pounds. This testimony was consistent and uncontradicted by any direct evidence to the contrary. While the Defendant correctly notes that eyewitnesses are not required, citing *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 94-1562 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/15/95), 652 So. 2d 46, the absence of eyewitnesses does not automatically defeat a claim where the claimant's testimony is credible and supported by the surrounding circumstances.

The Defendant attacks the credibility of Raul Salazar, the Claimant’s brother, citing his financial interest in the outcome. While this bias is a relevant factor for the trier of fact to consider, it does not render his testimony worthless. In *Pelt v. City of New Orleans*, 02-1053 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/5/03), 840 So. 2d 1219, the court affirmed an award where the claimant’s brother testified to observing the claimant in pain immediately after the accident. The court recognized that corroboration may arise from observations of the claimant's condition. Raul Salazar’s testimony that the Claimant was in significant pain at the end of the workday corroborates the Claimant’s account of the timing and onset of symptoms. The trial court was within its discretion to weigh this testimony alongside the Claimant’s own statements.

**B. Medical Evidence and Causation**

The Defendant asserts that the medical evidence is insufficient to establish causation, relying on *Scott v. City of New Orleans*, 94-0941 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/15/94), 647 So. 2d 1354. However, the record reflects that the Claimant sought treatment from Dr. Michael Davis on March 24, 2011, the very day after the alleged accident, reporting low-back and left shoulder pain specifically resulting from lifting at work. This prompt medical attention is strong proof of injury. As held in *Smith v. City of New Orleans*, 99-2373 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/21/00), 765 So. 2d 1242, medical evidence following an alleged accident is strong proof of injury, particularly when treatment is sought promptly.

The subsequent treatment by Dr. F. Allen Johnston and Dr. Ward Sudderth, which resulted in a finding of temporary total disability, further supports the Claimant’s claim. The medical records are uncontroverted regarding the existence of the injury and the disability. The Defendant failed to produce any medical evidence to suggest an alternative cause for the injuries. Under these circumstances, the trial court correctly found that the medical evidence established a causal link between the work activity and the Claimant’s condition.

**C. Reporting of the Accident**

The Defendant argues that the Claimant’s failure to immediately report the accident weighs heavily against his credibility, citing *Brown v. City of New Orleans*, 95-2183 (La. App. 4 Cir. 4/3/96), 672 So. 2d 953. While the Claimant did not report the accident on the day it occurred, he testified that he intended to report it to his supervisor, Bryan Monte, the following morning. The interruption by the presentation of the Personnel Action Form regarding a separate incident provides a reasonable explanation for the delay. Furthermore, the Claimant did report the accident to Monte shortly thereafter, leading to the completion of an accident report. Given these circumstances, the delay does not constitute the type of unexplained lapse that would mandate a rejection of the claimant's credibility.

**D. Reasonableness of Employer’s Controversion**

Finally, the Defendant contends that its denial of the claim was reasonable, precluding penalties and attorney fees under *Theriot v. Terrebonne Parish School Board*, 95-2345 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/28/96), 676 So. 2d 715. The Claimant counters that the denial was arbitrary and capricious because it lacked a reasonable basis, citing *Boudreaux v. State*, 06-1815 (La. 12/15/06), 947 So. 2d 133.

The insurer’s denial letter dated April 5, 2011, stated that the investigation was completed and the claim was not compensable, yet it failed to address the medical evidence already obtained or the supervisor's accident report. As noted in *Boudreaux v. State*, a denial of benefits without any medical investigation or contrary medical evidence constitutes unreasonable controversion. Here, the employer denied the claim despite the Claimant’s prompt reporting to the supervisor and the immediate medical documentation linking the injury to work. The employer’s reliance on the lack of eyewitnesses, in the face of uncontroverted medical evidence and the Claimant's credible testimony, was not a reasonable basis for denial. Therefore, the trial court did not err in finding the employer’s controversion unreasonable.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court’s findings are not manifestly erroneous. The Claimant met his burden of proving a compensable accident through credible testimony and uncontroverted medical evidence. The employer’s denial of the claim was unreasonable. Accordingly, the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge is affirmed.

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  

The defense’s arguments mischaracterize Louisiana workers’ compensation law, ignore binding precedent, and improperly reweigh evidence this Court cannot disturb under the manifest error standard. The trial court correctly found the claimant met his burden of proof through credible testimony, corroboration from non-eyewitness observations, and uncontroverted medical evidence. The employer’s challenges lack legal merit.  

### I. EYEWITNESSES ARE NOT REQUIRED, AND THE CLAIMANT’S TESTIMONY ALONE IS SUFFICIENT WHEN CREDIBLE AND SUPPORTED BY CIRCUMSTANCES  
The defense incorrectly asserts that the absence of eyewitnesses invalidates the claim. Louisiana law explicitly holds that eyewitnesses are not required to prove a compensable accident. *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 94-1562 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/15/95), 652 So. 2d 46, confirms that "the testimony of the claimant alone may be sufficient to establish an accident, but only where that testimony is credible and is supported by the surrounding circumstances." Here, the claimant provided detailed, consistent testimony about lifting heavy metal forms (up to 250 pounds) at the Earhart Boulevard site, corroborated by the supervisor’s testimony that the work required three to four men to lift such forms. The defense’s reliance on *Jones v. Dillard’s*, 10-2043 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1/26/11), 58 So. 3d 1152, is misplaced; *Jones* does not require eyewitnesses but instead stands for the proposition that uncorroborated credible testimony may suffice. The defense ignores that *Jones* involved no eyewitnesses yet affirmed the claimant’s testimony as sufficient where it was credible and medically supported. The surrounding circumstances—supervisor testimony about the physical demands of the job, the claimant’s immediate medical treatment, and his brother’s observation of pain—provide sufficient corroboration under *Bruno*.  

### II. RALUL SALAZAR’S TESTIMONY CONSTITUTES VALID CORROBORATION OF THE CLAIMANT’S CONDITION  
The defense wrongly characterizes Raul Salazar’s testimony as "mere hearsay" or "self-serving." Louisiana courts have repeatedly upheld testimony from family members observing the claimant’s physical condition as sufficient corroboration. *Pelt v. City of New Orleans*, 02-1053 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/5/03), 840 So. 2d 1219, specifically affirmed that a brother’s testimony regarding the claimant’s "pain and difficulty walking" immediately after the alleged accident constituted valid corroboration, even where the brother did not witness the accident itself. Here, Raul Salazar testified he observed his brother in significant pain at the end of the workday on March 23, 2011—a direct observation of the claimant’s condition, not hearsay. The defense’s focus on Raul’s financial interest is irrelevant; while bias is a factor for the trier of fact to weigh, it does not render testimony "worthless" as a matter of law. *Alexander v. Pellerin Milner & Mercer*, 93-1628 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1/27/94), 631 So. 2d 486, confirms that appellate courts must defer to the trial court’s credibility determinations where reasonable evaluations of evidence exist. The trial court reasonably credited Raul’s testimony as part of the totality of circumstances supporting the claimant’s account.  

### III. MEDICAL EVIDENCE ESTABLISHES A CAUSAL LINK BETWEEN THE WORK ACTIVITY AND INJURY  
The defense’s contention that medical evidence "does not establish causation" misstates the law. Louisiana precedent holds that prompt medical treatment following an alleged accident is strong proof of injury and causation. *Scott v. City of New Orleans*, 94-0941 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/15/94), 647 So. 2d 1354, explicitly states that "medical evidence following an alleged accident is strong proof of injury, particularly when treatment is sought promptly." Here, the claimant sought treatment from Dr. Davis on March 24, 2011—the day after the alleged accident—and reported low-back and left shoulder pain directly resulting from lifting at work. Dr. Davis documented the work-related cause and restricted him from work. Subsequent treatment by Dr. Johnston and Dr. Sudderth confirmed ongoing disability and linked it to the work incident. The medical records are uncontroverted; the employer produced no medical evidence suggesting an alternative cause or preexisting condition. The defense’s argument that the claimant was "referred to the Industrial Medical Clinic because his family doctor did not deal with workers’ compensation" is irrelevant; the referral does not undermine the initial medical evaluation or the causal connection established by the treating physician.  

### IV. THE EMPLOYER’S CONTROVERSION WAS UNREASONABLE AND WARRANTS PENALTIES AND ATTORNEY’S FEES  
The defense’s claim that its denial was "reasonable" is legally untenable. Under Louisiana law, an employer’s denial of benefits is unreasonable if it lacks a factual or medical basis, particularly when it ignores medical evidence. *Boudreaux v. State*, 06-1815 (La. 12/15/06), 947 So. 2d 133, holds that "denial of benefits without any medical investigation or contrary medical evidence constitutes unreasonable controversion." Here, the insurer’s denial letter dated April 5, 2011, stated the claim was "not compensable" despite having received:  
- The claimant’s immediate report to supervisor Monte (who prepared an accident report);  
- Medical records from Dr. Davis documenting work-related pain and restrictions;  
- Supervisor testimony confirming the physical demands of the job.  
The employer relied solely on the absence of eyewitnesses—despite *Bruno* and *Jones* establishing that eyewitnesses are not required—while ignoring the uncontroverted medical evidence and supervisor’s report. This is precisely the type of unreasonable controversion condemned in *Boudreaux*.  

### CONCLUSION  
The trial court’s findings were not manifestly erroneous. The claimant met his burden through credible testimony, corroboration of his physical condition by his brother, prompt medical treatment establishing causation, and uncontroverted medical records. The employer’s denial was unreasonable under *Boudreaux*. This Court must affirm the judgment.

## I. THE CLAIMANT'S TESTIMONY ALONE IS INSUFFICIENT WHERE IT IS UNCORROBORATED AND CONTRADICTED BY THE RECORD

The plaintiff correctly notes that Louisiana law does not require eyewitnesses to prove a compensable accident. However, the plaintiff misapplies this principle by ignoring the critical qualifier: the claimant's testimony must be "credible" and "supported by the surrounding circumstances." *Bruno v. The record contains significant contradictions that undermine the claimant's credibility. The claimant testified he did not sign the form "because there was no place on the form for him to sign"—an explanation that does not account for his failure to verbally inform Monte of his injury at that moment. This unexplained delay in reporting, combined with the claimant's subsequent actions, raises reasonable doubt about the accuracy of his account.

Furthermore, the plaintiff overstates the corroboration provided by the supervisor's testimony. While Bryan Monte testified that the work involved lifting forms weighing up to 250 pounds, this testimony does not corroborate that the claimant actually sustained an injury while performing this work. ## II. RUAL SALAZAR'S TESTIMONY IS BIASED AND DOES NOT CONSTITUTE RELIABLE CORROBORATION

The plaintiff cites *Pelt v. City of New Orleans*, 02-1053 (La. The plaintiff argues that the employer's failure to produce alternative medical evidence is dispositive. This misstates the burden of proof. **UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on an appeal by Command Construction, LLC (Defendant) seeking to reverse the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge which found in favor of Pedro Salazar (Claimant). The Defendant contends the Claimant failed to prove a compensable accident and that the trial court erred in finding the employer’s denial of the claim unreasonable. After reviewing the rebuttals of both parties and the record, the Court affirms the finding of compensability but reverses the finding of unreasonable controversion.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

In workers’ compensation cases, the appellate court’s review of a trial court’s factual findings is limited by the manifest error standard. A trial court’s determination of credibility and the weight of evidence should not be disturbed unless clearly wrong. As established in *Alexander v. Pellerin Milner & Mercer*, 93-1628 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1/27/94), 631 So. 2d 486, where a trial court's findings are based on reasonable evaluations of credibility and the evidence presented, the appellate court may not simply substitute its judgment for that of the trial court even if the appellate court would have weighed the evidence differently.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Sufficiency of Evidence Regarding the Accident**

The Defendant argues that the Claimant’s testimony is uncorroborated and contradicted by the record, specifically pointing to the Claimant’s failure to immediately report the injury when presented with a Personnel Action Form. While the Defendant highlights inconsistencies in the Claimant’s account, the trial court is the ultimate fact-fearer regarding credibility. Louisiana law is clear that a claimant’s uncorroborated testimony is sufficient to establish a work-related accident, provided that testimony is credible. In *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 94-1562 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/15/95), 652 So. 2d 46, the court held that the testimony of the claimant alone may be sufficient to establish an accident if credible and supported by the surrounding circumstances.

Here, the Claimant provided detailed testimony regarding the mechanism of injury—lifting heavy metal forms weighing up to 250 pounds—which was consistent with the supervisor’s description of the work duties. The Claimant’s explanation for the delay in reporting—that he was interrupted by the disciplinary matter involving the Personnel Action Form—was accepted by the trial court. The Defendant’s argument that this delay constitutes a fatal credibility defect is a request to reweigh the evidence, which this Court cannot do under the manifest error standard. The trial court’s determination that the Claimant’s testimony was credible and supported by the circumstances is not manifestly erroneous.

**B. Corroboration and Credibility of Witnesses**

The Defendant challenges the trial court’s reliance on Raul Salazar’s testimony, citing his financial interest in the outcome. While bias is a factor to be considered, it does not render testimony inadmissible or automatically devoid of value. In *Pelt v. City of New Orleans*, 02-1053 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/5/03), 840 So. 2d 1219, the court affirmed a judgment where a brother’s testimony regarding the claimant’s pain immediately after the incident served as valid corroboration. Raul Salazar’s testimony that the Claimant was in significant pain at the end of the workday corroborates the Claimant’s account of the onset of symptoms. The trial court was within its discretion to weigh this testimony, considering the bias, and still find it supportive of the Claimant’s claim.

**C. Medical Evidence and Causation**

The Defendant argues that the medical evidence is insufficient to establish causation because the records lack a definitive causation opinion and the initial treating physician referred the Claimant out. However, the Claimant sought treatment from Dr. Michael Davis on March 24, 2011, the day after the alleged accident, reporting low-back and left shoulder pain specifically resulting from lifting at work. As held in *Scott v. City of New Orleans*, 94-0941 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/15/94), 647 So. 2d 1354, medical evidence following an alleged accident is strong proof of injury, particularly when treatment is sought promptly.

The subsequent treatment by Dr. F. Allen Johnston and Dr. Ward Sudderth, which resulted in a finding of temporary total disability, further supports the Claimant’s claim. The medical records are uncontroverted regarding the existence of the injury and the disability. The Defendant’s argument that the Claimant failed to meet his burden of proof because the records do not contain a specific "causation opinion" ignores the fact that the treating physicians documented the work-related nature of the injury and removed the Claimant from work accordingly. The trial court did not err in finding that the medical evidence established a causal link between the work activity and the Claimant’s condition.

**D. Reasonableness of Employer’s Controversion**

The Defendant contends that its denial of the claim was reasonable, precluding penalties and attorney fees. The Claimant argues the denial was arbitrary and capricious under *Boudreaux v. State*, 06-1815 (La. 12/15/06), 947 So. 2d 133. However, the defense correctly distinguishes *Boudreaux*, noting that the employer there denied benefits without any medical investigation. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by both the Claimant, Pedro Salazar, and the Defendant, Command Construction, LLC. The Claimant seeks reversal of the appellate court's prior decision to vacate the trial court's finding of unreasonable controversion, urging the imposition of penalties and attorney fees. **II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for workers’ compensation cases is well settled. An appellate court may not set aside a trial court's finding of fact in the absence of manifest error. This standard is articulated in *Alexander v. Pellerin Milner & Mercer*, 93-1628 (La. App. 4 Cir. 1/27/94), 631 So. 2d 486. However, the determination of whether an employer's denial of a claim constitutes arbitrary, capricious, or unreasonable conduct is a legal question that depends on the reasonableness of the employer's position at the time of the denial, based on the facts then known. This analysis is governed by the principles established in *Boudreaux v. State*, 06-1815 (La. 12/15/06), 947 So. 2d 133, and *Theriot v. Terrebonne Parish School Board*, 95-2345 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/28/96), 676 So. 2d 715.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Compensability**

The Defendant has accepted the appellate court's affirmation of the trial court's finding that the Claimant sustained a compensable work-related accident. The Claimant’s rebuttal arguments regarding the sufficiency of his testimony, the credibility of his brother, and the medical causation are well-taken under the law. As established in *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 94-1562 (La. App. 4 Cir. 3/15/95), 652 So. 2d 46, a claimant’s uncorroborated testimony can be sufficient to establish an accident if credible. Furthermore, the medical evidence sought promptly after the accident, as discussed in *Scott v. City of New Orleans*, 94-0941 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/15/94), 647 So. 2d 1354, supports the finding of injury. However, as the Defendant concedes this point and the appellate court has ruled on it, the issue of compensability is not before the Court for further modification. The finding of compensability stands.

**B. Reasonableness of Employer’s Controversion**

The central dispute before this Court is whether the Defendant’s denial of the Claimant’s workers’ compensation claim on April 5, 2011, was unreasonable, thereby warranting penalties and attorney fees.

The Claimant argues that the denial was arbitrary under *Boudreaux v. State*, 06-1815 (La. 12/15/06), 947 So. 2d 133, asserting that the employer denied benefits without a sufficient medical investigation and despite uncontroverted medical evidence. However, the record contradicts the assertion that no investigation occurred. The insurer’s correspondence dated April 5, 2011, explicitly states, "We have completed our investigation." This factual distinction separates this case from *Boudreaux*, where the employer denied benefits without any investigation.

More importantly, the reasonableness of the denial must be evaluated based on the information available to the employer at the time of the denial, not with the benefit of hindsight. As held in *Theriot v. Terrebonne Parish School Board*, 95-2345 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/28/96), 676 So. 2d 715, penalties and attorney's fees are not warranted where the employer's denial is based on a genuine dispute about the facts or the law.

The Court finds that the Defendant has demonstrated the existence of a genuine dispute of fact at the time of the denial. First, there were no eyewitnesses to the accident. The Claimant’s own brother, who was working nearby, did not see the incident. The absence of corroboration regarding the mechanism of injury provided a legitimate factual basis for the employer to question the claim.

Second, the medical evidence available to the employer on April 5, 2011, was ambiguous regarding the long-term work-related nature of the injury. At that time, the employer had the records of Dr. Michael Davis, who had treated the Claimant for a short period (March 24 to March 29, 2011) and subsequently referred him to the Industrial Medical Clinic because his own clinic did not handle workers’ compensation cases. The definitive medical evidence establishing temporary total disability—provided by Dr. F. Allen Johnston—did not occur until April 20, 2011, two weeks after the denial. The employer could not have arbitrarily denied a claim for long-term disability based on medical evidence that did not yet exist.

The Claimant’s reliance on the "uncontroverted" nature of the medical records ignores the timeline of events. While Dr. Davis’s notes documented the immediate injury, the referral to a specialist and the lack of a definitive long-term prognosis created a genuine dispute regarding the extent of disability and the necessity of ongoing treatment. Under these circumstances, the employer’s decision to investigate and deny the claim pending further clarification was not arbitrary or capricious; it was a reasonable response to a factual dispute.

Therefore, the trial court’s finding of unreasonable controversion was manifestly erroneous. The appellate court correctly reversed this finding, and the Claimant’s arguments to reinstate penalties and attorney fees are rejected.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the appellate court’s ruling is affirmed. The judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge is affirmed in part regarding the finding of compensability, and the reversal of the finding of unreasonable controversion is upheld. The Claimant is not entitled to penalties or attorney fees.
