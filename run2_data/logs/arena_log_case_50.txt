## INTRODUCTION

The Estate of Audrey Blessing, through Personal Representative Cynthia Hagensen, respectfully submits this opening response in defense of the trial court's reversal and in support of the position that the children of Audrey's deceased second husband, Carl Blaschka, are not "stepchildren" within the meaning of Washington's wrongful death statute, RCW 4.20.020, and therefore are not entitled to participate in the wrongful death action brought by the Estate.

The respondents, the Blaschka children, seek to establish their status as "stepchildren" under the wrongful death statute to claim beneficiary status. However, the plain language of the statute, combined with established Washington precedent regarding the termination of stepparent-stepchild relationships, demonstrates that the trial court erred in declaring the Blaschka children to be stepchildren of Audrey Blessing at the time of her death. Because the stepparent-stepchild relationship legally terminated upon the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994—thirteen years before Audrey's death in 2007—the Blaschka children cannot qualify as stepchildren under RCW 4.20.020.

---

## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The following facts are drawn exclusively from the record and are not disputed by the parties:

Audrey P. Blessing was first married to Alvin Hendricks from 1949 until 1964, when they divorced. During that marriage, Audrey and Mr. Hendricks had three daughters, including Cynthia Hagensen.

In December 1964, Audrey married her second husband, Carl Blaschka. Mr. Blaschka had four children from his previous marriage to Marion: John, Julie, Diana, and Carla. Three of these children were Marion's that Mr. Blaschka had adopted. Importantly, Audrey did not adopt any of Mr. Blaschka's children. Audrey and Mr. Blaschka raised their seven children together, but no children were born to Audrey and Mr. Blaschka during their marriage.

Audrey and Carl Blaschka were married until his death in 1994. Following Mr. Blaschka's death, Audrey maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children.

In 2002, Audrey married her third husband, Robert Blessing. Mr. Blessing died in 2005. Audrey remained close with the Blaschka children while married to Mr. Blessing and after his death.

Audrey remained unmarried and died in September 2007, allegedly as the result of an automobile collision. Audrey's will was filed in probate in October 2007 and named Ms. Hagensen as personal representative. Notably, Audrey listed the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries of a portion of her estate.

Ms. Hagensen, as personal representative, made a wrongful death claim for the Estate arising from the automobile collision. The parties agree that funds derived from the wrongful death claim are not part of Audrey's estate.

In November 2009, the Blaschka children petitioned for a judicial determination that they were beneficiaries of the Estate's wrongful death claim. The Estate moved for judgment declaring that the Blaschka children are not "stepchildren" for purposes of the wrongful death statute.

In June 2010, the trial court denied the Estate's motion and declared the Blaschka children "stepchildren" of Audrey Blessing and beneficiaries in any wrongful death claim brought by her estate.

---

## LEGAL ANALYSIS

### I. The Wrongful Death Statute and the Definition of "Stepchild"

Under RCW 4.20.020, Washington's wrongful death statute, a surviving spouse, child, or dependent relative may maintain a wrongful death action. The statute defines the classes of beneficiaries who may recover damages for wrongful death.

The central question presented is whether the children of Audrey's deceased second husband—children who were her stepchildren during her marriage to their father—maintain that status for purposes of the wrongful death statute after the termination of the marriage by the husband's death and the wife's subsequent remarriage to another person.

### II. The Stepparent-Stepchild Relationship Terminates Upon the Death of the Stepparent

The defense submits that the stepparent-stepchild relationship is inherently dependent upon the existence of the marital relationship between the stepparent and the child's biological parent. When that marital relationship ends—whether by divorce or death—the stepparent-stepchild relationship likewise terminates.

This interpretation is consistent with the nature of the stepparent relationship, which arises solely from the marriage between a child and one of the child's parents. As the Washington Court of Appeals recognized in its decision in this very case, "Because the stepparent/stepchild relationship had legally ended before Audrey's death, the trial court erred."

The relationship between a stepparent and stepchild is not a blood relationship nor an adoptive relationship. It is a relational status that exists only so long as the qualifying marital relationship exists. Once the marriage terminating event occurs—here, the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994—the legal foundation for the stepparent-stepchild relationship disappears.

### III. The Blaschka Children Were Not Dependent Upon Audrey at the Time of Her Death

RCW 4.20.020 requires not merely a familial relationship but contemplates dependency. The statute is designed to provide recovery to those who were actually dependent upon the deceased for support.

At the time of Audrey's death in 2007, the Blaschka children were adults. They were not dependent upon Audrey for support. They had not been her stepchildren for thirteen years—since the death of their father in 1994. During those thirteen years, Audrey had married another person (Robert Blessing) and that marriage, too, had ended by the death of Mr. Blessing in 2005.

The factual context demonstrates that while Audrey maintained a "close relationship" with the Blaschka children, this relationship was not a stepparent-stepchild relationship. It was the relationship between an adult stepchild and their former stepparent—a relationship that, while possibly emotionally significant, carries no legal rights under the wrongful death statute.

### IV. The Legislative Intent Behind RCW 4.20.020 Supports the Estate's Position

The wrongful death statute must be interpreted in light of its purpose: to provide compensation to those who have lost a source of support due to another's wrongful act. The statute is not designed to provide a windfall to all persons who might claim a familial connection to the deceased.

The legislature, in enacting RCW 4.20.020, defined specific classes of beneficiaries. If the legislature had intended to include stepchildren regardless of whether the stepparent-stepchild relationship existed at the time of death, it could have expressly so provided. It did not.

To hold that the Blaschka children are entitled to recover as stepchildren would extend the scope of the statute beyond its plain language and legislative intent. It would create a perpetual stepchild status that survives the termination of the marriage from which the relationship arose—a result the legislature could not have intended.

### V. The Trial Court's Ruling Creates an Absurd Result

If the trial court's ruling were upheld, a former stepchild would maintain a legal claim against a stepparent's estate indefinitely, regardless of the passage of time, subsequent marriages, or the termination of any dependency. This would create an anomalous class of beneficiaries unknown to the statute and produce absurd results.

Consider: if Audrey had divorced Carl Blaschka in 1994 rather than him dying, would the Blaschka children still be her stepchildren for purposes of the wrongful death statute? The answer must be no. The death of a spouse functions similarly to a divorce in terminating the marital relationship. In either event, the stepparent-stepchild relationship ends.

The defense submits that the correct interpretation—that the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminates upon the death of the spouse who is the parent of the stepchild—produces a logical and consistent result that aligns with the statute's text and purpose.

---

## REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff (Blaschka children) likely argues that the relationship between Audrey and the Blaschka children remained a stepparent-stepchild relationship because: (1) Audrey never formally adopted the children, (2) Audrey maintained a close relationship with the children throughout her life, and (3) Audrey specifically included the Blaschka children in her will as residuary beneficiaries.

These arguments fail for the following reasons:

**First**, the failure to adopt is precisely the point. Because Audrey never adopted the Blaschka children, the only basis for the stepparent-stepchild relationship was her marriage to their father. Once that marriage ended by his death, the relationship ended.

**Second**, the continued emotional relationship between Audrey and the Blaschka children, while not disputed, does not create a legal relationship under the statute. The wrongful death statute concerns legal relationships and dependency, not emotional bonds. The statute specifically defines classes of beneficiaries based on legal status, not affection.

**Third**, Audrey's inclusion of the Blaschka children in her will as residuary beneficiaries is irrelevant to the question of wrongful death beneficiaries. The parties stipulated that "funds derived from the wrongful death claim are not part of Audrey's estate." The testamentary disposition of Audrey's estate is governed by different legal principles than the distribution of wrongful death proceeds. Audrey may have chosen to remember the Blaschka children in her will for any number of reasons, but this does not confer upon them the status of stepchildren under the wrongful death statute.

For the foregoing reasons, the Estate of Audrey Blessing respectfully requests that this Court uphold the reversal of the trial court's ruling and declare that the Blaschka children are not "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 and are not entitled to participate in the wrongful death action brought by the Estate.

The stepparent-stepchild relationship between Audrey Blessing and the Blaschka children terminated upon the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994. At the time of Audrey's death in 2007, no stepparent-stepchild relationship existed. Therefore, the Blaschka children cannot claim beneficiary status under Washington's wrongful death statute.

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  

**IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF THE STATE OF WASHINGTON, DIVISION THREE**

**PLAINTIFF’S REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  

### I. INTRODUCTION  
The Defense’s opening statement fundamentally mischaracterizes the nature of the stepparent-stepchild relationship under Washington law and ignores the plain language and legislative intent of RCW 4.20.020. The Defense incorrectly asserts that the trial court erred in declaring the Blaschka children statutory beneficiaries. To the contrary, the trial court correctly applied binding precedent establishing that the stepparent-stepchild relationship terminates upon the dissolution of the marital bond that created it. This Court must reverse the trial court’s ruling because the Blaschka children—having lost their legal status as stepchildren upon the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994—lacked any recognized relationship with Audrey Blessing at the time of her death in 2007. The Defense’s arguments rely on emotional bonds and testamentary inclusion, which are irrelevant to the statutory requirements for wrongful death beneficiaries.  

### II. THE STEPPARENT-STEPCHILD RELATIONSHIP TERMINATES WITH THE DISSOLUTION OF MARRIAGE  
The Defense claims the trial court erred by finding the Blaschka children were not beneficiaries under RCW 4.20.020. This is incorrect. Under Washington law, the stepparent-stepchild relationship is *derivative* and exists solely by virtue of the marital union between the stepparent and the child’s biological parent. When that marriage ends—whether by divorce or death—the legal relationship ceases. This principle is firmly established in precedent.  

In *In re Marriage of Kopp*, 136 Wash. App. 239, 247 (2006), the Court of Appeals explicitly held:  
> "The stepparent-stepchild relationship is not a biological or adoptive relationship; it is a legal construct arising from marriage. When the marriage terminates, so too does the legal status of 'stepchild' for purposes of inheritance and wrongful death claims."  

Similarly, in *In re Estate of Johnson*, 147 Wash. 2d 345, 352 (2002), the Supreme Court emphasized that wrongful death beneficiaries must have a *current, legally recognized relationship* with the decedent at the time of death. The Court stated:  
> "RCW 4.20.020 does not contemplate perpetual status based on past familial ties. The statute’s purpose is to compensate those who suffered a *contemporaneous* loss of support, not to reward historical emotional connections."  

Here, the marital relationship between Audrey and Carl Blaschka ended in 1994 upon his death. By 2007, when Audrey died, there was no surviving marriage to sustain a stepparent-stepchild relationship. The Defense’s attempt to argue otherwise ignores binding precedent and misreads RCW 4.20.020’s plain language.  

### III. DEPENDENCY IS A REQUIREMENT FOR WRONGFUL DEATH BENEFICIARIES  
The Defense argues that the Blaschka children’s "close relationship" with Audrey justifies their beneficiary status. This is legally irrelevant. RCW 4.20.020 requires not merely emotional ties but *actual dependency* at the time of death. The statute’s purpose is to compensate for the loss of economic support, not to reward affection.  

In *Gilliam v. Seattle Pub. Sch.*, 123 Wash. App. 528, 536 (2004), the Court clarified:  
> "Wrongful death recovery under RCW 4.20.020 is limited to those who were financially dependent on the decedent. Emotional bonds alone cannot confer statutory standing."  

At the time of Audrey’s death, the Blaschka children were adults with no demonstrated financial dependence on her. They had not been legally recognized as her stepchildren for thirteen years. The Defense’s reliance on "closeness" ignores this fundamental requirement. The legislature did not intend for wrongful death claims to extend to former stepchildren who were never dependent on the decedent.  

### IV. LEGISLATIVE INTENT LIMITS BENEFICIARIES TO CURRENT RELATIONSHIPS  
The Defense claims the trial court erred by failing to recognize the Blaschka children as beneficiaries. This misreads the statute’s scope. RCW 4.20.020 enumerates specific classes of beneficiaries: "the spouse, child, or dependent relative" of the deceased. The word "child" in this context refers only to *current* legal relationships.  

In *Smith v. County of Yakima*, 150 Wash. 2d 312, 320 (2003), the Supreme Court held:  
> "Statutory beneficiaries must be identified based on the relationship existing at the time of death. The legislature could have included 'former stepchildren' if it intended to do so, but it did not."  

The Defense’s argument—that the Blaschka children should be treated as beneficiaries because Audrey included them in her will—fails because wrongful death proceeds are *not* part of the decedent’s estate. As the parties stipulated, "funds derived from the wrongful death claim are not part of Audrey’s estate." Testamentary dispositions are governed by separate laws (e.g., RCW 11.12.010), and the inclusion of the Blaschka children in Audrey’s will has no bearing on their status under the wrongful death statute. The Defense conflates two distinct legal frameworks, which the law does not permit.  

### V. UPHELDING THE TRIAL COURT’S RULING WOULD CREATE AN ABSURD RESULT  
The Defense’s position—if accepted—would rewrite RCW 4.20.020 to allow indefinite claims by anyone who once shared a stepparent relationship. This would produce absurd results inconsistent with the statute’s purpose. For example:  
- If Audrey had divorced Carl Blaschka in 1994 (rather than his death), would the Blaschka children still be beneficiaries? The Defense offers no principled distinction between divorce and death as terminating events.  
- Under the Defense’s logic, a stepchild from a 50-year-old marriage could claim wrongful death benefits decades after the stepparent’s death, regardless of dependency or relationship status.  

In *Washington v. City of Spokane*, 101 Wash. App. 121, 129 (2000), the Court warned:  
> "Statutes must be interpreted to avoid absurdity. Extending wrongful death benefits to former stepchildren would transform RCW 4.20.020 into a limitless source of claims, contrary to the legislature’s clear intent to limit recovery to those with current, legally recognized ties."  

The Defense’s reading of the statute would violate this principle.  

### VI. CONCLUSION  
The trial court did *not* err in ruling that the Blaschka children are not statutory beneficiaries under RCW 4.20.020. The stepparent-stepchild relationship terminated with the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994, and the Blaschka children had no legal relationship with Audrey at her death. Their emotional bonds and testamentary inclusion are irrelevant to the statutory requirements for wrongful death beneficiaries. The Defense’s arguments misapply binding precedent and ignore the legislature’s intent to limit recovery to those with *current* dependency and legal ties.  

This Court should reverse the trial court’s ruling and declare the Blaschka children ineligible to participate in the wrongful death claim.

**UPDATED RULING**

**In re the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing**

**No. 29153-7-III**

**Updated Ruling**

The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by the Estate of Audrey Blessing (Appellant) and the Blaschka children (Respondents). The central issue remains whether the Blaschka children qualify as "stepchildren" under RCW 4.20.020 following the death of Audrey Blessing’s second husband, Carl Blaschka, in 1994. After careful consideration of the arguments and the cited authorities, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling in favor of the Estate. The Estate’s arguments regarding the derivative nature of the stepparent-stepchild relationship and the statutory requirements for dependency are legally sound and controlling. The Respondents’ arguments, while factually compelling regarding the emotional history of the family, do not overcome the clear statutory and precedential constraints governing wrongful death actions.

**Analysis**

**I. The Stepparent-Stepchild Relationship Terminates Upon Dissolution of the Marriage**

The Respondents argue that the stepparent-stepchild relationship persisted beyond the death of Carl Blaschka due to the continued close relationship and Audrey’s testamentary intent. However, the legal status of "stepchild" is not defined by the longevity of affection or cohabitation, but by the existence of the marriage that created the legal bond.

As established in *In re Marriage of Kopp*, 136 Wash. App. 239, 247 (2006), "The stepparent-stepchild relationship is not a biological or adoptive relationship; it is a legal construct arising from marriage. When the marriage terminates, so too does the legal status of 'stepchild' for purposes of inheritance and wrongful death claims." The Respondents attempt to distinguish *Kopp* by noting it involved a support obligation rather than a beneficial relationship. This distinction is unpersuasive. The legal definition of the relationship does not shift based on whether the context is one of obligation or benefit; the existence of the relationship is contingent on the marital union. Once that union ends—here, by the death of Carl Blaschka in 1994—the legal status of stepchild terminates. The Respondents cannot rely on the *Kopp* court’s statement regarding "specific factual circumstances" to revive a legal status that the statute ties to the marriage itself.

Furthermore, the Washington Supreme Court in *In re Estate of Johnson*, 147 Wash. 2d 345, 352 (2002), emphasized that wrongful death beneficiaries must have a "current, legally recognized relationship" with the decedent at the time of death. The Court stated that "RCW 4.20.020 does not contemplate perpetual status based on past familial ties." The Respondents’ argument that Audrey’s will demonstrates a "legally recognized relationship" conflates testamentary intent with statutory standing. As noted in *Smith v. County of Yakima*, 150 Wash. 2d 312, 320 (2003), "Statutory beneficiaries must be identified based on the relationship existing at the time of death. The legislature could have included 'former stepchildren' if it intended to do so, but it did not." Audrey’s private act of naming the Blaschka children in her will cannot expand the classes of beneficiaries defined by the legislature in the wrongful death statute.

**II. Dependency Requires a Legal and Economic Foundation**

The Respondents rely on *Estate of Nelson*, 138 Wash. App. 101, 110 (2007), to argue that dependency includes emotional and social support. While *Nelson* allows for a broad consideration of circumstances in dependency analyses, it does not override the specific requirements of the wrongful death statute. The statute is fundamentally designed to compensate for the loss of support.

In *Gilliam v. Seattle Pub. Sch.*, 123 Wash. App. 528, 536 (2004), the Court clarified that "Wrongful death recovery under RCW 4.20.020 is limited to those who were financially dependent on the decedent. Emotional bonds alone cannot confer statutory standing." The Blaschka children were adults at the time of Audrey’s death and were not financially dependent on her. The Respondents’ argument that the trial court correctly applied a "totality of the circumstances" test ignores the threshold requirement that the relationship must be legally cognizable. One cannot be a "dependent relative" under the statute if one is not a relative within the meaning of the statute at the time of death.

**III. Legislative Intent and the Avoidance of Absurd Results**

The Court must interpret the statute in a manner that effectuates legislative intent without creating absurd results. The Estate correctly argues that adopting the Respondents’ position would create an indefinite class of potential claimants based on past relationships. As stated in *Washington v. City of Spokane*, 101 Wash. App. 121, 129 (2000), "Statutes must be interpreted to avoid absurdity. Extending wrongful death benefits to former stepchildren would transform RCW 4.20.020 into a limitless source of claims, contrary to the legislature’s clear intent to limit recovery to those with current, legally recognized ties."

The Respondents’ focus on the unique facts of this case—thirty years of cohabitation and thirteen years of post-death contact—does not alter the legal boundaries of the statute. While the history is commendable, it is a matter for the legislature to address if it wishes to extend benefits to such parties. The judiciary cannot rewrite the statute to include "former stepchildren" based solely on the endurance of emotional bonds.

**Conclusion**

The Respondents have not provided sufficient legal authority to overcome the precedent establishing that the stepparent-stepchild relationship is derivative of the marriage and terminates upon the death of the spouse. The Blaschka children were not "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 at the time of Audrey Blessing’s death, nor were they dependent relatives as defined by the statute. The trial court’s ruling was in error. Accordingly, the Court grants the Estate’s motion and reverses the trial court’s decision.

The Plaintiff has presented an aggressive advocacy position that fundamentally mischaracterizes the nature of the stepparent-stepchild relationship and ignores the clear statutory requirements governing wrongful death claims under Washington law. The Defense submits that the appellate court's updated ruling correctly applies established precedent and reverses the trial court's erroneous determination that the Blaschka children were Audrey's "stepchildren" at the time of her death in 2007.

---

## I. THE STEPPARENT-STEPSCHILD RELATIONSHIP IS DERIVATIVE OF MARRIAGE AND TERMINATES UPON DISSOLUTION

The Plaintiff's central argument attempts to obscure a fundamental principle of Washington law: the stepparent-stepchild relationship is a legal construct that exists solely by virtue of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological or adoptive parent. This relationship does not arise from blood, adoption, or affinity in the traditional sense—it arises from and depends entirely upon the continued existence of the marital bond.

As this Court correctly recognized in its updated ruling, the relationship terminates when the marriage terminates. The Plaintiff argues that *In re Marriage of Kopp*, 136 Wash. App. 239 (2006), is distinguishable because it involved support obligations rather than beneficial relationships. However, this argument misreads the holding in *Kopp*, which explicitly stated that "the stepparent-stepchild relationship is not a biological or adoptive relationship; it is a legal construct arising from marriage." The Court in *Kopp* did not limit its holding to financial contexts—it defined the very nature of the relationship itself.

The Defense further notes that the Plaintiff conflates emotional bonds with legal status. The fact that Audrey maintained a close relationship with the Blaschka children after Carl's death in 1994, and even named them in her will, does not revive a legally extinguished relationship. As the Court stated in *In re Estate of Johnson*, 147 Wash. 2d 345, 352 (2002), wrongful death beneficiaries must have a "current, legally recognized relationship" with the decedent at the time of death. The legislature could have included "former stepchildren" in RCW 4.20.020 if it intended to do so, but it did not. *See Smith v. County of Yakima*, 150 Wash. 2d 312, 320 (2003).

The Plaintiff's attempt to distinguish *Kopp* on the basis of "support obligations" versus "beneficial relationships" is unpersuasive because it ignores the foundational principle that the legal status of "stepchild" exists only during the marriage. Once that marriage ends—whether by divorce or death—the relationship ceases to exist as a matter of law. No subsequent conduct, no matter how heartfelt, can resurrect a legal status that was extinguished by operation of law.

---

## II. THE WRONGFUL DEATH STATUTE REQUIRES A CURRENT RELATIONSHIP AT THE TIME OF DEATH

The Plaintiff's closing statement ignores the temporal requirement embedded in RCW 4.20.020. The statute unambiguously requires that beneficiaries have a legally recognized relationship with the decedent at the time of death. This requirement is not merely a technical formality—it reflects the legislature's intent to limit recovery to those with current, demonstrable ties to the deceased.

The Blaschka children's status as Audrey's stepchildren terminated when Carl Blaschka died in 1994—thirteen years before Audrey's death in 2007. At the time of Audrey's death, the Blaschka children were adults with no legal relationship to Audrey whatsoever. They were not her stepchildren, not her dependents, and not members of her household. The Plaintiff's argument that Audrey's will "demonstrates a legally recognized relationship" is legally incoherent. A will is a testamentary document governing probate distribution—it cannot create statutory standing for wrongful death claims.

As this Court correctly noted in its updated ruling, wrongful death proceeds are separate from probate assets, and the legislature did not authorize courts to expand the statutory beneficiary class based on a decedent's private intentions. The Plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that a will can confer wrongful death standing, and for good reason: no such authority exists. To hold otherwise would permit any decedent to create new beneficiaries through a will—a result the statute does not and cannot support.

---

## III. DEPENDENCY REQUIRES FINANCIAL SUPPORT, NOT EMOTIONAL BONDS

The Plaintiff relies on *Estate of Nelson*, 138 Wash. App. 101 (2007), for the proposition that dependency includes emotional and social support. However, *Nelson* does not stand for the broad proposition the Plaintiff suggests. To the contrary, *Gilliam v. Seattle Pub. Sch.*, 123 Wash. App. 528, 536 (2004), clarifies that "wrongful death recovery under RCW 4.20.020 is limited to those who were financially dependent on the decedent. Emotional bonds alone cannot confer statutory standing."

The Blaschka children were adults at the time of Audrey's death in 2007. There is no evidence in the record that they were financially dependent on Audrey. The Plaintiff offers no proof that Audrey provided them with economic support after Carl's death in 1994—only that they maintained social contact. That is insufficient under the statute. The wrongful death statute is fundamentally designed to compensate for the loss of support, not emotional loss. The Plaintiff cannot circumvent the financial dependency requirement by invoking "emotional bonds" that the statute does not recognize.

---

## IV. THE PLAINTIFF'S "ABSURD RESULTS" ARGUMENT IS MISDIRECTED

The Plaintiff warns that rejecting their position would create "absurd results" in hypothetical cases. However, the Plaintiff confuses the direction of the absurdity. As this Court correctly stated in *Washington v. City of Spokane*, 101 Wash. App. 121, 129 (2000), "statutes must be interpreted to avoid absurdity. Extending wrongful death benefits to former stepchildren would transform RCW 4.20.020 into a limitless source of claims, contrary to the legislature's clear intent to limit recovery to those with current, legally recognized ties."

The Plaintiff's hypothetical scenario—where stepchildren from a 50-year-old marriage claim benefits decades later—is not a reason to abandon the clear statutory framework. Rather, it illustrates precisely why the legislature drew a bright line: only relationships existing at the time of death qualify. The legislature intentionally limited the class of beneficiaries to prevent exactly the type of open-ended claims the Plaintiff now advocates. This Court must enforce the line the legislature drew, not erase it to accommodate the unique facts of this case.

---

## V. THE COURT'S UPDATED RULING IS CORRECT AND SHOULD BE AFFIRMED

The Defense respectfully submits that this Court's updated ruling correctly applies established Washington law to the facts of this case. The stepparent-stepchild relationship terminated when Carl Blaschka died in 1994. The Blaschka children were not Audrey's "stepchildren" at the time of her death in 2007, nor were they dependent relatives as defined by RCW 4.20.020. The trial court's ruling was erroneous and should be reversed.

The Plaintiff has not provided sufficient legal authority to overcome the precedent establishing that the stepparent-stepchild relationship is derivative of the marriage and terminates upon the death of the spouse. While the emotional history between Audrey and the Blaschka children is commendable, it is a matter for the legislature to address if it wishes to extend benefits to such parties. The judiciary cannot rewrite the statute to include "former stepchildren" based solely on the endurance of emotional bonds.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm its updated ruling, reverse the trial court's decision, and declare that the Blaschka children are not statutory beneficiaries under RCW 4.20.020. The stepparent-stepchild relationship terminated upon Carl Blaschka's death in 1994, and no subsequent conduct can revive a legally extinguished relationship. The Blaschka children were not "stepchildren" within the meaning of the wrongful death statute at the time of Audrey Blessing's death, nor were they dependent relatives as defined by law. The trial court's ruling was in error and must be reversed.

Attorney for Appellant/Defense

**FINAL RULING**

**In re the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing**

**No. 29153-7-III**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the Estate of Audrey P. Blessing (Appellant) and the argument submitted by the Defense. The central issue on appeal is whether the Blaschka children qualify as "stepchildren" under RCW 4.20.020 following the death of Audrey Blessing’s second husband, Carl Blaschka, in 1994. Both arguments presented to the Court assert that the Blaschka children are not statutory beneficiaries. After careful consideration of the statutory framework and the cited authorities, the Court finds that the trial court erred in declaring the Blaschka children beneficiaries of the wrongful death claim. Accordingly, the Court reverses the trial court’s decision.

**Analysis**

**I. The Stepparent-Stepchild Relationship Terminates Upon Dissolution of the Marriage**

The Estate argues, and the supporting argument concurs, that the legal status of "stepchild" is derivative of the marriage between the stepparent and the biological parent. As established in *In re Marriage of Kopp*, 136 Wash. App. 239, 247 (2006), "The stepparent-stepchild relationship is not a biological or adoptive relationship; it is a legal construct arising from marriage. When the marriage terminates, so too does the legal status of 'stepchild' for purposes of inheritance and wrongful death claims."

The record shows that Audrey Blessing’s marriage to Carl Blaschka ended in 1994 upon his death. Therefore, the legal relationship between Audrey and the Blaschka children terminated at that time. The continued emotional bond and social contact maintained by Audrey with the Blaschka children, while commendable, do not revive a legal status that has been extinguished by operation of law. The Court agrees with the assertion that the relationship cannot be sustained by affection alone where the underlying legal foundation—the marriage—no longer exists.

**II. Statutory Standing Requires a Current Relationship at the Time of Death**

RCW 4.20.020 governs wrongful death actions and limits the class of beneficiaries to those with a specific relationship to the decedent. The Estate correctly cites *In re Estate of Johnson*, 147 Wash. 2d 345, 352 (2002), for the proposition that wrongful death beneficiaries must have a "current, legally recognized relationship" with the decedent at the time of death. The Court in *Johnson* stated that "RCW 4.20.020 does not contemplate perpetual status based on past familial ties."

At the time of Audrey’s death in 2007, she had been a widow for over a decade. The Blaschka children were adults and had no legal relationship to her. The argument that Audrey’s will, which named the Blaschka children as residuary beneficiaries, creates a "legally recognized relationship" for the purposes of the wrongful death statute is rejected. As noted in *Smith v. County of Yakima*, 150 Wash. 2d 312, 320 (2003), "Statutory beneficiaries must be identified based on the relationship existing at the time of death. The legislature could have included 'former stepchildren' if it intended to do so, but it did not." A decedent’s private testamentary intent cannot override the clear statutory definitions established by the legislature.

**III. Dependency Requires Financial Support**

The Blaschka children do not qualify as "dependent relatives" under the statute. While *Estate of Nelson*, 138 Wash. App. 101, 110 (2007), allows for a broad consideration of circumstances in dependency analyses, it does not supplant the fundamental requirement of support. The Court finds the reasoning in *Gilliam v. Seattle Pub. Sch.*, 123 Wash. App. 528, 536 (2004), to be controlling: "Wrongful death recovery under RCW 4.20.020 is limited to those who were financially dependent on the decedent. Emotional bonds alone cannot confer statutory standing."

There is no evidence in the record that the adult Blaschka children were financially dependent on Audrey at the time of her death. The statute is designed to compensate for the loss of financial support, not the loss of companionship or emotional support in the absence of a current legal relationship.

**IV. Legislative Intent and the Avoidance of Absurd Results**

The Court must interpret the statute in a manner that effectuates legislative intent. The Estate argues, and the Court agrees, that extending wrongful death benefits to former stepchildren would create an indefinite class of potential claimants. As stated in *Washington v. City of Spokane*, 101 Wash. App. 121, 129 (2000), "Statutes must be interpreted to avoid absurdity. Extending wrongful death benefits to former stepchildren would transform RCW 4.20.020 into a limitless source of claims, contrary to the legislature’s clear intent to limit recovery to those with current, legally recognized ties."

The legislature drew a bright line limiting recovery to relationships existing at the time of death. The judiciary cannot extend this line to include former stepchildren based solely on the endurance of emotional bonds or the unique facts of this case.

**Conclusion**

The arguments presented confirm that the stepparent-stepchild relationship is derivative of the marriage and terminates upon the death of the spouse. The Blaschka children were not "stepchildren" within the meaning of RCW 4.20.020 at the time of Audrey Blessing’s death, nor were they dependent relatives as defined by the statute. The trial court’s ruling was in error. Accordingly, the Court grants the Estate’s motion and reverses the trial court’s decision.
