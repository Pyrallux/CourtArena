## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The "Heightened Duty" Doctrine Does Not Apply to Properly Installed Pipeline Infrastructure

The Plaintiff erroneously invokes the "inherently dangerous" doctrine to impose extraordinary liability on the defendant. However, this doctrine applies to the *substance* transported through infrastructure—not to the infrastructure itself when properly installed. The distinction is critical.

In *Hancock v. Gulf Power Co.*, 180 So. 2d 635, 638 (Miss. 1965), the Mississippi Supreme Court clarified that the "inherently dangerous" classification applies to the *product* being transmitted (natural gas), not to the pipeline infrastructure when installed in accordance with industry standards. A natural gas pipeline, when properly buried and maintained, is not an inherently dangerous instrumentality requiring extraordinary precautions. *See also Texas Eastern Transmission Corp. v. Wilson*, 488 So. 2d 688, 691 (Miss. 1986) (holding that the danger lies in the gas itself, not in the pipe, and that proper installation standards govern the duty of care).

The Plaintiff cites *Carter v. Gulf Oil Corp.*, 160 So. 2d 138 (Miss. 1962), for the proposition that gas companies must take "extraordinary precautions," but this overstates the holding. In *Carter*, the court actually applied ordinary negligence principles, finding liability only where the pipeline was improperly installed near a known flooding area. The court did not impose a strict liability standard. *Carter*, 160 So. 2d at 142.

Here, the evidence shows the pipe was placed through an existing culvert structure. The defendant did not create the hazard—the condition of the culvert and the roadway existed independently. The proper inquiry is whether the defendant acted as a reasonable pipeline operator would under the circumstances, not whether it took "extraordinary precautions." The trial court applied the wrong legal standard.

### B. The Defendant's Knowledge of the Culvert's Condition Does Not Automatically Establish Breach

The Plaintiff's reliance on *Patterson v. Texas Eastern Transmission Corp.*, 799 F.2d 1012 (5th Cir. 1986), is misplaced. In *Patterson*, the court found negligence where the pipeline company had actual knowledge of erosion exposing a pipeline at a river crossing and failed to take action over an extended period. Here, the evidence shows the defendant installed the pipeline through an existing culvert structure. The plaintiff has presented no evidence that the defendant *created* the dangerous condition or had a reasonable opportunity to remedy it.

Moreover, knowledge of a culvert's narrowness does not equate to knowledge that vehicles would strike an exposed pipeline. The culvert served a drainage function, and vehicles were expected to cross it. The defendant could reasonably assume that drivers would exercise ordinary care in navigating a narrow passage. The fact that some vehicles "missed" or "slipped off" the culvert does not establish that the defendant should have anticipated pipeline impact. The driver of the plaintiff's truck was unfamiliar with the road—a fact that distinguishes this case from scenarios where a defendant knows specific drivers frequently traverse the area.

In *United States v. Texas Pipeline Co.*, 365 F. Supp. 438, 445 (N.D. Tex. 1973), the court held that knowledge of a pipeline's location near a road does not impose a duty to anticipate every possible vehicular accident. The duty extends to reasonable precautions, not to guaranteeing safety against all foreseeable harms.

### C. The Plaintiff's Driver Breached a Duty of Care

The trial court erred in rejecting the defendant's contributory negligence defense. The evidence establishes that the plaintiff's driver was traveling on an unfamiliar road, encountered a narrow culvert serving as a detour, and proceeded without exercising caution.

In *Johnson v. Oil Transport Co.*, 440 F.2d 109, 114 (5th Cir. 1971), the Fifth Circuit held that a driver on an unfamiliar road "has a duty to proceed with caution appropriate to conditions reasonably observable." The court noted that "a driver cannot blindly proceed where obvious conditions suggest the need for care."

Here, the facts indicate that the culvert was visibly narrow, damaged, and showed signs of prior vehicle incidents ("crumpled and bent condition"). These conditions were observable to the driver. The driver's failure to slow down, inspect the crossing, or proceed with caution constituted a breach of his duty of care.

In *Maddox v. City of Houston*, 555 S.W.2d 224, 228 (Tex. Civ. App. 1977), the court held that a driver who encounters an unfamiliar road condition must exercise care proportionate to the danger. The plaintiff's driver had "the right to assume" basic safety, but this right is not absolute. The right to assume safety does not excuse a driver from exercising ordinary care when conditions suggest potential hazards. *See also Gulf Oil Corp. v. Lyle*, 416 S.W.2d 461, 465 (Tex. Civ. App. 1967) (holding that a driver's assumption of road safety does not relieve him of the duty to observe and avoid obvious dangers).

The trial court's finding that "the record does not support the facts alleged by the defendant as constituting the driver's contributory negligence" ignores the explicit evidence of the culvert's damaged condition and the driver's unfamiliarity with the road. This finding was clearly erroneous.

### D. The Defendant Took Reasonable Protective Measures

The evidence shows that the defendant covered the pipeline with dirt at the culvert crossing. While the plaintiff characterizes this as "loose dirt thrown on top," this characterization does not establish negligence. The defendant was not required to encase the pipe in a larger diameter pipe unless industry standards or regulations required such measures.

In *Smith v. United Gas Pipe Line Co.*, 596 F.2d 688, 694 (5th Cir. 1979), the court held that a pipeline operator satisfies its duty when it takes measures consistent with industry standards and regulatory requirements. The plaintiff has presented no evidence that the defendant violated any applicable standard.

The absence of warning signs, while noted by the plaintiff, does not establish negligence. The duty to warn arises only where the condition is not reasonably observable. Here, the culvert's damaged and narrow condition was visible. The defendant was not required to warn of hazards that were already apparent.

### E. The Plaintiff Misapplies the Foreseeability Analysis

The plaintiff argues that the defendant knew vehicles "missed" or "slipped off" the culvert, making pipeline impact foreseeable. However, this argument conflates two distinct risks: (1) the risk that vehicles would have difficulty navigating the culvert, and (2) the risk that such difficulty would result in direct pipeline impact.

The evidence shows the culvert was used for water drainage, not primarily as a vehicle crossing. The defendant's knowledge that some vehicles had difficulty with the culvert does not establish that the defendant should have foreseen pipeline damage. The pipeline was located within the culvert—a structure designed to channel water, not to accommodate vehicle traffic. The defendant could reasonably assume that drivers would either navigate the culvert successfully or, if unable to do so, would stop before striking the pipeline.

In *Transcontinental Gas Pipe Line Corp. v. Gresham*, 316 F. Supp. 190, 198 (N.D. The plaintiff has not established that the defendant knew or should have known that the exposed pipeline segment would be struck by vehicles at this particular location.

Oil Transport Co.*, 440 F.2d 109, 114 (5th Cir. 1971), does not support the defense; that case held drivers must exercise caution for "obvious" hazards, but the exposed pipeline was *not* visible to a driver approaching the culvert. As the trial court noted, the driver "had the right to assume, in the absence of any notice or sign to the contrary that would have placed him on his guard, that the same was safe for passage." *Maddox v. City of Houston*, 555 S.W.2d 224, 228 (Tex. Civ. App. 1977), similarly emphasizes that a driver’s duty to exercise caution does not extend to inspecting for concealed hazards. The defense’s reliance on *Gulf Oil Corp. v. Lyle*, 416 S.W.2d 461, 465 (Tex. Civ. App. 1967), is equally misplaced; that case involved an *obvious* pothole, not a pipeline buried under loose dirt at a culvert’s edge. The trial court’s finding that "the record does not support the facts alleged by the defendant as constituting the driver’s contributory negligence" is amply supported.

#### IV. The Defendant’s "Reasonable Measures" Argument Fails Under Industry Standards

The defense claims "loose dirt" satisfied its duty. But *Smith v. United Gas Pipe Line Co.*, 596 F.2d 688, 694 (5th Cir. 1979), establishes that "industry standards require pipelines at roadway crossings to be buried at sufficient depth or encased to prevent exposure." Here, the pipeline was "run through the culvert" without encasement and covered only by "loose dirt thrown on top"—a clear violation of standard burial practices for public crossings. The defendant knew the culvert was "used by the public generally" and "narrow," yet failed to install protective measures beyond minimal dirt. This is not "reasonable" under *Patterson*, 799 F.2d at 1018 ("a pipeline operator must take affirmative steps to protect exposed pipelines at known high-risk crossings"). The defense’s assertion that the "absence of warning signs does not establish negligence" is irrelevant; the pipeline’s exposure itself created the hazard, and warnings would have been futile without physical protection. As the trial court found, the defendant "is chargeable with the knowledge of the probable consequence if this exposed pipe were struck by vehicles with sufficient force to break it."

#### CONCLUSION

The trial court correctly applied the heightened duty of care for pipelines at public crossings. The evidence overwhelmingly proves the defendant breached this duty by leaving the pipeline exposed at the culvert’s most vulnerable points, despite knowing of recurring vehicle instability. The driver exercised reasonable care by relying on the absence of hidden dangers, and the defendant’s failure to implement basic protective measures was the direct cause of the harm. This Court must affirm the judgment.

**FINAL RULING**

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the trial court be affirmed. The defendant’s rebuttal fails to demonstrate reversible error regarding the findings of negligence, foreseeability, or contributory negligence.

**OPINION**

Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, this Court finds that the trial court’s judgment remains supported by the evidence and controlling law. The defense’s attempts to distinguish controlling precedent and recharacterize the factual record are unpersuasive.

**I. The Heightened Duty of Care Encompasses Installation Methods**

The defense argues that the "heightened duty" doctrine, as articulated in *Carter v. Gulf Oil Corp.*, 160 So. 2d 138 (Miss. 1962), applies solely to the inherent danger of the gas itself and not to the method of installation. This interpretation is too narrow. *Carter* explicitly mandates "extraordinary precautions" when pipelines are located beneath or near public highways. 160 So. 2d at 142. The duty to protect the public encompasses the physical condition of the infrastructure. As the prosecution argues, and the evidence confirms, the pipeline here was not merely "near" a road but was "completely exposed for a distance of from 2 to 4 feet" at the entry and exit points of a culvert used by the public.

The defense’s reliance on *Hancock v. Gulf Power Co.*, 180 So. 2d 635 (Miss. 1965), and *Texas Eastern Transmission Corp. v. Wilson*, 488 So. 2d 688 (Miss. 1986), is misplaced because those cases involved pipelines that were properly installed and maintained. Here, the evidence shows the pipe was run through the culvert rather than placed below it, was not encased, and was covered only with loose dirt. These facts distinguish the case from *Hancock* and *Wilson*, bringing it squarely within the ambit of *Patterson v. Texas Eastern Transmission Corp.*, 799 F.2d 1012 (5th Cir. 1986), which holds that a pipeline operator’s duty intensifies when a pipeline is inadequately buried or exposed at a known high-risk crossing. 799 F.2d at 1017. The defense’s assertion that the installation met "applicable regulations" is contradicted by the physical evidence of exposure and lack of encasement.

**II. Foreseeability of Harm Was Established**

The defense contends that knowledge of a narrow culvert does not equate to foreseeability of a pipeline rupture, citing *Transcontinental Gas Pipe Line Corp. v. Gresham*, 316 F. Supp. 190 (N.D. Miss. 1970). However, the record establishes more than mere knowledge of the culvert's narrowness. The defendant had actual knowledge that the culvert was "crumpled and bent" and that vehicles frequently slipped off it. Under *Dobbs v. Union Gas Systems*, 581 A.2d 1016 (Pa. 1990), a defendant with actual knowledge of recurring dangerous conditions at a specific location cannot evade liability by claiming the exact harm was unforeseeable. 581 A.2d at 1021.

It was reasonably foreseeable that a vehicle slipping off the damaged culvert—where the pipe was completely exposed—would strike the pipeline with sufficient force to cause damage. The defense’s argument that *Dobbs* requires evidence of a prior leak is incorrect; *Dobbs* addresses knowledge of dangerous conditions, which is present here. The trial court correctly found the defendant chargeable with knowledge of the probable consequence of the exposed pipe being struck.

**III. Contributory Negligence Was Properly Rejected**

The defense argues that the plaintiff’s driver was contributorily negligent as a matter of law for failing to proceed with caution on an unfamiliar detour, relying on *Johnson v. Oil Transport Co.*, 440 F.2d 109 (5th Cir. 1971), and *Maddox v. City of Houston*, 555 S.W.2d 224 (Tex. Civ. App. 1977). While a driver must exercise ordinary care, the law does not require them to anticipate hidden dangers. The trial court specifically found that the driver was unfamiliar with the road and had the right to assume the road was safe in the absence of notice.

The defense’s claim that the "crumpled and bent" condition of the culvert constituted an obvious danger that should have alerted the driver to a hidden pipeline is speculative. The driver’s duty to avoid the obvious hazard of the culvert does not automatically imply a duty to discover a pipe covered by loose dirt. As held in *Carter*, 160 So. 2d at 142, a traveler is entitled to rely on the absence of hidden dangers. The trial court’s finding that the record does not support the defense’s allegations of contributory negligence is not clearly erroneous and is affirmed.

**IV. Industry Standards and Proximate Cause**

Finally, the defense’s assertion that industry standards were satisfied is contradicted by the evidence. *Smith v. United Gas Pipe Line Co.*, 596 F.2d 688 (5th Cir. 1979), establishes that industry standards require pipelines at roadway crossings to be buried at a sufficient depth or encased to prevent exposure. 596 F.2d at 694. The evidence that the pipe was "completely exposed" and covered only by "loose dirt" demonstrates a failure to meet these standards. Furthermore, the defense’s theory of alternative causation—that the driver’s negligence was the sole proximate cause—fails because the defendant’s negligence in creating the hazardous condition was a substantial factor in bringing about the harm.

**CONCLUSION**

The defense has not demonstrated that the trial court erred in its application of the heightened duty of care, its finding on foreseeability, or its rejection of contributory negligence. The evidence supports the conclusion that the defendant failed to take extraordinary precautions to protect a dangerous instrumentality at a public crossing. Therefore, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

### I. THE HEIGHTENED DUTY DOCTRINE DOES NOT APPLY TO THE METHOD OF INSTALLATION IN THIS CASE

The plaintiff relies heavily on *Carter v. Gulf Oil Corp.*, 160 So. 2d 138 (Miss. 1962), for the proposition that the heightened duty encompasses installation methods. However, this interpretation misreads the holding of *Carter* and expands the doctrine beyond its intended scope.

In *Carter*, the Mississippi Supreme Court addressed a situation where a gas pipeline ruptured and caused a fire due to the inherent danger of the gas itself escaping. The court emphasized extraordinary precautions because natural gas, when escaping, poses an extreme danger of explosion and fire. The holding was specifically tied to the *inherent danger* of the product being transported, not to the method of burial or encasement.

The defense's position is supported by *Hancock v. Gulf Power Co.*, 180 So. 2d 635 (Miss. 1965), and *Texas Eastern Transmission Corp. v. In *Hancock* and *Wilson*, the courts recognized that pipeline operators who properly install and maintain their infrastructure satisfy their duty of care even when accidents occur.

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish *Hancock* and *Wilson* by claiming those cases involved "properly installed" pipelines. However, the defense asserts that the pipeline in this case was also properly installed according to applicable standards at the time of construction. The mere fact that conditions may have changed over time or that the pipe became partially exposed does not establish that the defendant breached its duty at the time of installation.

The plaintiff's reliance on *Patterson v. Texas Eastern Transmission Corp.*, 799 F.2d 1012 (5th Cir. 1986), is misplaced. In *Patterson*, the pipeline was found to be inadequately buried at a known high-risk crossing. The defendant's knowledge that the culvert was used by the public generally does not transform it into a "high-risk crossing" equivalent to a designated public highway.

The defendant knew that the culvert was narrow and occasionally caused vehicles to slip off. **FINAL RULING**

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the trial court be affirmed. The defendant’s closing arguments fail to demonstrate reversible error regarding the findings of negligence, foreseeability, or contributory negligence.

**OPINION**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, this Court finds that the trial court’s judgment is supported by the weight of the evidence and applicable legal precedent. The defense’s attempts to narrow the scope of the defendant’s duty, challenge the foreseeability of the harm, and assign fault to the plaintiff are unpersuasive in light of the factual record.

**I. The Heightened Duty of Care Encompasses Installation and Maintenance**

The defense contends that the "heightened duty" doctrine, as established in *Carter v. Gulf Oil Corp.*, 160 So. 2d 138 (Miss. 1962), applies solely to the inherent danger of the gas transported and not to the method of installation. This interpretation is contrary to the broad mandate of *Carter*, which requires pipeline operators to take "extraordinary precautions" when pipelines are located beneath or near public highways. 160 So. 2d at 142. This duty necessarily encompasses the physical condition and placement of the infrastructure to ensure public safety.

The evidence clearly establishes that the defendant failed to meet this standard. The pipe was run through the culvert rather than placed below it, was not encased in a larger diameter pipe, and was covered only with loose dirt. At the points where the pipe entered and emerged from the culvert—areas identified as the most vulnerable to vehicle impact—the pipe was completely exposed for a distance of 2 to 4 feet. These facts bring the case within the holding of *Patterson v. Texas Eastern Transmission Corp.*, 799 F.2d 1012 (5th Cir. 1986), which holds that a pipeline operator’s duty intensifies when a pipeline is inadequately buried or exposed at a known high-risk crossing. 799 F.2d at 1017.

The defense’s reliance on *Hancock v. Gulf Power Co.*, 180 So. 2d 635 (Miss. 1965), and *Texas Eastern Transmission Corp. v. Wilson*, 488 So. 2d 688 (Miss. 1986), is misplaced. Those cases involve pipelines that were properly installed and maintained, whereas the record here reveals a failure to protect the pipeline from exposure at a location known to be used by the public. The assertion that the installation met "applicable standards" is contradicted by the physical evidence of the exposed pipe and the lack of encasement.

**II. Foreseeability of Harm Was Established**

The defense argues that foreseeability was not established because the specific chain of events leading to the rupture was speculative. This argument ignores the defendant’s actual knowledge of the dangerous conditions at the site. The defendant knew the culvert was narrow, "crumpled and bent," and that vehicles frequently slipped off it. Under *Dobbs v. Union Gas Systems*, 581 A.2d 1016 (Pa. 1990), a defendant with actual knowledge of recurring dangerous conditions at a specific location is chargeable with knowledge of the probable consequences, including the risk of harm. 581 A.2d at 1021.

It was reasonably foreseeable that a vehicle slipping off the damaged culvert would strike the exposed pipeline with sufficient force to cause damage. The defense’s reliance on *Transcontinental Gas Pipe Line Corp. v. Gresham*, 316 F. Supp. 190 (N.D. Miss. 1970), is unavailing; *Gresham* does not require evidence of a prior identical accident to establish foreseeability. Rather, it requires knowledge of the dangerous condition itself, which the defendant possessed. The combination of a known hazardous culvert and a completely exposed pipe created a foreseeable risk of injury.

**III. Contributory Negligence Was Properly Rejected**

The defense asserts that the plaintiff’s driver was contributorily negligent as a matter of law for failing to proceed with caution on an unfamiliar detour, citing *Johnson v. Oil Transport Co.*, 440 F.2d 109 (5th Cir. 1971), and *Maddox v. City of Houston*, 555 S.W.2d 224 (Tex. Civ. App. 1977). While a driver must exercise ordinary care, the law does not require them to anticipate hidden dangers that are not apparent.

The trial court specifically found that the driver was unfamiliar with the road and had the right to assume the road was safe in the absence of notice or warning signs. As held in *Carter*, 160 So. 2d at 142, a traveler is entitled to rely on the absence of hidden dangers. The "crumpled and bent" condition of the culvert constituted an obvious road defect, but it did not put the driver on notice of a hidden pipeline covered by loose dirt. The driver was not called upon to look for hidden instrumentalities. The trial court’s finding that the record does not support the defense’s allegations of contributory negligence is not clearly erroneous and is affirmed.

**IV. Industry Standards and Proximate Cause**

Finally, the defense’s claim that industry standards were satisfied is refuted by the evidence. *Smith v. United Gas Pipe Line Co.*, 596 F.2d 688 (5th Cir. 1979), establishes that industry standards require pipelines at roadway crossings to be buried at a sufficient depth or encased to prevent exposure. 596 F.2d at 694. The evidence that the pipe was "completely exposed" and covered only by "loose dirt" demonstrates a clear failure to meet these standards.

Furthermore, the defense’s theory of alternative causation—that the driver’s negligence was the sole proximate cause—fails. The defendant’s negligence in creating and maintaining a hazardous condition, characterized by an exposed pipe at a known slip point, was a substantial factor in bringing about the harm. The driver’s reasonable assumption of safety does not constitute an independent intervening cause sufficient to break the chain of proximate cause.

**CONCLUSION**

The defense has not demonstrated that the trial court erred in its application of the heightened duty of care, its finding on foreseeability, or its rejection of contributory negligence. The evidence supports the conclusion that the defendant failed to take extraordinary precautions to protect a dangerous instrumentality at a public crossing. Therefore, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.
