# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE FOR APPELLEE HEALTH COALITION, INC.

## May it Please the Court:

The defense for Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III's appeal and in support of the trial court's order modifying and extending the temporary injunction. Bradley's arguments fail both legally and factually, and this Court should affirm the trial court's decision.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Bradley attempts to escape his binding contractual obligations through a manufactured narrative of employer misconduct. The record demonstrates that Bradley voluntarily resigned from his position in April 1994, subsequently violated his noncompetition covenant by immediately going to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, and began soliciting the employer's customers. His present claims—that the employer refused to pay commissions and instructed him to commit fraud—are after-the-fact justifications for his breach of contract, not legitimate defenses to enforcement. The trial court properly applied the law in granting injunctive relief, and its order should be affirmed.

## II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY GRANTED THE TEMPORARY INJUNCTION

### A. Standard for Temporary Injunction

Under Florida law, a party seeking a temporary injunction must demonstrate: (1) likelihood of success on the merits; (2) likelihood of irreparable harm absent injunctive relief; (3) balance of hardships favors the movant; and (4) the public interest supports injunctive relief. See *Wheaton v. L.A. Gutzman, Inc.*, 380 So. 2d 78, 80 (Fla. 5th DCA 1980). The trial court correctly applied this standard and found that Health Coalition demonstrated each element.

### B. Bradley Cannot Demonstrate a Likelihood of Success on His Material Breach Defense

Bradley's primary argument—that he is excused from the noncompetition covenant because the employer allegedly committed material breaches—fails for several reasons.

First, the evidence does not support Bradley's claim of material breach. Bradley testified that the employer refused to pay certain commissions, but the employment agreement expressly provided that commissions were payable only upon receipt of payment from customers. The employer introduced evidence that several customers had not yet paid their invoices, thus triggering the contractual condition precedent to commission payment. Under *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 433 (Fla. 1963), a party's failure to perform under a contract is not a breach when performance is expressly conditioned upon an event that has not occurred. Bradley cannot claim a material breach when the employer legitimately withheld commissions per the contract's terms.

Second, even assuming arguendo that a dispute existed regarding commission payment, Bradley's remedy was to sue for the commissions—not to breach the covenant and go work for a competitor. The existence of a good-faith dispute over contract performance does not excuse a party from performing his own obligations. In *Havens v. Boring*, 456 So. 2d 1247 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984), this Court held that an employee's unilateral decision to quit and work for a competitor, even if motivated by dissatisfaction with working conditions, does not excuse the employee from honoring valid noncompetition covenants.

Third, Bradley's claim regarding invoice alteration is unsupported by the evidence and was properly rejected by the trial court. Bradley testified that he was instructed to alter invoices, but the employer categorically denied giving any such instruction. Significantly, Bradley never reported any such instruction to anyone during his employment, never documented the alleged instruction, and never complained about it to any supervisor or in any exit interview. His sudden allegation—raised for the first time two years after termination—lacks credibility and was properly disregarded by the trial court.

### C. The Factual Disputes Do Not Preclude Injunctive Relief

Bradley argues that because factual disputes exist regarding his material breach claims, the trial court could not find a "clear likelihood of success on the merits." This argument misapprehends the applicable law.

A temporary injunction hearing is not a trial on the merits. As this Court held in *Broward County v. G.B.V. International, Ltd.*, 578 So. 2d 345, 347 (Fla. 4th DCA 1991), "the purpose of a temporary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending a full evidentiary hearing on the merits." The movant need not prove success on the merits beyond dispute—only that the evidence presents questions of law and fact making a fair ground for litigation. See *Florida Audubon Society v. Joyce*, 407 So. 2d 1185, 1186 (Fla. 5th DCA 1982).

Here, the trial court properly found that Health Coalition demonstrated a likelihood of success based on: (1) the valid, signed employment agreement containing the noncompetition covenant; (2) Bradley's admission that he went to work for Bio-Med, Inc. selling blood plasma products; and (3) evidence that Bradley solicited the employer's customers. These undisputed facts establish a prima facie case for enforcement. Bradley's defenses—based on alleged non-payment of commissions and invoice instructions—present factual disputes that should be resolved at trial, not at the injunction stage. The trial court correctly declined to resolve these disputed fact questions in Bradley's favor at the preliminary injunction stage, and this Court should not disturb that determination.

Bradley's reliance on *City of Miami Beach v. Berenson*, 476 So. 2d 663 (Fla. 1985), is misplaced. In *Berenson*, the trial court granted a temporary injunction despite clear factual disputes regarding the very existence of a valid contract. Here, by contrast, the existence of the noncompetition covenant is undisputed—Bradley signed the employment agreement. The disputes Bradley raises go to the affirmative defenses of material breach, not to the existence of the contract itself. The trial court properly applied the injunction standard.

## III. THE NONCOMPETITION COVENANT IS ENFORCEABLE

Bradley briefly argues that the noncompetition covenant is overly broad because it prohibits solicitation of "any active, paid up customers" without limitation on time, geography, or scope. This argument fails.

Florida courts consistently enforce noncompetition agreements when they are reasonable and necessary to protect legitimate business interests. See *Radiology Regional Center, P.A. v. Rising*, 510 So. 2d 642, 644 (Fla. 2d DCA 1987). The covenant here protects legitimate interests: (1) confidential customer information; (2) goodwill and customer relationships developed at the employer's expense; and (3) trade secrets regarding blood plasma products. The two-year duration is reasonable, as this Court noted in *Hensley* that one-year covenants are presumptively reasonable.

Moreover, the covenant is limited to "solicitation" of "active, paid up customers"—not to all customers everywhere. This limitation actually narrows the covenant's scope and demonstrates its reasonableness. The cases Bradley would cite for the proposition that such covenants are unenforceable involved covenants that prohibited all competition within broad geographic areas without any limitation on customer solicitation. Here, the covenant is properly tailored.

## IV. BRADLEY'S EQUITABLE DEFENSES FAIL

Bradley argues that the employer "cannot seek equitable relief" because of alleged misconduct. This argument fails.

Initially, the injunction enforces a legal contract—not equitable principles. The noncompetition covenant is a legal obligation, and its enforcement through injunction is a legal remedy. The doctrine Bradley cites—that a party seeking equitable relief must have "clean hands"—applies only when the party seeks purely equitable remedies. See *Holly v. Auld*, 450 So. 2d 217, 218 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984). Here, Health Coalition seeks to enforce a contractual covenant, and the injunction is simply the mechanism for preserving the contractual rights pending trial.

Furthermore, Bradley cannot invoke the clean-hands doctrine when his own hands are far from clean. Bradley signed the employment agreement, voluntarily resigned (or was terminated for cause), and then immediately went to work for a direct competitor in violation of his covenant. His own breach of contract—soliciting customers for a competitor—constitutes the very wrongdoing he accuses the employer of committing. Under the doctrine of unclean hands, a party who has breached his own obligations cannot obtain equitable relief from the consequences of his breach. See *Dolan v. Rotunda*, 489 So. 2d 800, 803 (Fla. 4th DCA 1986).

## V. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY BALANCED THE HARDSHIPS

The trial court also correctly found that the balance of hardships favors Health Coalition. Without injunctive relief, Health Coalition faces the permanent loss of customers and goodwill it spent years developing—a harm that cannot be compensated by monetary damages. Bradley, by contrast, faces only the inconvenience of honoring his contractual obligations for two years, during which he remains free to work in any capacity that does not involve soliciting the employer's customers. The covenant does not prohibit Bradley from working for a competitor; it merely restricts his ability to solicit the employer's customers.

For the foregoing reasons, Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully requests that this Court affirm the trial court's order modifying and extending the temporary injunction. Bradley's arguments are without merit, and the trial court properly applied the law in granting injunctive relief. The noncompetition covenant is valid and enforceable, and Bradley should be held to his contractual obligations pending a full trial on the merits.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on an appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III argues that the trial court erred in granting the injunction because Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. committed a material breach of the employment agreement, thereby excusing Bradley from his obligations under the noncompetition covenant. Specifically, Bradley contends that Health Coalition wrongfully withheld earned commissions and instructed him to engage in fraudulent invoicing. Health Coalition counters that the commissions were not yet due under the contract terms and that the factual disputes regarding these allegations do not preclude injunctive relief. After reviewing the arguments and the record provided, we reverse the temporary injunction.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard for granting a temporary injunction is well established. The party seeking injunctive relief must demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a substantial threat of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the injury to the opposing party; and (4) that the injunction would not disserve the public interest. See *Wheaton v. L.A. Gutzman, Inc.*, 380 So. 2d 78, 80 (Fla. 5th DCA 1980). The trial court’s decision to grant or deny a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion; however, the trial court’s application of the law to the facts is reviewed de novo. See *City of Miami Beach v. Berenson*, 476 So. 2d 663, 665 (Fla. 1985).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Likelihood of Success on the Merits and the Material Breach Defense**

The central issue on appeal is whether Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits given Bradley’s affirmative defense of material breach. It is undisputed that Bradley signed an employment agreement containing a noncompetition covenant. However, Bradley argues that Health Coalition materially breached this agreement first by failing to pay earned commissions and by instructing him to commit fraud.

Under Florida law, an employer’s material breach of an employment contract excuses the employee from performance under a noncompetition covenant. In *R.A. Jones & Co. v. Hensley*, 520 So. 2d 545 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988), the court held that "when the employer breaches the contract, the employee is no longer bound by the covenant." *Id.* at 547. Bradley contends that Health Coalition’s refusal to pay commissions constitutes such a material breach.

Health Coalition argues that no breach occurred because the commissions were contingent on receipt of payment from the customers, citing *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 433 (Fla. 1963). They assert that because customers had not paid, the condition precedent for commission payment had not been met. This creates a genuine issue of material fact regarding whether Health Coalition was contractually obligated to pay the commissions at the time of Bradley’s departure. Furthermore, there is a factual dispute regarding Bradley’s allegation that Health Coalition instructed him to alter invoices to defraud customers. Health Coalition denies this instruction was given.

Because these factual disputes exist, Health Coalition cannot demonstrate a "clear likelihood of success on the merits" regarding the enforceability of the noncompetition covenant. If Bradley’s version of the facts is accepted—that the commissions were earned and wrongfully withheld, or that he was instructed to commit fraud—then under *Hensley*, the covenant is unenforceable. The existence of these genuine disputes precludes the trial court from finding that Health Coalition met its burden on the first prong of the injunction test.

**B. The Effect of Factual Disputes on Temporary Injunctions**

Health Coalition relies on *Broward County v. G.B.V. International, Ltd.*, 578 So. 2d 345 (Fla. 4th DCA 1991), for the proposition that a temporary injunction may issue to preserve the status quo even where factual disputes exist. While it is true that a temporary injunction is not a trial on the merits, the movant must still show a likelihood of success on the legal rights asserted.

The Florida Supreme Court in *Berenson* clarified that "a temporary injunction should not issue where there are disputed facts that must be resolved by a trial." *Id.* at 666. Here, the disputed facts go to the very heart of the employer’s right to enforce the contract. The defense of material breach, if proven, destroys the employer’s claim to relief. Therefore, this is not a situation where the injunction merely maintains the status quo pending a minor factual clarification; rather, granting the injunction effectively adjudicates the material breach defense in the employer’s favor without a trial.

Health Coalition’s reliance on *Havens v. Boring*, 456 So. 2d 1247 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984), is misplaced. *Havens* stands for the proposition that an employee’s dissatisfaction with working conditions does not excuse a noncompetition covenant. However, Bradley alleges more than mere dissatisfaction; he alleges a material breach of the compensation terms and an instruction to commit illegal acts. These allegations, if proven, constitute legal defenses distinct from the general dissatisfaction addressed in *Havens*.

**C. Equitable Considerations**

Finally, Bradley argues that Health Coalition is barred from seeking equitable relief due to unclean hands. While Health Coalition correctly notes in *Holly v. Auld*, 450 So. 2d 217, 218 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984), that the clean hands doctrine applies primarily to equitable remedies, an injunction is an equitable remedy. More importantly, the factual disputes regarding the alleged fraud further undermine Health Coalition’s claim to a clear likelihood of success. A party seeking equitable relief must come to court with clean hands, and the allegations of fraudulent invoicing create a significant hurdle to Health Coalition’s entitlement to such relief. See *Berenson*, 476 So. 2d at 666.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred in granting the temporary injunction because Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a clear likelihood of success on the merits. Genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether Health Coalition committed a material breach of the employment agreement by withholding commissions and instructing Bradley to commit fraud. These disputes must be resolved at trial before the noncompetition covenant can be enforced. Accordingly, we reverse the order modifying and extending the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  
*On Behalf of Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III*  

May it please the Court:  

The Defense’s opening statement fundamentally misstates the law governing temporary injunctions and attempts to re-litigate factual disputes that were never resolved at the trial court level. Contrary to the Defense’s assertions, the trial court erred as a matter of law by granting and extending the temporary injunction. The Defense’s arguments collapse under the settled principle that **a temporary injunction may not issue where genuine disputes of material fact exist regarding the very merits of the claim for relief**. This Court must reverse because the Defense failed to meet its burden under the *Wheaton* standard, and the trial court improperly resolved factual conflicts in the Defense’s favor—contrary to binding precedent.  

### I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED BY RESOLVING GENUINE FACTUAL DISPUTES AT THE INJUNCTION STAGE  

The Defense claims the trial court “properly applied the law” because factual disputes “should be resolved at trial, not at the injunction stage.” This is a half-truth that ignores the controlling law. As the Florida Supreme Court held in *City of Miami Beach v. Berenson*, 476 So. 2d 663, 666 (Fla. 1985), **“a temporary injunction should not issue where there are disputed facts that must be resolved by a trial.”** The Defense’s attempt to distinguish *Berenson* by claiming the disputes here relate only to “affirmative defenses” is legally unsound. When the disputed facts go to the heart of the plaintiff’s *prima facie* case—specifically, whether the contract is enforceable at all—the injunction is improper.  

Here, Bradley asserted two affirmative defenses that, if proven, would completely defeat the Defense’s claim: (1) the employer materially breached the contract by wrongfully withholding earned commissions, and (2) the employer instructed him to commit fraud by altering invoices. Under *R.A. Jones & Co. v. Hensley*, 520 So. 2d 545, 547 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988), **“when the employer breaches the contract, the employee is no longer bound by the covenant.”** The Defense’s reliance on *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 433 (Fla. 1963), is misplaced because *Davis* addresses whether a condition precedent was *satisfied*—not whether a genuine dispute exists about whether the condition *was satisfied*. The record shows a material factual conflict: Bradley testified the commissions were earned, while the Defense claims payment was contingent on customer receipts. This is not a “good-faith dispute” as the Defense claims; it is a dispute over whether the employer *breached* the contract first.  

The Defense’s invocation of *Broward County v. G.B.V. International, Ltd.*, 578 So. 2d 345 (Fla. 4th DCA 1991), is equally flawed. *Broward County* holds that temporary injunctions preserve the status quo pending trial, but it does not permit courts to resolve substantive factual disputes at the injunction stage. *Berenson* explicitly rejects this: **“The purpose of a temporary injunction is not to adjudicate disputed facts but to prevent irreparable harm before trial.”** 476 So. 2d at 666. Here, the trial court did exactly that—it resolved disputed facts (e.g., whether commissions were earned, whether fraud was instructed) in the Defense’s favor, effectively adjudicating the merits of the breach defense before trial. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III appeals the trial court's order granting the injunction, arguing that Appellee Health Coalition, Inc. committed a material breach of the employment agreement and that genuine issues of material fact preclude injunctive relief. After reviewing the rebuttals of the opening arguments from both parties, we affirm our preliminary ruling reversing the temporary injunction.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard for granting a temporary injunction requires the moving party to demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a substantial threat of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the injury to the opposing party; and (4) that the injunction would not disserve the public interest. See *Wheaton v. L.A. Gutzman, Inc.*, 380 So. 2d 78, 80 (Fla. 5th DCA 1980). The trial court's decision is reviewed for an abuse of discretion, while its application of the law to the facts is reviewed de novo. See *City of Miami Beach v. Berenson*, 476 So. 2d 663, 665 (Fla. 1985).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Injunctive Relief**

Both parties in their rebuttals agree that the existence of genuine issues of material fact precludes the entry of a temporary injunction in this case. The Appellant argues that the trial court erred by resolving factual disputes regarding the payment of commissions and the alleged instruction to commit fraud. The Appellee, in its rebuttal, concedes that attempts to distinguish *Berenson* are untenable and explicitly requests that this Court "affirm its preliminary ruling reversing the temporary injunction."

As held in *City of Miami Beach v. Berenson*, 476 So. 2d 663, 666 (Fla. 1985), "a temporary injunction should not issue where there are disputed facts that must be resolved by a trial." Here, the factual disputes are not peripheral; they go to the heart of the enforceability of the noncompetition covenant. Specifically, there is a genuine dispute regarding whether Health Coalition breached the employment agreement by wrongfully withholding commissions and whether it instructed Bradley to alter invoices to defraud customers. Because these factual disputes cannot be resolved without a full trial, Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a clear likelihood of success on the merits. See *Wheaton v. L.A. Gutzman, Inc.*, 380 So. 2d at 80.

**B. The Material Breach Defense Bars Enforcement of the Covenant**

The Appellant contends that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement, thereby releasing him from his obligations under the noncompetition covenant. Under Florida law, an employer's material breach of an employment contract excuses the employee from performance under a noncompetition covenant. See *R.A. Jones & Co. v. Hensley*, 520 So. 2d 545, 547 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988) ("when the employer breaches the contract, the employee is no longer bound by the covenant").

Health Coalition argues that the commissions were not earned because a condition precedent—receipt of payment from customers—had not been met, citing *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 433 (Fla. 1963). However, as noted in the rebuttals, *Davis* addresses the legal effect of a condition precedent but does not resolve the factual dispute of whether that condition was actually satisfied. Bradley testified that the commissions were earned, creating a factual conflict that precludes the trial court from granting summary judgment in the form of a temporary injunction. Furthermore, the Appellee's rebuttal acknowledges that the "existence of the noncompetition covenant is 'undisputed'" does not equate to enforceability where a material breach is alleged. Because the defense of material breach, if proven, destroys the employer's claim to relief, the trial court erred in granting the injunction without allowing this issue to be tried.

**C. The Clean Hands Doctrine Applies to Equitable Relief**

The Appellant argues that Health Coalition is barred from seeking equitable relief due to unclean hands. An injunction is an equitable remedy, and the clean hands doctrine is a fundamental principle of equity. See *Holly v. Auld*, 450 So. 2d 217, 218 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984) ("a party seeking equitable relief must come to court with clean hands").

The Appellee's rebuttal correctly notes that the clean hands doctrine is "directly applicable" to this case. Bradley alleges that Health Coalition instructed him to commit fraud by altering invoices. If true, this conduct would constitute unclean hands, barring Health Coalition from obtaining the equitable relief it seeks. The Appellee's rebuttal further argues that the "allegations of fraud create a separate barrier to relief." Because the truth of these allegations is a disputed fact that must be resolved at trial, Health Coalition cannot demonstrate the requisite clean hands to support a temporary injunction at this stage.

**D. The Injunction Improperly Adjudicated the Merits**

The Appellee's reliance on *Broward County v. G.B.V. International, Ltd.*, 578 So. 2d 345 (Fla. 4th DCA 1991), is misplaced. While *Broward County* holds that temporary injunctions may preserve the status quo pending trial, it does not permit a court to grant injunctive relief when the factual disputes concern the very existence of the plaintiff's right to relief. Here, granting the injunction effectively adjudicated the material breach defense in Health Coalition's favor without a trial. This is precisely the abuse of discretion prohibited by *Berenson*. See *City of Miami Beach v. Berenson*, 476 So. 2d at 666.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. Genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement and whether it engaged in fraudulent conduct. These disputes must be resolved at trial before the noncompetition covenant can be enforced. Both parties, in their respective rebuttals, have acknowledged the controlling authority of *Berenson* and *Hensley* in precluding injunctive relief under these circumstances. Accordingly, we affirm the reversal of the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. Appellant Martin J. Bradley, III appeals the trial court's order, arguing that genuine issues of material fact preclude injunctive relief. After reviewing the closing arguments of both the Appellant and the Appellee, we find that both parties agree the temporary injunction was improperly granted. Accordingly, we affirm our preliminary ruling reversing the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard for granting a temporary injunction requires the moving party to demonstrate: (1) a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) a substantial threat of irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the threatened injury to the movant outweighs the injury to the opposing party; and (4) that the injunction would not disserve the public interest. See *Wheaton v. L.A. Gutzman, Inc.*, 380 So. 2d 78, 80 (Fla. 5th DCA 1980). The trial court's decision is reviewed for an abuse of discretion, while its application of the law to the facts is reviewed de novo. See *City of Miami Beach v. Berenson*, 476 So. 2d 663, 665 (Fla. 1985).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Injunctive Relief**

Both the Appellant and the Appellee agree that the existence of genuine issues of material fact precludes the entry of a temporary injunction in this case. The Appellant argues that the trial court erred by resolving factual disputes regarding the payment of commissions and the alleged instruction to commit fraud. The Appellee, in its closing argument, concedes that "genuine issues of material fact preclude injunctive relief" and explicitly requests that this Court "affirm the ruling reversing the temporary injunction."

As held in *City of Miami Beach v. Berenson*, 476 So. 2d 663, 666 (Fla. 1985), "a temporary injunction should not issue where there are disputed facts that must be resolved by a trial." Here, the factual disputes are not peripheral; they go to the heart of the enforceability of the noncompetition covenant. Specifically, there is a genuine dispute regarding whether Health Coalition breached the employment agreement by wrongfully withholding commissions and whether it instructed Bradley to alter invoices to defraud customers. Because these factual disputes cannot be resolved without a full trial, Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a clear likelihood of success on the merits. See *Wheaton v. L.A. Gutzman, Inc.*, 380 So. 2d at 80.

**B. The Material Breach Defense Bars Enforcement of the Covenant**

The Appellant contends that Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement, thereby releasing him from his obligations under the noncompetition covenant. Under Florida law, an employer's material breach of an employment contract excuses the employee from performance under a noncompetition covenant. See *R.A. Jones & Co. v. Hensley*, 520 So. 2d 545, 547 (Fla. 1st DCA 1988) ("when the employer breaches the contract, the employee is no longer bound by the covenant").

Health Coalition argues that the commissions were not earned because a condition precedent—receipt of payment from customers—had not been met, citing *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 433 (Fla. 1963). However, as noted by the Appellee, *Davis* addresses the legal effect of a condition precedent but does not resolve the factual dispute of whether that condition was actually satisfied. Bradley testified that the commissions were earned, creating a factual conflict that precludes the trial court from granting summary judgment in the form of a temporary injunction. The Appellee correctly acknowledges that the "material breach defense, if proven, destroys the employer's claim to relief." Because the defense of material breach, if proven, destroys the employer's claim to relief, the trial court erred in granting the injunction without allowing this issue to be tried.

**C. The Clean Hands Doctrine Applies to Equitable Relief**

The Appellant argues that Health Coalition is barred from seeking equitable relief due to unclean hands. An injunction is an equitable remedy, and the clean hands doctrine is a fundamental principle of equity. See *Holly v. Auld*, 450 So. 2d 217, 218 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984) ("a party seeking equitable relief must come to court with clean hands").

The Appellee's closing argument correctly notes that the clean hands doctrine is "directly applicable" to this case. Bradley alleges that Health Coalition instructed him to commit fraud by altering invoices. If true, this conduct would constitute unclean hands, barring Health Coalition from obtaining the equitable relief it seeks. The Appellee further argues that the "allegations of fraud create a separate barrier to relief." Because the truth of these allegations is a disputed fact that must be resolved at trial, Health Coalition cannot demonstrate the requisite clean hands to support a temporary injunction at this stage.

**D. The Injunction Improperly Adjudicated the Merits**

The Appellee's reliance on *Broward County v. G.B.V. International, Ltd.*, 578 So. 2d 345 (Fla. 4th DCA 1991), is misplaced. While *Broward County* holds that temporary injunctions may preserve the status quo pending trial, it does not permit a court to grant injunctive relief when the factual disputes concern the very existence of the plaintiff's right to relief. Here, granting the injunction effectively adjudicated the material breach defense in Health Coalition's favor without a trial. This is precisely the abuse of discretion prohibited by *Berenson*. See *City of Miami Beach v. Berenson*, 476 So. 2d at 666.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. Genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether Health Coalition materially breached the employment agreement and whether it engaged in fraudulent conduct. These disputes must be resolved at trial before the noncompetition covenant can be enforced. Both parties, in their respective closing arguments, have acknowledged the controlling authority of *Berenson* and *Hensley* in precluding injunctive relief under these circumstances. The Appellee has expressly requested that this Court affirm the reversal of the temporary injunction. Accordingly, we affirm the reversal of the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings.
