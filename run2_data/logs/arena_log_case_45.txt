## I. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH MALICE

The State contends that the defendant's conduct in driving through a red light at 60 to 70 miles per hour establishes malice sufficient for second-degree murder. This argument fails because it ignores the critical fact that the defendant had just suffered a head injury that rendered him unconscious or semiconscious, and his blood alcohol level was between .24 and .32 percent—three to four times the legal limit.

### A. The Legal Standard for Second-Degree Murder

Second-degree murder requires proof of malice, which may be express or implied. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). However, implied malice arises only when the defendant commits an act "so inherently dangerous to human life as to evince a wanton and reckless disregard for human life." State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983). The critical element is that the defendant must have engaged in the dangerous conduct *willfully* or *intentionally*, with an awareness of the risk to human life. State v. Ward, 300 N.C. 442, 268 S.E.2d 74 (1980).

### B. The Defendant's Mental State Precluded the Formation of Malice

The evidence at trial established that the defendant was struck in the head by the tavern owner, fell, and struck his head on the base of a door. He then staggered to his car and could not remember anything that occurred after being struck. The trial evidence indicated he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident.

This evidence is crucial because malice requires a culpable mental state. As this Court held in State v. Brown, 300 N.C. 715, 268 S.E.2d 207 (1980), the State must prove not merely that the defendant's conduct was dangerous, but that the defendant possessed the mental state necessary to make that conduct culpable. Where a defendant is unconscious or lacks capacity to appreciate the risk of his conduct, malice cannot be established.

The State's reliance on State v. Wilkerson is misplaced. In Wilkerson, the defendant was fully conscious and alert when he deliberately drove at 80 miles per hour through a red light. 295 N.C. at 563, 247 S.E.2d at 908. There was no evidence that the defendant in Wilkerson suffered from any impairment that prevented him from appreciating the danger of his actions. Here, by contrast, the defendant had just been knocked unconscious, had no memory of the events, and had a blood alcohol level as high as .32 percent.

### C. The Evidence Does Not Support an Inference of Malice

The State asks this Court to infer malice from the mere fact of driving at high speed through a red light. However, the circumstances here distinguish this case from those where such an inference is appropriate. The defendant did not choose to drive recklessly; he was rendered unconscious or incapacitated by a head injury and was unable to make a conscious choice to drive at all.

The facts show that after being struck, the defendant "staggered" to his car. This description suggests impaired motor function and diminished capacity. He had no memory of the accident. These facts are consistent with unconsciousness or severe impairment, not with a "wanton and reckless disregard for human life."

---

## II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN EXCLUDING EVIDENCE OF UNCONSCIOUSNESS

The trial court committed reversible error by refusing to allow the defendant to present evidence that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident and by refusing to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness.

### A. The Defense of Unconsciousness

Unconsciousness is a complete defense to murder because a person who is unconscious cannot form the requisite intent or malice. State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E.2d 348 (1975). Even if the defendant committed the act that caused death while unconscious, he cannot be guilty of murder. State v. Teague, 241 N.C. 158, 84 S.E.2d 645 (1954).

The defendant offered evidence that he could not remember anything after being struck and that he may have been unconscious at the time of the accident. This evidence was relevant to the question of whether the defendant possessed the mental state necessary for second-degree murder. The trial court erred in excluding this evidence.

### B. The Jury Should Have Been Instructed on Unconsciousness

Where there is evidence that the defendant may have been unconscious at the time of the killing, the defendant is entitled to have the jury instructed on the defense of unconsciousness. State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E.2d 348 (1975). The trial court erred in refusing this instruction.

The failure to give this instruction deprived the jury of the opportunity to consider whether the defendant was capable of forming malice. The defendant was entitled to have the jury decide whether, under all the circumstances, he possessed the mental state necessary for second-degree murder.

---

## III. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY IDENTIFIED THE LACK OF MALICE EVIDENCE

The State argues that the Court of Appeals erred in sua sponte identifying the lack of malice evidence as plain error. However, the Court of Appeals' identification of this issue, while perhaps procedurally unusual, was correct in substance.

The evidence at trial was insufficient to establish malice. The defendant was rendered unconscious or incapacitated by a head injury and extreme intoxication. He had no memory of the events. Under these circumstances, the jury could not have found beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant acted with malice.

The Court of Appeals correctly reversed the convictions. This Court should affirm that reversal.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defendant-appellee respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the three second-degree murder convictions.

The evidence was insufficient to establish that the defendant acted with malice. The trial court erred in excluding evidence of unconsciousness and in refusing to instruct the jury on that defense. The Court of Appeals correctly identified these errors and reversed the convictions.

The State has not demonstrated that the Court of Appeals erred. The convictions should remain reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF NORTH CAROLINA v. MICHAEL RAY WALKER**

**NO. COA**

**Before the Court**

**Preliminary Ruling**

This matter comes before the Court on the State’s appeal of a Court of Appeals decision reversing three convictions for second-degree murder. The central issue for determination is whether the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to establish the element of malice necessary to sustain convictions for second-degree murder. After careful review of the record, the arguments of counsel, and the applicable precedent, we hold that the Court of Appeals erred in finding there was "no evidence" of malice. We therefore reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**I. Standard of Review**

Murder in the second degree is defined as the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not a necessary element, the crime requires an intentional act sufficient to show malice which proximately causes death. State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from conduct that is "inherently dangerous to human life" and evinces a "wanton and reckless disregard for human life." State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983).

**II. Sufficiency of the Evidence to Establish Malice**

The State argues, and we agree, that the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to allow a reasonable jury to find that the defendant acted with malice. The evidence tended to show that the defendant, while highly intoxicated, drove his vehicle at a speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour through a red light that had been red for some time. To avoid a collision, the defendant swerved, striking another vehicle and killing three occupants.

This conduct falls squarely within the definition of implied malice established by our precedent. In State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978), this Court held that driving at an excessive speed through a red light, resulting in a fatal collision, constitutes evidence of malice. The defendant’s speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour in an urban intersection, coupled with his disregard for the traffic signal, constitutes an intentional act "so inherently dangerous to human life as to evince a wanton and reckless disregard for human life." State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983). The fact that the defendant was intoxicated does not negate the wantonness of the act; rather, it underscores the recklessness of the conduct.

The Court of Appeals concluded that there was "no evidence" of malice. This conclusion is incorrect as a matter of law. The evidence of the defendant’s driving—specifically the high rate of speed and the running of a red light—is itself substantive evidence of malice. See State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). While the defendant may have offered evidence to rebut the inference of malice, the existence of conflicting evidence does not render the State’s evidence non-existent. The jury is charged with resolving conflicts in the evidence, and the State’s evidence was legally sufficient to support the verdicts.

**III. The Defense of Unconsciousness**

The defendant argues that the evidence was insufficient to establish malice because he was rendered unconscious by a head injury prior to the accident. The defense relies on State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E.2d 348 (1975), and State v. Teague, 241 N.C. 158, 84 S.E.2d 645 (1954), for the proposition that unconsciousness negates the mental state required for murder.

We acknowledge that unconsciousness is a valid defense to murder if proven. State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E.2d 348 (1975). However, the presence of evidence supporting a defense does not equate to a lack of evidence of malice by the State. The State presented evidence that the defendant "staggered" to his car, drove it at a high rate of speed, and navigated through traffic (albeit recklessly) before the collision. This evidence supports an inference that the defendant was conscious and acting voluntarily at the time of the offense. The question of whether the defendant was actually unconscious, or merely intoxicated, is a factual issue for the jury. The trial court’s refusal to instruct the jury on unconsciousness or to admit certain evidence regarding that defense may constitute error, but that is a separate matter from the sufficiency of the State’s evidence of malice.

The Court of Appeals’ finding of "no evidence" of malice effectively removed the case from the jury’s consideration on an element for which the State had presented substantial evidence. This was error. The evidence of the defendant’s driving was sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss and to support the jury’s finding of malice.

**IV. Conclusion**

The Court of Appeals erred in concluding that there was no evidence of malice and in reversing the convictions on that basis. The State’s evidence established that the defendant engaged in conduct inherently dangerous to human life with a wanton and reckless disregard for safety, satisfying the requirements for second-degree murder. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978).

Accordingly, we reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals. We remand this case to the Court of Appeals for consideration of the defendant’s remaining arguments regarding the trial court’s exclusion of evidence and refusal to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness, issues which were properly raised and preserved for appeal.

The defense argues that the evidence of unconsciousness negates the inference of malice. Specifically, the defense relies on State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E.2d 348 (1975), and State v. Teague, 241 N.C. 158, 84 S.E.2d 645 (1954), for the proposition that unconsciousness negates the mental state required for murder. We agree that unconsciousness is a complete defense to murder if proven. However, the existence of evidence supporting a defense does not render the State's evidence of malice non-existent.

The State presented evidence that the defendant "staggered" to his car, struck it with his fist, entered the vehicle, and drove it for a significant distance and time at a high rate of speed. This evidence supports an inference that the defendant was conscious and acting voluntarily. The defendant's evidence of a head injury and lack of memory creates a conflict in the evidence. It is the jury's role to resolve this conflict and determine whether the defendant was truly unconscious or merely intoxicated. The Court of Appeals' finding of "no evidence" of malice improperly removed this question from the jury's consideration.

However, this is a distinct issue from the sufficiency of the State's evidence.

The Court of Appeals reversed the convictions on the basis that there was "no evidence" of malice. This was error. The State presented substantial evidence of malice through the defendant's driving conduct. The error, if any, lies in the trial court's exclusion of the defendant's evidence, not in the State's failure to present evidence. Therefore, the reversal by the Court of Appeals cannot stand on the ground of insufficient evidence.

**III. Conclusion**

We hold that the Court of Appeals erred in finding that there was no evidence of malice. The State's evidence was sufficient to support the jury's verdict of second-degree murder. We reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals on this specific issue.

However, we agree with the defense that the trial court's exclusion of evidence regarding the defense of unconsciousness and its refusal to instruct the jury on that defense raise significant constitutional concerns. These issues were properly preserved for appeal and require careful consideration by the Court of Appeals.

**STATE OF NORTH CAROLINA v. MICHAEL RAY WALKER**

**NO. COA**

**Before the Court**

**Final Ruling**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution, and upon review of the record and the preliminary ruling, we find that the Court of Appeals erred in reversing the defendant's convictions for second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals improperly found that there was "no evidence" of malice to support the convictions. We reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals on this issue and remand the case for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**I. Standard of Review**

When reviewing the sufficiency of the evidence to support a conviction, the evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the State, and the State is entitled to every reasonable inference to be drawn therefrom. State v. Vause, 328 N.C. 231, 401 S.E.2d 467 (1991). It is the exclusive responsibility of the jury to weigh the evidence and determine its credibility. State v. Lynch, 334 N.C. 402, 432 S.E.2d 349 (1993). An appellate court may not reweigh evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the jury. Id.

**II. Sufficiency of Evidence to Establish Malice**

The defendant was convicted of three counts of second-degree murder. Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E.2d 188 (1983); State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978). While an intent to kill is not required, the crime does require "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E.2d 317 (1983).

Malice may be implied from conduct that is reckless and evinces a wanton disregard for human life. In State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E.2d 905 (1978), this Court held that driving at an excessive speed through a red light constitutes evidence of malice because it demonstrates a "wanton and reckless disregard for human life." The evidence in the present case is indistinguishable from the conduct condemned in Wilkerson.

The State presented evidence that the defendant, after being ejected from a tavern, entered his vehicle and drove at a speed of 60 to 70 miles per hour. The defendant entered an intersection where the traffic light had been red for some time. To avoid one car, the defendant swerved and crashed into another vehicle, killing three occupants. This evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the State, is sufficient to permit a jury to find that the defendant acted with malice.

The Court of Appeals concluded that there was "no evidence" of malice. This conclusion was based on a misapplication of the standard of review. The existence of evidence supporting a defense of unconsciousness does not render the State's evidence of malice non-existent. State v. Johnson, 293 N.C. 246, 235 S.E.2d 497 (1977). The State's evidence showed that the defendant "staggered" to his car, struck it with his fist, and drove a significant distance at a high rate of speed. These actions support an inference of conscious, voluntary conduct. The defendant's claim of memory loss and potential unconsciousness created a conflict in the evidence for the jury to resolve. It was not the province of the Court of Appeals to resolve this conflict in favor of the defendant by finding "no evidence" of malice. State v. Lynch, 334 N.C. 402, 432 S.E.2d 349 (1993).

**III. The Defense of Unconsciousness**

The defense argues that the trial court committed error by excluding evidence of the defendant's unconsciousness and by refusing to instruct the jury on that defense. Under State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E.2d 348 (1975), a defendant is entitled to such an instruction if there is substantial evidence to support the theory. The trial court's refusal to allow this evidence and instruction may constitute prejudicial error.

However, the error of the trial court, if any, is distinct from the sufficiency of the State's evidence. The Court of Appeals reversed the convictions solely on the ground that there was "no evidence" of malice. As discussed above, this was error. The State presented substantial evidence of malice through the defendant's reckless driving. The issue of the trial court's exclusion of the unconsciousness defense is a matter of trial error that must be considered on its own merits. It does not justify a finding of "no evidence" of malice.

**IV. Conclusion**

We hold that the Court of Appeals erred in finding that there was no evidence of malice. The State's evidence was sufficient to support the jury's verdict of second-degree murder. We reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals on this specific issue.

We remand this case to the Court of Appeals for further consideration. The Court of Appeals should address the defendant's arguments regarding the trial court's exclusion of the unconsciousness defense and the refusal to instruct the jury thereon, to determine whether those errors prejudiced the defendant and warrant a new trial.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
