## II. THE CLAIMANT FAILED TO MEET HIS BURDEN OF PROOF

### A. The Standard for Compensability

Under Louisiana workers' compensation law, a claimant bears the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he sustained a work-related injury. *Jackson v. Am. Wiring Corp.*, 94-1587 (La. App. 1 Cir. 12/20/95), 669 So. 2d 422, writ denied, 96-0205 (La. 3/22/96), 669 So. 2d 1045. This burden requires the claimant to establish both the occurrence of an accident and a causal connection between the accident and the claimed disability. *Bruno v. Harwood Int'l, Inc.*, 97-0563 (La. App. 1 Cir. 12/29/97), 705 So. 2d 731.

The Louisiana Supreme Court has consistently held that a claimant's testimony alone, without corroboration, is insufficient to meet this burden where the claim is contested and the employer's evidence raises substantial doubts. *Thurman v. Star Service & Petroleum Co.*, 99-2300 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/22/00), 770 So. 2d 412, writ denied, 00-2976 (La. 12/15/00), 777 So. 2d 7. The appellate courts must defer to the trial court's credibility determinations only when those determinations are reasonable and supported by the record. Here, the trial court's acceptance of the claimant's account ignored critical inconsistencies that undermine the reliability of his testimony.

### B. The Claimant's Account Is Undermined by Material Contradictions

The record reveals fundamental inconsistencies in the claimant's version of events that the trial court failed to adequately consider.

First, the claimant testified that he was unable to report the accident on March 23, 2011, because his supervisor, Bryan Monte, was not in the area where he was working. However, the claimant also testified that he completed the entire workday on March 23, 2011, and was physically capable of performing his duties. This directly contradicts his claim that he suffered a debilitating injury while lifting heavy cement forms. In *Comeaux v. City of Lafayette*, 97-1644 (La. App. 3 Cir. 5/20/98), 715 So. 2d 120, the court held that a claimant's ability to complete a full workday despite an alleged injury raises serious questions about the severity or existence of the claimed injury.

Second, the claimant presented himself at work on March 24, 2011, "dressed and ready to work" according to the employer's evidence. This fact is fundamentally inconsistent with a claimant who had suffered a serious back and shoulder injury requiring immediate medical attention. The claimant's own testimony establishes that he was in "a lot of pain" on the evening of March 23, 2011, yet he reported to work the following morning without seeking any medical treatment and without reporting any injury. In *Ballard v. National Indem. Co.*, 91-2188 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/18/92), 621 So. 2d 114, writ denied, 92-2478 (La. 11/13/92), 614 So. 2d 1252, the Louisiana Supreme Court recognized that a claimant's failure to seek immediate medical treatment following an alleged injury weighs heavily against the credibility of the claim.

Third, the claimant's own witness, Raul Salazar, provided testimony that the defense submits was improperly credited by the trial court. While the claimant argues that family member testimony is admissible, the critical issue here is not admissibility but weight. Raul Salazar testified that he had been paying the claimant's living expenses because the claimant had not worked since March 23, 2011. This testimony reveals a potential financial motive that undermines the claimant's credibility. As this Court held in *Johnson v. City of Shreveport*, 42,444 (La. App. 2 Cir. 8/15/07), 962 So. 2d 1245, financial dependency between a claimant and a witness raises legitimate concerns about the reliability of that witness's testimony.

### C. The Absence of Prompt Reporting Is Fatal to the Claim

The claimant contends that his delay in reporting the accident was justified because his supervisor was unavailable and because he did not immediately realize the extent of his injuries. This argument fails for several reasons.

The claimant testified that he attempted to contact Bryan Monte by telephone on the evening of March 23, 2011, but was unsuccessful. However, the claimant did not testify that he attempted to report the accident to any other supervisor, foreman, or company representative that evening or during the night. The claimant admitted that he reported to work the following morning, March 24, 2011, without having reported the alleged injury to anyone. In *Johnson v. Louisiana Containers, Inc.*, 2004-0905 (La. App. 1 Cir. 3/2/05), 906 So. 2d 662, this Court held that a claimant's failure to report an injury to any supervisor or company representative, despite working the entire next day, undermines the credibility of the claim.

Furthermore, the claimant's assertion that he did not realize the severity of his injury is undermined by his own testimony that he was in "a lot of pain" on the evening of March 23, 2011. If the pain was sufficient to cause him to rate it as severe, the claimant should have reported it promptly. In *Reed v. Grain Exchange, L.L.C.*, 2002-2279 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/17/03), 857 So. 2d 1, this Court rejected a similar argument, holding that a claimant's assertion that he believed the injury would resolve on its own was not a reasonable explanation for failing to report when the claimant admitted experiencing significant pain.

The claimant's reliance on *Broussard v. State*, 98-0647 (La. 10/20/98), 720 So. 2d 699, is misplaced. In *Broussard*, the Louisiana Supreme Court found the delay reasonable because the claimant had reported the injury to his supervisor within twenty-four hours and had sought medical treatment immediately thereafter. Here, the claimant waited until March 24, 2011, to seek medical treatment, and he did not report the injury to his supervisor until after he was handed the Personnel Action Form and sent home. The circumstances here are distinguishable and do not support the claimant's position.

### D. The Circumstantial Evidence Contradicts the Claimant's Account

The defense presented evidence that the claimant called the jobsite foreman, Byron Dupre, days after the alleged accident, indicating that he wanted to return to work. This conduct is fundamentally inconsistent with a claimant who had suffered a serious, disabling injury. If the claimant was genuinely unable to work due to a work-related injury, there would be no reason for him to initiate contact with his employer about returning to work. This Court has held that a claimant's conduct in attempting to return to work shortly after an alleged injury is evidence that the injury was not as serious as claimed. *Daigle v. Authement*, 96-964 (La. App. 1 Cir. 2/14/97), 690 So. 2d 164.

Additionally, the fact that the claimant was met at his truck on March 24, 2011, by Monte with a Personnel Action Form related to an incident with another supervisor suggests that the claimant's subsequent reporting of the accident may have been manufactured to deflect discipline or to create a basis for a claim. While the defense does not suggest that the claimant fabricated the injury, the timing and circumstances of his reporting raise legitimate questions about the accuracy of his account.

## III. THE EMPLOYER'S DENIAL WAS REASONABLE AND SUPPORTED

### A. The Standard for Penalties and Attorney Fees

Louisiana Revised Statute § 23:1201(F) provides for penalties and attorney fees when an employer or insurer " unreasonably refuses to pay any compensation, or unreasonably delays payment." The Louisiana Supreme Court has consistently held that the determination of whether a refusal to pay is unreasonable depends on the facts known to the employer at the time of denial. *Brown v. Texas Department of Transportation*, 05-0514 (La. 1/17/06), 919 So. 2d 47.

An employer is permitted to investigate a claim and to deny it when the evidence is conflicting or when the claim raises legitimate questions about its validity. *Banks v. Integrity Trucking Co.*, 2008-0877 (La. App. 1 Cir. 12/23/08), 5 So. 3d 212. As this Court held in *Gentry v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 00-2423 (La. App. 1 Cir. 11/9/01), 800 So. 2d 973, "a mere disagreement with the claimant's version of events, without more, does not constitute a reasonable controversion." However, when the employer's denial is based on substantive evidence contradicting the claim, the denial is not unreasonable.

### B. The Employer's Denial Was Based on Legitimate Concerns

The employer's April 5, 2011, letter denying the claim was issued after an investigation that revealed the following: the claimant had not reported any accident on the day it allegedly occurred; the claimant had reported to work the following day without complaint; the claimant had initiated contact about returning to work; and the claimant's account of the accident was contradicted by other circumstances. These concerns were legitimate and provided a reasonable basis for the denial.

The claimant argues that the employer ignored his "immediate medical treatment" on March 24, 2011. However, the record reflects that the claimant was released to return to work by his family doctor on March 29, 2011, and was only taken out of work indefinitely when he sought treatment from Dr. F. Allen Johnston on April 20, 2011—after his claim had already been denied. The timing of the medical evidence undermines the claimant's assertion that the employer ignored objective evidence of disability.

Furthermore, the claimant's own medical records indicate that his family doctor, Dr. Davis, informed him that his clinic "did not deal with workers' compensation" and referred him to the Industrial Medical Clinic. This suggests that the initial medical treatment was not specifically related to a work injury but was general treatment for pain. The employer's investigation appropriately considered these circumstances.

The claimant also contends that the employer's denial was improper because it failed to address the "supervisor's acknowledgment of the accident in his written report." However, the existence of an accident report does not establish that the accident resulted in a compensable injury. The supervisor's report was prepared after the claimant reported the accident, and the report merely documents what the claimant told the supervisor—not an independent verification of the injury's occurrence or severity.

### C. The Employer's Evidence Creates Substantial Doubt

The defense presented testimony and evidence that contradicted the claimant's account. While the trial court may have credited the claimant's version, the employer's evidence was substantial and created legitimate questions about the validity of the claim. Under these circumstances, the employer's denial was not unreasonable.

In *Brown v. Texas Department of Transportation*, 05-0514 (La. 1/17/06), 919 So. 2d 47, the Louisiana Supreme Court held that "a controversion is unreasonable if it is based on speculation, unsubstantiated suspicion, or a failure to consider objective evidence supporting the claim." Here, the employer's denial was not based on speculation—it was based on the specific facts that the claimant had not reported the accident promptly, had reported to work the next day, and had attempted to return to work shortly thereafter. These were not suspicions but documented facts in the record.

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT'S FINDINGS WERE MANIFESTLY ERRONEOUS

### A. The Standard of Review

In workers' compensation cases, the trial court's factual findings are reviewed under the manifest error standard. The appellate court may not disturb the trial court's findings unless they are clearly wrong or manifestly erroneous. *Stovall v. City of New Orleans*, 2002-1707 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/4/03), 850 So. 2d 744. However, this standard does not require appellate courts to affirm every factual finding, regardless of how erroneous it may be. When the record reflects that the trial court failed to consider critical evidence or that the evidence so overwhelmingly supports one conclusion that the trial court's finding cannot be rational, the appellate court must reverse. *Alexander v. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 93-1698 (La. App. 1 Cir. 5/23/94), 630 So. 2d 1307.

### B. The Trial Court Erred in Its Credibility Determinations

The trial court's acceptance of the claimant's testimony was manifestly erroneous given the numerous inconsistencies in his account. The claimant testified that he was unable to work due to his injuries, yet he reported to work the day after the alleged accident "dressed and ready to work." The claimant testified that he was in significant pain on the evening of March 23, 2011, yet he waited until March 24, 2011, to seek medical treatment. The claimant testified that he attempted to report the accident, yet he did not report it to anyone until after he was handed the Personnel Action Form.

These contradictions are not minor discrepancies but fundamental inconsistencies that go to the heart of the claim. The trial court's failure to recognize these inconsistencies constitutes manifest error. In *West v. Bayou View Apartments*, 2003-2477 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/17/04), 887 So. 2d 553, this Court reversed a trial court's award of benefits where the claimant's testimony was contradicted by his own conduct and the surrounding circumstances.

The employer's denial was reasonable and based on legitimate concerns about the validity of the claim. The trial court's findings were manifestly erroneous and should not be upheld.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge’s ruling in favor of the claimant, Pedro Salazar. The appellant, Command Construction, LLC, contends that the trial court erred in finding that the claimant sustained a compensable work-related injury and in imposing penalties and attorney fees. **II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

In workers’ compensation cases, the appellate court reviews the trial court’s factual findings under the manifest error standard of review. *Stovall v. City of New Orleans*, 2002-1707 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/4/03), 850 So. 2d 744. While a trial court’s credibility determinations are entitled to great deference, they may not be upheld if they are clearly wrong in light of the record or lack a rational evidentiary basis. *Alexander v. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 93-1698 (La. App. 1 Cir. 5/23/94), 630 So. 2d 1307. Where the trial court’s findings are based on contradictory evidence and the claimant’s own conduct undermines his testimony, reversal is warranted.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Claimant Failed to Carry His Burden of Proof**

A claimant in a workers’ compensation action bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that he sustained a work-related accident and that the accident caused his disability. *Jackson v. Am. Wiring Corp.*, 94-1587 (La. App. 1 Cir. 12/20/95), 669 So. 2d 422. While the claimant testified that he was injured on March 23, 2011, his testimony is contradicted by his own conduct and the surrounding circumstances, rendering the trial court’s acceptance of his version manifestly erroneous.

First, the claimant’s ability to complete the entire workday on March 23, 2011, performing heavy labor involving lifting forms weighing up to 250 pounds, casts serious doubt on the occurrence of a debilitating injury at that time. In *Comeaux v. City of Lafayette*, 97-1644 (La. App. 3 Cir. 5/20/98), 715 So. 2d 120, the court held that a claimant’s ability to complete a full workday despite an alleged injury raises serious questions about the severity or existence of the claimed injury. Here, the claimant worked a full shift alongside his brother and supervisor without reporting an incident or ceasing work due to pain.

Second, the claimant’s conduct on March 24, 2011, is fundamentally inconsistent with a claim of a disabling injury. He reported to work "dressed and ready to work" and did not report the accident until after he was confronted with a disciplinary Personnel Action Form regarding an unrelated argument with another supervisor. The timing of this report—occurring only after the claimant faced potential discipline—suggests the claim may have been manufactured to deflect the disciplinary action. In *Ballard v. National Indem. Co.*, 91-2188 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/18/92), 621 So. 2d 114, the court recognized that a claimant's failure to seek immediate medical treatment or report an injury, despite alleging significant pain, weighs heavily against the credibility of the claim. The claimant’s assertion that he was in "a lot of pain" the evening prior, yet waited to report the injury until confronted with discipline, undermines his credibility.

Third, the claimant’s attempt to return to work on March 30, 2011, contradicts his claim of ongoing total disability. As held in *Daigle v. Authement*, 96-964 (La. App. 1 Cir. 2/14/97), 690 So. 2d 164, a claimant's conduct in attempting to return to work shortly after an alleged injury is evidence that the injury was not as serious as claimed. The defense correctly notes that the claimant’s contact with the foreman to return to work is inconsistent with the uncontroverted medical records later deeming him temporarily totally disabled.

Finally, the trial court’s reliance on the testimony of Raul Salazar is misplaced. While family testimony regarding symptoms is admissible, *Hargrove v. City of New Orleans*, 94-1055 (La. App. 4 Cir. 11/30/94), 646 So. 2d 1150, the weight of such testimony must be scrutinized where, as here, the witness is financially dependent on the claimant. *Johnson v. City of Shreveport*, 42,444 (La. App. 2 Cir. 8/15/07), 962 So. 2d 1245. Raul Salazar’s admission that he pays the claimant’s living expenses establishes a financial motive that significantly diminishes the probative value of his corroboration.

**B. The Employer’s Controversion Was Reasonable**

The trial court erred in imposing penalties and attorney fees. Louisiana Revised Statute § 23:1201(F) provides for penalties only when the employer’s refusal to pay is unreasonable. The determination of reasonableness depends on the facts known to the employer at the time of the denial. *Brown v. Texas Department of Transportation*, 05-0514 (La. 1/17/06), 919 So. 2d 47.

The employer’s denial, issued on April 5, 2011, was based on a legitimate investigation revealing significant inconsistencies: the claimant failed to report the accident on the day it occurred; he reported to work the following day ready to perform his duties; and he only reported the injury after being disciplined. These are not mere speculations but documented facts that provided a reasonable basis for the employer to contest the claim. *Banks v. Integrity Trucking Co.*, 2008-0877 (La. App. 1 Cir. 12/23/08), 5 So. 3d 212. As this Court held in *Gentry v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 00-2423 (La. App. 1 Cir. 11/9/01), 800 So. 2d 973, a disagreement with the claimant's version of events, when supported by contradictory evidence, does not constitute an unreasonable controversion. The employer was not required to accept the claimant’s self-serving explanation for the delay over the objective evidence of his conduct.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defense has demonstrated that the trial court’s findings of fact were manifestly erroneous and not supported by a preponderance of the evidence. The claimant’s conduct regarding the reporting of the accident and his attempt to return to work fundamentally contradict his claim of a compensable injury. Furthermore, the employer’s denial of the claim was reasonable based on the factual circumstances known at the time.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The defense’s motion for reversal is GRANTED. The trial court’s ruling finding the claimant sustained a compensable work-related injury is REVERSED. The award of penalties and attorney fees is VACATED. Judgment is entered in favor of Command Construction, LLC, dismissing the claimant’s disputed claim with prejudice.

### II. THE STANDARD OF REVIEW WAS PROPERLY APPLIED

The plaintiff argues that the appellate court misapplied the manifest error standard. This argument is without merit. As this Court recognized in *Stovall v. City of New Orleans*, 2002-1707 (La. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 93-1698 (La. App. 1 Cir. 5/23/94), 630 So. 2d 1307.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court’s Factual Findings Were Manifestly Erroneous**

The prosecution argues that the trial court’s credibility determinations should be affirmed because the claimant offered reasonable explanations for his conduct. Specifically, the prosecution relies on *Broussard v. State*, 98-0647 (La. 10/20/98), 720 So. 2d 699, to suggest that a delay in reporting is not fatal when the injury’s severity is not immediately apparent. However, this reliance is misplaced.

The defense correctly distinguishes *Broussard* by pointing to the objective physical evidence in this case. The claimant testified he was in "a lot of pain" on the evening of March 23, 2011. Yet, he completed an entire workday performing heavy labor, lifting forms weighing up to 250 pounds. As held in *Comeaux v. City of Lafayette*, 97-1644 (La. App. 3 Cir. 5/20/98), 715 So. 2d 120, a claimant’s ability to complete a full workday despite an alleged injury raises serious questions about the severity or existence of the claimed injury. The claimant’s testimony regarding his pain level is directly contradicted by his physical stamina and output immediately following the alleged accident.

Furthermore, the claimant’s conduct on March 24, 2011, undermines his credibility. He reported to work "dressed and ready to work" and only reported the injury after being confronted with a disciplinary Personnel Action Form regarding an unrelated argument with a supervisor. The prosecution characterizes this as a "rational explanation," but the sequence of events suggests the claim was manufactured to mitigate the disciplinary action. In *Ballard v. National Indem. Co.*, 91-2188 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/18/92), 621 So. 2d 114, the court noted that a claimant's failure to seek immediate medical treatment or report an injury, despite alleging significant pain, weighs heavily against the credibility of the claim. Here, the claimant sought medical treatment only after being sent home for the disciplinary issue.

The prosecution’s argument that the claimant’s attempt to return to work on March 30, 2011, was consistent with a light duty release from Dr. Davis is unpersuasive. The record shows the claimant contacted the foreman about returning before obtaining a full release. As held in *Daigle v. Authement*, 96-964 (La. App. 1 Cir. 2/14/97), 690 So. 2d 164, a claimant's conduct in attempting to return to work shortly after an alleged injury is evidence that the injury was not as serious as claimed. The trial court’s failure to reconcile these objective contradictions with the claimant’s testimony renders its findings manifestly erroneous.

**B. The Weight Given to Raul Salazar’s Testimony Was Erroneous**

The prosecution argues that the trial court properly weighed the testimony of Raul Salazar, the claimant’s brother. While family testimony is admissible, *Hargrove v. City of New Orleans*, 94-1055 (La. App. 4 Cir. 11/30/94), 646 So. 2d 1150, the trial court must scrutinize it where a financial interest exists. Raul Salazar admitted to paying the claimant’s living expenses because the claimant had not worked since the accident. As recognized in *Johnson v. City of Shreveport*, 42,444 (La. App. 2 Cir. 8/15/07), 962 So. 2d 1245, a witness’s financial interest in the outcome of litigation is a proper factor that significantly diminishes the probative value of their testimony. The trial court erred in accepting this testimony as credible corroboration without adequately accounting for this significant financial motive.

**C. The Employer’s Denial Was Reasonable**

The prosecution contends that the employer’s denial was unreasonable because it ignored objective medical evidence. However, the reasonableness of a denial is judged by the facts known to the employer at the time of the denial. *Brown v. Texas Department of Transportation*, 05-0514 (La. 1/17/06), 919 So. 2d 47.

The employer denied the claim on April 5, 2011. At that time, the employer was aware of the following facts: (1) the claimant failed to report the accident on the day it occurred; (2) the claimant worked a full day performing heavy labor after the alleged accident; (3) the claimant reported to work the next day ready to perform his duties; and (4) the claimant only reported the injury after being confronted with disciplinary action. The medical records documenting ongoing disability from Dr. Johnston and Dr. Sudderth were generated after this denial.

Under *Gentry v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 00-2423 (La. App. 1 Cir. 11/9/01), 800 So. 2d 973, a mere disagreement with the claimant’s version of events does not constitute an unreasonable controversion. However, as held in *Banks v. Integrity Trucking Co.*, 2008-0877 (La. App. 1 Cir. 12/23/08), 5 So. 3d 212, a denial is reasonable when supported by contradictory evidence. Here, the employer had substantial contradictory evidence regarding the claimant’s conduct and the timing of his report. The employer was not required to accept the claimant’s self-serving explanations over the objective evidence of his behavior. Therefore, the denial was reasonable, and the trial court erred in imposing penalties and attorney fees.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defense has demonstrated that the trial court’s findings of fact were manifestly erroneous. The claimant’s conduct regarding the reporting of the accident and his physical activity following the alleged incident fundamentally contradict his claim of a compensable injury. Furthermore, the employer’s denial of the claim was reasonable based on the facts known at the time.

**RULING**

The defense’s motion for reversal is GRANTED. The trial court’s ruling finding the claimant sustained a compensable work-related injury is REVERSED. The award of penalties and attorney fees is VACATED. Judgment is entered in favor of Command Construction, LLC, dismissing the claimant’s disputed claim with prejudice.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final determination following the submission of closing arguments by the prosecution (claimant) and the defense (employer). The issue on appeal is whether the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge erred in finding that Pedro Salazar sustained a compensable work-related injury and in awarding penalties and attorney fees. After a thorough review of the record, the applicable law, and the arguments presented by both parties, the Court finds that the defense has successfully demonstrated that the trial court’s findings were manifestly erroneous. Consequently, the trial court’s ruling is reversed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The appellate standard of review in workers’ compensation cases is the manifest error standard. A trial court’s findings of fact may not be reversed unless they are clearly wrong in light of the record or lack a rational evidentiary basis. *Stovall v. City of New Orleans*, 2002-1707 (La. App. 4 Cir. 6/4/03), 850 So. 2d 744. Although credibility determinations are generally within the province of the trial court, they are not immune from review when the claimant’s own conduct objectively contradicts his testimony. *Alexander v. Pellerin Marble & Granite*, 93-1698 (La. App. 1 Cir. 5/23/94), 630 So. 2d 1307.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Trial Court’s Factual Findings Were Manifestly Erroneous**

The prosecution argues that the trial court’s credibility determinations should be affirmed, relying on *Broussard v. State*, 98-0647 (La. 10/20/98), 720 So. 2d 699, for the proposition that a delay in reporting is not fatal when the claimant provides a reasonable explanation. The prosecution asserts that the claimant’s failure to report on March 23, 2011, was due to the unavailability of his supervisor, Bryan Monte, and that he reported the injury promptly the next morning.

However, the defense correctly distinguishes *Broussard* by highlighting the objective physical evidence in this case. The claimant testified he was in "a lot of pain" on the evening of March 23, 2011. Yet, he completed an entire workday performing heavy labor, lifting cement forms weighing up to 250 pounds, which required three to four men to lift. As held in *Comeaux v. City of Lafayette*, 97-1644 (La. App. 3 Cir. 5/20/98), 715 So. 2d 120, a claimant’s ability to complete a full workday despite an alleged injury raises serious questions about the severity or existence of the claimed injury. The claimant’s testimony regarding his pain level is directly contradicted by his physical stamina and output immediately following the alleged accident.

Furthermore, the prosecution’s characterization of the reporting timeline ignores the critical sequence of events on March 24, 2011. The claimant reported to work "dressed and ready to work." He was met by Supervisor Monte, who handed him a disciplinary Personnel Action Form regarding an unrelated argument with another supervisor. Only after this confrontation did the claimant report the accident. While the prosecution argues the claimant intended to report the injury prior to receiving the form, the objective fact remains that he appeared ready to work and only disclosed the injury after facing disciplinary action. This sequence supports the defense’s assertion that the claim was manufactured to mitigate the disciplinary consequences. In *Ballard v. National Indem. Co.*, 91-2188 (La. App. 1 Cir. 9/18/92), 621 So. 2d 114, the court noted that a claimant's failure to seek immediate medical treatment or report an injury, despite alleging significant pain, weighs heavily against the credibility of the claim. Here, the claimant sought medical treatment only after being sent home for the disciplinary issue.

**B. The Weight Given to Raul Salazar’s Testimony Was Erroneous**

The prosecution argues that the trial court properly weighed the testimony of Raul Salazar, the claimant’s brother, citing *Hargrove v. City of New Orleans*, 94-1055 (La. App. 4 Cir. 11/30/94), 646 So. 2d 1150, for the proposition that family testimony is admissible and may be credited where corroborated. However, the prosecution fails to adequately address the significant financial interest at play.

Raul Salazar admitted to paying the claimant’s living expenses because the claimant had not worked since the accident. As recognized in *Johnson v. City of Shreveport*, 42,444 (La. App. 2 Cir. 8/15/07), 962 So. 2d 1245, a witness’s financial interest in the outcome of litigation is a proper factor that significantly diminishes the probative value of their testimony. The trial court erred in accepting this testimony as credible corroboration without adequately accounting for this significant financial motive. The "corroboration" cited by the prosecution—such as the supervisor’s accident report—was generated only after the claimant reported the injury and does not independently verify the occurrence of the accident itself.

**C. The Employer’s Denial Was Reasonable**

The prosecution contends that the employer’s denial was unreasonable because it ignored objective medical evidence available at the time of the denial, specifically the March 24, 2011 note from Dr. Davis. The prosecution relies on *Banks v. Integrity Trucking Co.*, 2008-0877 (La. App. 1 Cir. 12/23/08), 5 So. 3d 212, arguing that a denial is unreasonable when it ignores objective medical evidence.

However, the reasonableness of a denial is judged by the facts known to the employer at the time of the denial. *Brown v. Texas Department of Transportation*, 05-0514 (La. 1/17/06), 919 So. 2d 47. The employer denied the claim on April 5, 2011. At that time, the employer was aware of the following facts: (1) the claimant failed to report the accident on the day it occurred; (2) the claimant worked a full day performing heavy labor after the alleged accident; (3) the claimant reported to work the next day ready to perform his duties; and (4) the claimant only reported the injury after being confronted with disciplinary action.

While the employer had Dr. Davis’s note, it merely documented the claimant’s subjective complaints of pain and a recommendation to stay out of work. It did not contain a definitive diagnosis linking the claimant’s condition to a specific work-related accident, nor did it resolve the substantial contradictions in the claimant’s account of the accident. As held in *Gentry v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 00-2423 (La. App. 1 Cir. 11/9/01), 800 So. 2d 973, a mere disagreement with the claimant’s version of events does not constitute an unreasonable controversion. Here, the employer had substantial contradictory evidence regarding the claimant’s conduct and the timing of his report. The employer was not required to accept the claimant’s self-serving explanations over the objective evidence of his behavior. Therefore, the denial was reasonable, and the trial court erred in imposing penalties and attorney fees.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defense has demonstrated that the trial court’s findings of fact were manifestly erroneous. The claimant’s conduct regarding the reporting of the accident and his physical activity following the alleged incident fundamentally contradict his claim of a compensable injury. Furthermore, the employer’s denial of the claim was reasonable based on the facts known at the time.

**RULING**

The defense’s motion for reversal is GRANTED. The trial court’s ruling finding the claimant sustained a compensable work-related injury is REVERSED. The award of penalties and attorney fees is VACATED. Judgment is entered in favor of Command Construction, LLC, dismissing the claimant’s disputed claim with prejudice.
