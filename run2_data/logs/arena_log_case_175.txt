## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A BIVENS REMEDY

### A. The Legal Framework for Bivens Claims

The Supreme Court has consistently held that Bivens represents a narrow, judicially created exception to sovereign immunity that should be extended only in limited circumstances. In Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), the Court recognized an implied damages remedy for Fourth Amendment violations by federal agents. However, the Court has emphasized that this remedy is an anomaly that should not be expanded beyond three specific contexts: Fourth Amendment violations (Bivens itself), Eighth Amendment prison conditions (Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14 (1980)), and Fifth Amendment gender discrimination (Davis v. Passman, 442 U.S. 228 (1979)). See Ziglar v. Abbasi, 582 U.S. ___, ___ (2017).

The controlling precedent in Ziglar v. Abbasi, 582 U.S. ___ (2017), establishes that when a plaintiff seeks to assert a Bivens claim in a "new context"—meaning one distinct from the three recognized scenarios—courts must engage in a two-step analysis. First, the court must determine whether any "special factors counsel hesitation" before implying a damages remedy. Id. at ___ (2017). Second, if no such factors exist, the court may consider whether the claim states a viable Bivens remedy. Id.

### B. This Case Presents a New Context Where Special Factors Counsel Hesitation

The district court committed reversible error by failing to properly analyze whether this case presents a "new context" requiring the application of Ziglar's special factors analysis. The alleged conduct involves military detention and interrogation practices during an armed conflict—a context wholly distinct from the three scenarios where Bivens has been recognized.

As the Supreme Court explained in Ziglar, "the decision to provide a damages remedy is the exclusive province of Congress," and courts should not create new Bivens remedies when "special factors counselling hesitation" exist. Ziglar, 582 U.S. at ___ (2017), quoting Wilkie v. Robbins, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). This principle reflects the Court's deep concern for separation of powers and the judiciary's role in preserving the structural balance of government. Id.

This case presents precisely the type of new context where such special factors counsel hesitation. The alleged conduct involves: (1) detention of a civilian contractor in a theater of armed conflict; (2) decisions regarding interrogation practices by military officials; and (3) conditions of confinement in military facilities. Each of these involves core executive branch functions related to national security and military operations.

The district court failed to acknowledge this critical distinction, instead improperly assuming that Bivens automatically applies to any alleged constitutional violation by federal officials. This error contravenes Ziglar's clear directive that courts must not "automatically infer" a damages remedy in new settings. Ziglar, 582 U.S. at ___ (2017).

### C. Special Factors Counsel Against Implying a Bivens Remedy

Multiple special factors counsel against implying a Bivens remedy in this case:

**1. Military and National Security Context**

The detention at issue occurred in Iraq during an ongoing armed conflict. As the Supreme Court recognized in Department of the Navy v. Egan, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988), the executive branch has "broad authority" over matters relating to national security and military operations. Courts are ill-equipped to second-guess decisions made by military officials regarding detention and interrogation practices in a theater of operations. Implying a Bivens remedy would require courts to assess the reasonableness of military detention decisions—a task for which the judiciary is institutionally unsuited.

**2. Separation of Powers Concerns**

Allowing damages claims against high-level executive officials for decisions made in their official capacity would impermissibly intrude upon the executive branch's constitutional functions. See Nixon v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 731, 754 (1982) (recognizing the "imperative of candor and vigor" in executive decision-making that would be chilled by personal liability). The threat of personal liability would impair the executive's ability to make difficult decisions regarding national security without fear of retrospective judicial second-guessing.

**3. Alternative Remedies and Congressional Intent**

Congress has not provided a damages remedy for the conduct alleged, and the Detainee Treatment Act, 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., which Doe invoked, does not create a private cause of action. The district court correctly dismissed Doe's DTA claims on this basis. This congressional silence weighs heavily against implying a Bivens remedy. See Ziglar, 582 U.S. at ___ (2017) (noting that "when Congress has not provided a damages remedy, courts must pause before implying one").

**4. The Availability of Other Relief**

Doe has alternative means of seeking relief, including administrative remedies and potential congressional oversight. The existence of these alternative channels counsels against judicial creation of a Bivens remedy. See Wilkie v. Robbins, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007).

Given these special factors, this Court should decline to imply a Bivens remedy and should reverse the district court's denial of Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss.

## II. ALTERNATIVELY, SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

Even if this Court were to find that a Bivens remedy is theoretically available—which it is not—Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because the constitutional right at issue was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct.

### A. The Qualified Immunity Standard

Qualified immunity shields government officials from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violates a clearly established constitutional right. Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194, 201 (2001). The inquiry involves two questions: (1) whether the facts, taken in the light most favorable to the plaintiff, show that the official's conduct violated a constitutional right; and (2) whether that right was clearly established at the time. Id.

The "clearly established" requirement does not demand that there be a case directly on point, but the "contours of the right must be sufficiently clear that every reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 670 (2009), quoting Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987). This is an objective standard that protects officials who make reasonable mistakes about the law. Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982).

### B. The Right Allegedly Violated Was Not Clearly Established

Doe's substantive due process claim rests on the alleged right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience." However, the district court erred in concluding that this right was clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct in 2005-2006.

The "shock the conscience" standard is inherently fact-specific and requires a high degree of culpability. County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 846-47 (1998). The Supreme Court has recognized that this standard requires conduct that "shocks the conscience" and involves "execrable" or "outrageous" behavior that "violates the decencies of civilized conduct." Id. at 846, quoting Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952).

No prior case held that the specific conduct alleged here—detention, interrogation without counsel, and conditions of confinement in a military context—violated the Constitution under this standard. The district court's conclusion that the right was clearly established is unsupported by precedent and ignores the requirement that the contours of the right must be sufficiently clear.

### C. Secretary Rumsfeld's Actions Were Objectively Reasonable

Secretary Rumsfeld, as Secretary of Defense, was responsible for establishing policies regarding detention and interrogation of enemy combatants and other detainees in the Iraq conflict. The decisions made at the policy level are entitled to substantial deference and cannot be second-guessed through Bivens actions. See Wood v. Moss, 572 U.S. ___, ___ (2014) (recognizing that officials are entitled to qualified immunity for policy-level decisions).

Doe's allegations, even accepted as true, do not establish that Secretary Rumsfeld personally participated in the alleged constitutional violations. Rather, Doe alleges that subordinates engaged in certain conduct. A superior official cannot be held liable under Bivens absent direct personal participation in the constitutional violation. See Iqbal, 556 U.S. at 677 ("Government officials may not be held liable for the unconstitutional conduct of their subordinates under a theory of respondeat superior.").

These are separate inquiries. Under Ziglar, even if a constitutional violation is alleged, a Bivens remedy may not be available if special factors counsel hesitation—which they do here.

Second, the plaintiff ignores the Supreme Court's repeated admonitions that Bivens is a narrow exception that should not be expanded beyond the three contexts where it has been recognized. Every attempt to extend Bivens to new contexts has been rejected by the Supreme Court. See, e.g., Wilkie v. Robbins, 551 U.S. 537 (2007); FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471 (1994). The plaintiff's request would mark an unprecedented expansion of Bivens that the Supreme Court has explicitly declined to authorize.

Third, the plaintiff's reliance on the "shock the conscience" standard does not establish a clearly established right. This standard is so vague and fact-specific that it provides little guidance to officials in the field. As the Supreme Court has recognized, qualified immunity protects officials who make reasonable mistakes about the law, and the "shock the conscience" standard provides insufficient clarity to put officials on notice that their conduct is unlawful.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court** is an interlocutory appeal from the United States District Court for the District of Columbia. The district court denied Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss Appellee John Doe’s substantive due process claims asserted under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971). The district court held that Doe could maintain a *Bivens* action for his alleged detention and interrogation in Iraq and that Secretary Rumsfeld was not entitled to qualified immunity. For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the order of the district court.

**I. Standard of Review**

We review *de novo* the district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity and the legal question of whether a *Bivens* remedy is available. See *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008). In reviewing a motion to dismiss, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. See *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009).

**II. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The primary question before this Court is whether the district court erred in implying a cause of action under *Bivens* in the context of Doe’s alleged detention and interrogation by military personnel in a war zone.

The Supreme Court has recognized that *Bivens* actions are a "limited exception" to the separation of powers principle that Congress, not the courts, is the body responsible for authorizing damages remedies against the United States. See *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 582 U.S. ___ (2017). The Court has extended *Bivens* remedies to only three specific contexts: (1) Fourth Amendment violations by federal agents (*Bivens*, 403 U.S. at 388); (2) Eighth Amendment violations regarding prison conditions (*Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980)); and (3) Fifth Amendment violations in the context of gender discrimination (*Davis v. Passman*, 442 U.S. 228 (1979)). See *Ziglar*, 582 U.S. at ___.

The arguments presented establish that Doe’s claims arise in a "new context" distinct from these three recognized scenarios. The alleged conduct involves the detention, interrogation, and confinement of a civilian contractor by military officials in a theater of armed conflict. This context is fundamentally different from the civilian law enforcement or prison administration contexts previously addressed by the Supreme Court.

When a plaintiff seeks to assert a *Bivens* claim in a new context, the Court must determine whether there are "special factors counselling hesitation" before implying a damages remedy. *Ziglar*, 582 U.S. at ___; see also *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). The arguments presented persuasively demonstrate that such special factors are present here.

First, the military and national security context of this case weighs heavily against implying a remedy. As noted in *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988), the executive branch possesses "broad authority" over national security and military operations. The judiciary is ill-equipped to second-guess the decisions of military officials regarding the detention and interrogation of individuals in a combat zone. See *Ziglar*, 582 U.S. at ___.

Second, separation of powers concerns counsel hesitation. Subjecting high-level executive officials to personal liability for decisions made in the conduct of military operations would intrude upon the executive branch's constitutional functions. See *Nixon v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 731, 754 (1982).

Third, Congress has not provided a damages remedy for the conduct alleged, and the Detainee Treatment Act, 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., does not create a private cause of action. Congressional silence in this area, particularly regarding the conduct of military operations, is a significant factor counseling against the creation of a judicial remedy. See *Ziglar*, 582 U.S. at ___.

Finally, the existence of alternative remedial structures, such as administrative channels and congressional oversight, further supports the conclusion that a *Bivens* remedy is inappropriate. See *Wilkie*, 551 U.S. at 550.

Because this case presents a new context involving national security and military operations, and because special factors counselling hesitation are present, the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action.

**III. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Alternatively, even if a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages unless the official violated a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. *Saucier v. Katz*, 533 U.S. 194, 201 (2001); *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982).

The arguments presented demonstrate that the right Doe alleges—the right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience" in a military context—was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct in 2005 and 2006. The "shock the conscience" standard is inherently fact-specific and requires a high degree of culpability. See *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846-47 (1998).

To be clearly established, the contours of the right must be sufficiently clear that "every reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 670 (2009) (quoting *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987)). The arguments correctly note that no prior case held that the specific conduct alleged here—detention and interrogation of a civilian contractor in a war zone—violated the Constitution under the "shock the conscience" standard. The absence of controlling authority means that a reasonable official in Secretary Rumsfeld's position could not have known that his actions were unlawful.

Furthermore, Doe’s allegations do not establish that Secretary Rumsfeld personally participated in the alleged constitutional violations. Government officials may not be held liable for the unconstitutional conduct of their subordinates under a theory of respondeat superior. *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 677. As a high-level policymaker, Secretary Rumsfeld’s decisions regarding detention policies are entitled to deference and are shielded by qualified immunity in the absence of clearly established law. See *Wood v. Moss*, 572 U.S. ___ (2014).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy in this new context involving military detention and national security. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Accordingly, we reverse the order of the district court denying Secretary Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss and remand with instructions to dismiss Doe’s *Bivens* claims.

## II. THIS CASE PRESENTS A NEW CONTEXT FOR BIVENS PURPOSES

### A. The appellee's reliance on *Carlson v. Green* is misplaced

The appellee's argument that his claims are "indistinguishable" from *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the "new context" analysis mandated by *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 582 U.S. ___ (2017). In *Carlson*, the Supreme Court recognized a Bivens remedy for Eighth Amendment claims arising from the failure to provide adequate medical treatment to a federal prisoner in a domestic correctional facility. The context there involved routine prison administration subject to judicial oversight.

The appellee's claims arise from the detention and interrogation of a civilian contractor by military personnel in Iraq—a theater of armed conflict. This context bears no meaningful resemblance to the domestic prison setting addressed in *Carlson*. As this Court recognized in *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008), while Bivens is not "limited to the exact factual scenario of prior cases," the constitutional violation must be of the same "type" and arise in a comparable context. The detention of enemy combatants or suspected threats in a military theater is fundamentally distinct from the administration of a civilian prison.

The appellee's attempt to conflate these contexts ignores the explicit language of *Ziglar*, which emphasized that courts must examine whether the claims arise in a "different context" involving "different actors" or "different interests." 582 U.S. at ___. Here, the actors are military officials operating under wartime authorities; the interests involve national security and the conduct of armed conflict; and the location is a foreign theater of operations. These differences are dispositive.

### B. The special factors counseling hesitation are concrete and specific

The appellee's dismissal of special factors as inapplicable misapplies *Ziglar* and *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007). In *Wilkie*, the Supreme Court instructed that courts must identify "concrete and specific" factors counseling hesitation. Here, such factors abound:

**1. Military and National Security Context**

The appellee's claims directly implicate decisions made in the conduct of military operations. As the Supreme Court held in *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988), the executive branch possesses "broad authority" over national security and military operations. The judiciary lacks competence to second-guess detention decisions made in a combat zone where the safety of military personnel and the success of military operations may be at stake. The appellee's characterization of these as "routine constitutional violations" ignores the reality that military detention involves battlefield assessments of threat levels that civilian courts are ill-equipped to evaluate.

**2. Separation of Powers**

The appellee's argument that "no separation of powers concerns arise" contradicts settled precedent. In *Nixon v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 731, 754 (1982), the Supreme Court recognized that exposing high-level executive officials to personal liability for official decisions would intrude upon the executive branch's constitutional functions. This concern is heightened where, as here, the official is the Secretary of Defense making policy decisions regarding the treatment of detainees in an ongoing military conflict.

**3. Congressional Silence**

The appellee's argument that congressional silence "weighs against hesitation" misreads *Ziglar*. In *Ziglar*, the Court noted that congressional silence is a factor to consider—neither automatically supporting nor automatically opposing a Bivens remedy. However, where Congress has specifically addressed the subject matter through legislation like the Detainee Treatment Act and chosen not to create a private damages remedy, this silence is meaningful. The Detainee Treatment Act, 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., specifically addressed the treatment of detainees but did not create a private cause of action. This legislative choicecounsel judicial restraint.

**4. Alternative Remedial Structures**

The existence of administrative channels, congressional oversight, and military justice mechanisms provides alternative avenues for addressing the conduct alleged. While these may not provide the same remedy as a Bivens damages action, their existence supports hesitation before judicially creating a new damages remedy. See *Wilkie*, 551 U.S. at 550.

## III. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

### A. The "shock the conscience" standard was not clearly established in the military detention context

The appellee's argument that the "shock the conscience" standard was "clearly established" conflates the existence of a general standard with its application to the specific context at issue. While *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), established that substantive due process violations must "shock the conscience," this standard is inherently fact-specific and was never applied to the detention of a civilian contractor in a military theater during active hostilities.

To defeat qualified immunity, the right must be "clearly established" in a manner that "every reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 670 (2009) (quoting *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987)). This requires " preexisting law" that "dictates, virtually to a certainty, that the officer's conduct violates the Constitution." *Id.* at 640.

No binding precedent in 2005-2006 held that the detention of a civilian contractor in Iraq, under the circumstances alleged, violated the Due Process Clause. The appellee's reliance on *Rochin v. A reasonable official in Secretary Rumsfeld's position could not have known that the specific conduct alleged would violate the Constitution.

The "shock the conscience" standard requires a "high degree of culpability." *Lewis*, 523 U.S. at 846-47. In the military context, where quick decisions regarding detention of suspected threats are necessary, the standard requires even more specific guidance than in civilian contexts. Without binding precedent establishing that specific wartime detention practices violate substantive due process, qualified immunity applies.

### B. The appellee's allegations fail to establish Rumsfeld's personal participation

The appellee's argument that he pled sufficient facts to show Rumsfeld's "personal involvement" misapplies *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662 (2009). The defense correctly notes that *Wood v. Moss*, 572 U.S. ___ (2014), supports qualified immunity for high-level officials when the law is not clearly established. **UPON CONSIDERATION** of the rebuttals filed by both Appellee John Doe and Appellant Donald Rumsfeld, and for the reasons set forth below, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling reversing the district court's order denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss.

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The Appellee argues that this case falls within the established *Bivens* context recognized in *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), which involved the failure to provide adequate medical care to a federal prisoner. The Appellee contends that because his claims involve "inhumane conditions of confinement," they are functionally identical to those in *Carlson*. We are not persuaded.

As the Appellant correctly notes, the Supreme Court's decision in *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 582 U.S. ___ (2017), requires courts to determine whether a case presents a "new context" before implying a *Bivens* remedy. This analysis extends beyond merely identifying the type of constitutional violation; it requires examining the "actors" involved and the "interests" at stake. *Ziglar*, 582 U.S. at ___. The context here—the detention and interrogation of a civilian contractor by military personnel in a theater of armed conflict—is fundamentally distinct from the domestic prison administration context of *Carlson*. As this Court recognized in *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008), while *Bivens* is not limited to exact factual scenarios, the violation must arise in a comparable context. The military detention of individuals in a war zone involves battlefield assessments and national security concerns wholly absent in the operation of a civilian prison.

Furthermore, "special factors counselling hesitation" are plainly present. The Appellee's assertion that "no separation of powers concerns arise" ignores the executive branch's "broad authority" over national security and military operations. *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988). Subjecting high-level military officials to personal liability for detention decisions made in a combat zone would intrude upon the executive branch's constitutional functions. *Nixon v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 731, 754 (1982). While the Appellee notes the absence of an alternative damages remedy, this factor does not outweigh the significant institutional competence and separation-of-powers concerns implicated here. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550 (2007) (requiring "concrete and specific" factors). The existence of administrative channels and congressional oversight further supports the conclusion that the judiciary should not imply a remedy in this sphere. Accordingly, the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even assuming a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The Appellee argues that the "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established by *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), and cases such as *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), and *Hudson v. McMillian*, 503 U.S. 1 (1992). However, the Appellee's reliance on these domestic cases is misplaced.

To defeat qualified immunity, the right violated must be "clearly established" such that "every reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates that right." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 670 (2009) (quoting *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987)). The Appellee has not pointed to any precedent in 2005 or 2006 establishing that the detention and interrogation of a civilian contractor in a war zone violated substantive due process. The "shock the conscience" standard is inherently fact-specific and requires a high degree of culpability. *Lewis*, 523 U.S. at 846-47. Applying this standard to the unique circumstances of military detention in Iraq was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct. *Wood v. Moss*, 572 U.S. ___ (2014).

Finally, the Appellee's allegations fail to establish Secretary Rumsfeld's personal participation. The Appellee claims that Rumsfeld "directly authorized interrogation and detention protocols," but *Iqbal* makes clear that a plaintiff must allege more than conclusory statements that a high-level policymaker authorized the general policies under which the violation occurred. *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 677. The complaint lacks specific facts showing that Secretary Rumsfeld personally directed the alleged abuses of Doe. Mere supervisory liability is insufficient to state a claim under *Bivens*.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Appellee's arguments fail to demonstrate that the district court correctly implied a *Bivens* remedy or that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity. We therefore **AFFIRM** the preliminary ruling reversing the district court's order denying the motion to dismiss.

**FINAL RULING**

**UPON CONSIDERATION** of the closing arguments submitted by the parties, and for the reasons set forth below, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling reversing the district court's order denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss.

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The Appellant argues, and the Court agrees, that the district court erred in implying a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971). The Supreme Court's decision in *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 582 U.S. 120, 137 S.Ct. 1843, 198 L.Ed.2d 285 (2017), requires a two-step analysis for extending *Bivens* to new contexts. First, we must ask whether the case presents a new context that is different in a meaningful way from previous *Bivens* cases decided by the Supreme Court. *Ziglar*, 582 U.S. at 136-37. If the case is a new context, we must then ask whether special factors counsel hesitation before implying a remedy. *Id.*

This case presents a "new context" distinct from the *Bivens* action recognized in *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 100 S.Ct. 1018, 64 L.Ed.2d 347 (1980). *Carlson* involved the failure of prison officials to provide adequate medical care to a federal inmate. The context there was the routine administration of a domestic prison. Here, by contrast, the Appellee alleges detention and interrogation by military personnel in a theater of armed conflict. As noted in *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008), while *Bivens* does not require identical factual scenarios, the constitutional violation must arise in a "comparable context." The military detention of a civilian contractor in a war zone involves battlefield assessments and national security concerns wholly absent in the operation of a civilian prison.

Furthermore, "special factors counselling hesitation" are plainly present. The Appellant correctly identifies the executive branch's broad authority over national security and military operations. *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530, 108 S.Ct. 2422, 101 L.Ed.2d 450 (1988). Subjecting high-level military officials to personal liability for detention decisions made in a combat zone would intrude upon the executive branch's constitutional functions. *Nixon v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 731, 754, 102 S.Ct. 2690, 73 L.Ed.2d 349 (1982). The judiciary lacks competence to second-guess such battlefield decisions. The existence of administrative channels and congressional oversight further supports the conclusion that the judiciary should not imply a remedy in this sphere. *Wilkie v. Robbins*, 551 U.S. 537, 550, 127 S.Ct. 2588, 168 L.Ed.2d 389 (2007). Accordingly, the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even assuming a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. To overcome qualified immunity, the Appellee must show that the right violated was "clearly established" at the time of the conduct. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). The Appellee relies on the "shock the conscience" standard from *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846-47, 118 S.Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998), and cases such as *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172, 72 S.Ct. 205, 96 L.Ed. 183 (1952), and *Hudson v. McMillian*, 503 U.S. 1, 5-6, 112 S.Ct. 995, 117 L.Ed.2d 156 (1992). However, these cases involve domestic law enforcement or prison contexts, not the detention of a civilian contractor in a war zone.

No precedent in 2005 or 2006 established that the detention and interrogation of a civilian contractor by military personnel in a war zone violated substantive due process. The "shock the conscience" standard is inherently fact-specific and requires a high degree of culpability. *Lewis*, 523 U.S. at 846-47. Applying this standard to the unique circumstances of military detention in Iraq was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct. *Wood v. Moss*, 572 U.S. 744, 756-57, 134 S.Ct. 2056, 189 L.Ed.2d 62 (2014). Therefore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity.

Finally, the Appellee's allegations fail to establish Secretary Rumsfeld's personal participation. The Appellee claims that Rumsfeld "directly authorized interrogation and detention protocols," but *Iqbal* makes clear that a plaintiff must allege more than conclusory statements that a high-level policymaker authorized the general policies under which the violation occurred. *Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 677. The complaint lacks specific facts showing that Secretary Rumsfeld personally directed the alleged abuses of Doe. Mere supervisory liability is insufficient to state a claim under *Bivens*.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Appellee's arguments fail to demonstrate that the district court correctly implied a *Bivens* remedy or that Secretary Rumsfeld is not entitled to qualified immunity. We therefore **AFFIRM** the preliminary ruling reversing the district court's order denying the motion to dismiss.
