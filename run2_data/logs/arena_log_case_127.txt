## I. Statutory Interpretation: A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) Must Be Applied Gender-Neutrally

### A. The Constitutional Imperative of Obergefell v. Hodges

The prosecution erroneously contends that Obergefell v. Hodges, 576 U.S. 644 (2015), only requires procedural equality and does not authorize courts to interpret statutes gender-neutrally. This argument fundamentally misunderstands the Supreme Court's holding and its implications for Arizona's parentage statutes.

In Obergefell, the Supreme Court held that same-sex couples have a fundamental right to marry, and this right encompasses "the constellation of benefits that the States have linked to marriage." 576 U.S. at 665. The Court explicitly recognized that parenthood is among those benefits, noting that marriage "transforms the marriage into a formal, legal recognition of a relationship that creates a bundle of legal rights and benefits." Id. at 665.

The prosecution's argument that Obergefell does not authorize judicial alteration of substantive statutory terms conflates two distinct concepts. The question before this Court is not whether to rewrite a statute, but whether to interpret existing statutory language in a manner consistent with constitutional mandates. As the Arizona Supreme Court recognized in State v. Garcia, 181 Ariz. 235, 889 P.2d 601 (App. 1994), statutes must be interpreted to avoid unconstitutional results. To interpret A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) as applying only to opposite-sex couples would deny same-sex spouses the equal benefits of marriage, thereby creating an unconstitutional classification that violates the Equal Protection Clause.

The prosecution's reliance on State v. Williams, 125 Ariz. 104, 608 P.2d 15 (1980), is misplaced. While this Court emphasized that statutory interpretation begins with plain language, Williams also acknowledged that courts must interpret statutes to avoid absurd results. Applying a gender-specific interpretation that denies same-sex spouses the presumption of parentage produces precisely the absurd result Williams cautioned against.

### B. McLaughlin v. Jones Provides Correct Guidance

The prosecution argues that McLaughlin v. Jones, 240 Ariz. 560, 382 P.3d 118 (App. 2016), is not binding because the Arizona Supreme Court granted review. However, this Court correctly noted in its ruling that regardless of McLaughlin's precedential status, the constitutional requirement for equal treatment independently supports the family court's ruling.

McLaughlin held that A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) must be read and applied gender-neutrally. 240 Ariz. at 564 ¶ 14, 382 P.3d at 122. This holding represents the correct interpretation of the statute in light of Obergefell. The prosecution's attempt to distinguish McLaughlin by arguing it was "wrongly decided" ignores the sound constitutional reasoning underlying that decision.

Even if this Court has concerns about McLaughlin's precedential status, the doctrine of stare decisis counsels deference to its reasoning. More importantly, the constitutional analysis in Obergefell independently supports reading A.R.S. § 25-814(A)(1) gender-neutrally. This Court need not rely solely on McLaughlin to reach the correct result.

### C. Pre-Obergefell Cases Are Inapplicable

The prosecution's reliance on In re Marriage of Babb, 152 Ariz. 391, 732 P.2d 1079 (App. 1987), and State v. In Babb, this Court addressed whether a man could claim paternity of a child born to his wife through artificial insemination. 152 Ariz. at 393, 732 P.2d at 1081. This holding turned on the absence of biological connection, not on gender-specific statutory language.

Similarly, in Superior Court, the court addressed whether a man could claim paternity of a child born during a non-marital relationship. 180 Ariz. at 23, 881 P.2d at 1103. Neither Babb nor Superior Court considered the question now before this Court: whether the presumption of parentage applies equally to same-sex spouses.

These cases cannot control the outcome here because they were decided in a legal landscape that did not recognize same-sex marriage. Following Obergefell, statutes governing marital relationships must be applied equally to all legally married couples, regardless of gender.

## II. Equitable Estoppel Properly Applies to Prevent Turner from Denying Oakley's Parental Status

### A. The Elements of Equitable Estoppel Are Satisfied

The doctrine of equitable estoppel requires: (1) a representation by the party to be estopped, (2) reasonable reliance by the other party, and (3) resulting detriment. Contractors Sales Corp. v. Jensen, 8 Ariz. App. 222, 445 P.2d 190 (1968). All three elements are satisfied here.

**First, Turner made clear representations of Oakley's parental status.** The record demonstrates that Turner: (1) listed Oakley on C.T.'s birth certificate in the section designated "father"; (2) referred to Oakley as "mom" on social media posts; (3) executed a will naming Oakley as the child's guardian if Turner were to die; and (4) informed hospital staff that Oakley should be listed on the birth certificate. These representations were explicit and unambiguous.

**Second, Oakley reasonably relied on Turner's representations.** Oakley actively participated in the child's conception, including reviewing sperm donor profiles, accompanying Turner to medical appointments, and being present during the insemination procedures. Oakley was present at C.T.'s birth and cut the umbilical cord. Following C.T.'s birth, Oakley participated fully in raising the child as a co-parent. Every aspect of the parties' relationship and conduct reflected Turner's representation that Oakley was C.T.'s legal parent.

**Third, significant detriment would result if Turner is permitted to rebut the presumption.** Allowing Turner to now deny Oakley's parental status would deprive C.T. of a legal relationship with one of the two parents who raised him. It would also deprive Oakley of the parental rights she reasonably believed she possessed based on Turner's representations. The harm to both Oakley and C.T. is substantial and ongoing.

### B. The Prosecution Misreads In re Pima County Juvenile Action

The prosecution argues that In re Pima County Juvenile Action No. J-73421, 140 Ariz. 347, 682 P.2d 404 (App. 1984), prohibits the use of estoppel to create parentage where no statutory basis exists. This argument mischaracterizes the family court's ruling.

Pima County Juvenile addressed whether estoppel could create a parent-child relationship where no statutory basis existed at all. 140 Ariz. at 350, 682 P.2d at 407. Here, by contrast, a statutory basis exists—the presumption of parentage applies to Oakley as Turner's spouse. The question is whether Turner can rebut that presumption, not whether Oakley can claim parentage absent statutory authority.
