### II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

### III. ARGUMENT

#### A. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN ENTERING DEFAULT JUDGMENT PURSUANT TO PA.R.C.P. 4019

Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019 provides for sanctions for failure to comply with discovery orders, but the sanctions must be proportionate to the violation. As this Court held in *Floyd v. McCloskey*, 556 Pa. 73, 726 A.2d 1052 (1999), "the court must balance the equities and consider the nature and severity of the violation in determining what sanction is appropriate." The sanction of default judgment is the most severe available and should be imposed only in extreme circumstances where lesser sanctions would be futile and the moving party has suffered severe prejudice that cannot be cured.

In *Kline v. Old Governors Club*, 456 Pa. 470, 322 A.2d 86 (1974), this Court emphasized that dismissal with prejudice is a drastic remedy that should be reserved for cases where the party's conduct has made it impossible for the opposing party to proceed. The moving party bears the burden of demonstrating that the violation was willful and that lesser sanctions would be futile.

Here, the trial court failed to properly balance the equities. The record demonstrates that while Defendant may have failed to appear for scheduled depositions, the circumstances surrounding these failures must be examined in context. Defendant was not properly served at all times, and his counsel indicated on multiple occasions that he was unable to locate Defendant. The discovery disputes in this case occurred over a period of years, and Plaintiff failed to take timely action to compel compliance or seek sanctions until after Defendant's death.

#### B. THE PLAINTIFF HAS FAILED TO ESTABLISH THE PREJUDICE NECESSARY TO JUSTIFY DEFAULT JUDGMENT

The plaintiff argues that Defendant's failure to appear for depositions caused severe and incurable prejudice. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the prejudice claimed by Plaintiff is not the result of Defendant's discovery conduct—it is the result of Defendant's death in January 2012. Defendant cannot be held responsible for his own death, which occurred naturally and was not caused by any deliberate act to evade discovery. As the Court held in *D'Ambrosio v. Pennsylvania National Mutual Casualty Insurance Co.*, 494 Pa. 501, 431 A.2d 966 (1981), prejudice must be directly attributable to the discovery violation, not to subsequent events beyond the party's control.

Second, Plaintiff has not demonstrated that Defendant's deposition testimony would have been material to his case. The facts establish that the fire originated from an unattended pot on the stove and spread rapidly due to kerosene leaking from a defective container stored near the stove. These facts were established through the investigation of fire officials, not through Defendant's testimony. There is no evidence in the record suggesting that Defendant had any involvement in starting the fire or any knowledge of the kerosene container's defective condition.

Third, Defendant did provide an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire, in which he denied being the cause of the fire. While this statement may not constitute sworn testimony, it nonetheless provides evidence of Defendant's position regarding his involvement in the incident.

#### C. THE EVIDENCE IN THE RECORD DOES NOT SUPPORT A FINDING THAT DEFENDANT WAS NEGLIGENT OR THAT HIS CONDUCT WAS A SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR IN CAUSING THE FIRE

The trial court erred in failing to consider the substantive evidence regarding the cause of the fire. The undisputed facts establish that the fire was caused by (1) an unattended pot on the electric stove, and (2) kerosene leaking from a defective container stored near the stove.

The record contains no evidence that Defendant placed the pot on the stove, that Defendant was responsible for the defective kerosene container, or that Defendant had any involvement in causing the fire to start or spread. Defendant was asleep on the couch at the time the fire started. He had consumed a six-pack of beer, but there is no evidence that his consumption of alcohol caused the fire or contributed to its origin.

As this Court held in *Ruberto v. Constable*, 488 Pa. 77, 410 A.2d 744 (1980), and subsequently affirmed in *Knight v. Jewett*, 534 Pa. 320, 632 A.2d 1110 (1993), a defendant cannot be held liable for negligence unless the plaintiff establishes that the defendant's conduct was a substantial factor in causing the harm. Here, Plaintiff has failed to present any evidence establishing that Defendant's conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire that killed Ms. Plotts.

The trial court's entry of default judgment effectively presumed negligence without any evidentiary foundation. This was error.

#### D. THE TRIAL COURT FAILED TO PROPERLY CONSIDER LESSER SANCTIONS

Even assuming arguendo that Defendant violated discovery orders, the trial court was required to consider lesser sanctions before entering default judgment. In *Butch v. City of Philadelphia*, 556 Pa. 524, 729 A.2d 1191 (1999), this Court held that "the court must impose the least severe sanction that will accomplish the desired purpose" and that dismissal is appropriate only when lesser sanctions would be futile.

The trial court had numerous lesser sanctions available, including:

1. Monetary sanctions or fines for each missed deposition;
2. An order precluding Defendant from presenting certain defenses or evidence;
3. An order that certain facts be deemed established against Defendant;
4. An order striking all or part of Defendant's pleadings;
5. Contempt of court proceedings with appropriate penalties.

The trial court failed to explain why these lesser sanctions would have been futile. Instead, the court jumped directly to the most severe sanction available—default judgment—without adequate consideration of alternatives.

#### E. PLAINTIFF CONTRIBUTED TO ANY ALLEGED PREJUDICE BY FAILING TO DILIGENTLY PURSUE DISCOVERY

The record demonstrates that Plaintiff failed to diligently pursue Defendant's deposition before his death. Plaintiff first requested a deposition in June 2007, but Defendant died in January 2012—nearly five years later. During this time, Plaintiff could have sought court intervention earlier, requested alternative discovery methods, or moved for sanctions before Defendant's death.

In *Holschuh v. Holland*, 545 Pa. 227, 681 A.2d 707 (1996), this Court recognized that the moving party's diligence is relevant in determining whether sanctions are appropriate. A party who fails to diligently pursue discovery cannot later claim prejudice when the opposing party becomes unavailable.

Here, Plaintiff waited years before filing motions for contempt and sanctions. By the time Plaintiff sought the extreme remedy of default judgment, Defendant had already died. This timing is not coincidental—it reflects Plaintiff's own failure to take timely action to protect his interests.

#### F. THE PLAINTIFF'S RELIANCE ON IN RE: ESTATE OF KLINE IS MISPLACED

Plaintiff cites *In re: Estate of Kline*, 552 Pa. 132, 713 A.2d 648 (1998), for the proposition that persistent noncompliance with discovery orders justifies severe sanctions. However, Kline is distinguishable.

In Kline, the Court affirmed the dismissal of a party's claims where that party had repeatedly ignored discovery orders over an extended period and had demonstrated a pattern of willful defiance. Critically, however, the party in Kline was still alive and available to participate in litigation. The Court in Kline emphasized that the party's "persistent noncompliance" was "willful" and "without justification."

Here, by contrast, Defendant died in January 2012. The alleged discovery violations occurred before his death, and Defendant was not deliberately evading discovery—he was simply unavailable. Moreover, the trial court in this case failed to make specific findings that Defendant's conduct was willful in the same manner as the conduct at issue in Kline.

The trial court failed to properly consider lesser sanctions. And Plaintiff himself contributed to any alleged prejudice by failing to diligently pursue discovery before Defendant's death.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to Pa. R.A.P. 1928**

**Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, Administrator of the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer v. Joseph Michael Angelichio**

**No.**

**OPINION**

**Before the Court**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on an interlocutory appeal by Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer ("Defendant"), challenging the trial court’s entry of default judgment against him as a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders. The trial court imposed the sanction pursuant to Pa. R.C.P. 4019 following Defendant’s persistent failure to appear for a videotaped deposition. For the reasons that follow, the trial court’s order is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The grant of a default judgment as a discovery sanction is committed to the sound discretion of the trial court. An appellate court will not reverse the decision of the trial court absent a clear abuse of discretion. See *In re: Estate of Kline*, 552 Pa. 132, 713 A.2d 648 (1998). An abuse of discretion is not merely an error in judgment, but rather a misapplication of the law or an unreasonable exercise of judicial power.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Finding Defendant’s Conduct Willful**

The trial court’s determination that Defendant engaged in willful noncompliance is supported by the record. Pennsylvania law is clear that "persistent noncompliance with court-ordered discovery, especially after explicit warnings, demonstrates a level of defiance that justifies severe sanctions." *In re: Estate of Kline*, 552 Pa. 132, 713 A.2d 648 (1998). Here, the record establishes a history of obstruction spanning several years. While Defendant’s counsel initially cited an inability to locate the Defendant as the reason for missed depositions in 2007 and 2008, the nature of the noncompliance shifted in 2009 after Plaintiff successfully located the Defendant.

Following the court’s explicit orders in July 2009 and August 2010 requiring Defendant to appear for a videotaped deposition, Defendant actively refused to comply. The most damning evidence is the January 28, 2011 correspondence from Defendant’s counsel, stating, "obviously, Mr. Diefenderfer will not appear Monday, nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled." This unequivocal statement demonstrates a conscious decision to defy the court’s authority. As held in *Holschuh v. Holland*, 545 Pa. 227, 681 A.2d 707 (1996), "a party’s intentional avoidance of discovery, particularly after judicial orders, constitutes willfulness warranting dismissal." The trial court correctly identified this conduct as willful and in bad faith.

**B. The Prejudice to Plaintiff Was Severe and Incurable**

The trial court properly found that Plaintiff suffered severe and incurable prejudice. Prejudice under Rule 4019 exists when the discovery violation "renders the opposing party unable to present its case fully." *D’Ambrosio v. Pennsylvania National Mutual Casualty Insurance Co.*, 494 Pa. 501, 431 A.2d 966 (1981). Defendant was the sole individual present in the residence at the time of the fire other than the decedent. His testimony was critical to establishing the facts surrounding the origin of the fire and the condition of the premises.

Defendant argues that any prejudice resulted from his death in January 2012, rather than his discovery violations. This argument is unpersuasive. The prejudice stems from the inability to obtain testimony. Defendant’s refusal to appear for depositions in 2009, 2010, and 2011—while he was alive—directly caused the absence of sworn testimony. Had Defendant complied with the court orders when they were issued, the testimony would have been secured prior to his death. Because Defendant chose to obstruct discovery until his death, the prejudice is directly attributable to his conduct. The death of the party merely rendered the prejudice incurable; it did not cause it.

**C. Lesser Sanctions Would Have Been Futile**

The trial court acted within its discretion in concluding that lesser sanctions would be futile. Pennsylvania law mandates that "the court must impose the least severe sanction that will accomplish the desired purpose," and dismissal is appropriate only when lesser sanctions would be futile. *Butch v. City of Philadelphia*, 556 Pa. 524, 729 A.2d 1191 (1999). The record reflects that the trial court attempted to accommodate Defendant’s concerns regarding the videotaping of the deposition by restricting the tape’s use in the August 25, 2010 order. Despite this accommodation and multiple court orders, Defendant explicitly refused to appear.

Given Defendant’s categorical refusal to participate—"nor any other time"—monetary sanctions or further continuances would not have compelled compliance. The trial court was not required to engage in a futile exercise of imposing fines on a party who had already signaled his intent to permanently defy the court’s orders. The finding of futility is amply supported by the record.

**D. The Trial Court Properly Balanced the Equities**

Defendant argues that the trial court failed to balance the equities and that the evidence did not support a finding of negligence. However, the entry of default judgment as a discovery sanction is not a ruling on the merits of the negligence claim, but rather a remedy for the destruction of the adversarial process. As noted in *Floyd v. McCloskey*, 556 Pa. 73, 726 A.2d 1052 (1999), the court must balance the nature of the violation against the severity of the sanction. Here, the violation was total and persistent, effectively preventing Plaintiff from proving his case. The defense’s argument regarding the lack of evidence of negligence is misplaced because the lack of evidence is the direct result of Defendant’s obstruction. The trial court did not presume negligence; it sanctioned Defendant for preventing Plaintiff from discovering the facts necessary to prove or disprove it.

Furthermore, the defense’s contention that Plaintiff failed to diligently pursue discovery is contradicted by the record. Plaintiff hired a private investigator to locate Defendant and filed multiple motions for contempt. The delay in securing the deposition was largely driven by Defendant’s evasiveness and subsequent refusal to comply.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court carefully considered the history of noncompliance, the explicit refusal to obey court orders, and the resulting prejudice to Plaintiff. The sanction of default judgment was a proportional response to Defendant’s willful obstruction of the discovery process. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that lesser sanctions would have been futile and that the extreme remedy was necessary to address the egregious conduct.

Accordingly, the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, are affirmed.

## DEFENSE REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S REBUTTAL BRIEF

---

### I. INTRODUCTION

Plaintiff's rebuttal mischaracterizes the nature of this appeal and ignores fundamental principles governing the proportionality of discovery sanctions. The trial court's entry of default judgment as a sanction for discovery violations, while the underlying defendant is deceased and cannot offer testimony to benefit or harm either party, constitutes a manifest abuse of discretion that this Court should reverse. Plaintiff conflates the procedural issue of discovery compliance with the substantive merits of the negligence claim, but the critical question remains: whether the extreme sanction of default judgment is proportionate to the alleged violation when the defendant is deceased and the prejudice alleged is, in fact, curable through other means of proof.

---

### II. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY IMPOSING THE MOST SEVERE SANCTION AVAILABLE WHEN THE DEFENDANT IS DECEASED AND CANNOT BENEFIT FROM NON-COMPLIANCE

Plaintiff's reliance on *In re: Estate of Kline*, 552 Pa. 132, 713 A.2d 648 (1998), while understandable, misses a critical distinguishing factor: the defendant in *Kline* was alive and capable of benefiting from his obstruction. Here, Tyrone Diefenderfer died in January 2012, and the default judgment entered against his estate benefits no one—not the decedent, who cannot testify, and not the plaintiff, who cannot obtain the testimony that was the supposed object of the obstruction.

The purpose of discovery sanctions is to compel compliance and preserve the ability of parties to present their cases. *See* *Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019 (Committee Note)*. When a party cannot be compelled to comply because they are deceased, the rationale for severe sanctions evaporates. The estate has no personal ability to compel the decedent's testimony, and the sanction of default judgment serves no remedial purpose—it is purely punitive against a party who cannot possibly benefit from non-compliance.

This Court should hold that where a discovery sanction is sought against a deceased party's estate, the trial court must demonstrate extraordinary justification for the most severe sanctions, and must consider whether the sanction bears any reasonable relationship to the goals of discovery enforcement. *See* *Floyd v. McCloskey*, 556 Pa. 73, 726 A.2d 1052 (1999)* (requiring proportionality analysis between violation and sanction)*.

---

### III. THE PREJUDICE ALLEGED BY PLAINTIFF IS NOT INCURABLE AND CAN BE ADDRESSED THROUGH OTHER EVIDENCE

Plaintiff claims he suffered "severe and incurable prejudice" because defendant was "the sole individual present in the residence at the time of the fire other than the decedent." This overstates both the importance of defendant's testimony and the uniqueness of evidence available to plaintiff.

The record demonstrates that the cause and origin of the fire were determined by fire officials. The fire marshal's investigation established that the fire originated from an unattended pot on the stove and was exacerbated by a defective kerosene container. These findings do not depend on defendant's testimony. Additionally, the landlords, Betsy Jo Myers and Joanne E. Myers, who owned the premises, remain available as witnesses and can testify regarding the condition of the stove, the storage of the kerosene container, and the premises generally.

Plaintiff's theory of the case—that defendant's cooking caused the fire—is not dependent solely on defendant's admission. The fire marshall's report, physical evidence from the scene, and testimony from fire personnel provide alternative means of establishing the facts. *See* *D'Ambrosio v. Pennsylvania National Mutual Casualty Insurance Co.*, 494 Pa. 501, 431 A.2d 966 (1981)* (prejudice must render party "unable to present its case fully," not merely make case more difficult)*.

The trial court erred in finding prejudice "incurable" when plaintiff retains substantial evidence to prosecute his negligence claim. The appropriate remedy for discovery violations, when the opposing party retains meaningful evidence, is not default judgment but rather sanctions that address the actual prejudice, such as adverse inference instructions or costs associated with the obstruction. *See* *Butch v. City of Philadelphia*, 556 Pa. 524, 729 A.2d 1191 (1999)* (dismissal appropriate only when lesser sanctions would be futile)*.

---

### IV. DEFENDANT'S CONDUCT DOES NOT RISE TO THE LEVEL OF WILLFULNESS REQUIRED FOR DEFAULT JUDGMENT

Plaintiff characterizes defendant's conduct as "willful" based on the January 2011 letter from defendant's counsel. However, this characterization ignores the unique circumstances that distinguish this case from those where severe sanctions were upheld.

Initially, from 2007 to 2008, defendant's counsel represented that he could not locate defendant. This is not willful non-compliance—it is a representation of fact that proved accurate when a private investigator was eventually required to locate defendant in 2009. The inability to locate a client, while concerning, does not constitute the "intentional avoidance" that warrants severe sanctions. *See* *Holschuh v. Holland*, 545 Pa. 227, 681 A.2d 707 (1996)* (willfulness requires intentional conduct, not mere inability)*.

Subsequently, defendant objected to being videotaped, which plaintiff does not dispute. While the court ultimately ordered videotaped depositions, defendant's resistance to this specific form of discovery does not equate to general willfulness. Defendant never denied that the fire occurred or refused to provide any testimony—he merely objected to the method. The appropriate response to such an objection is a court order compelling compliance, not a finding of willfulness that supports default judgment.

The January 2011 letter, while regrettable in its tone, came after years of procedural disputes and after defendant had been diagnosed with a terminal illness. Courts have recognized that party's conduct must be evaluated in context, and that health circumstances may inform the reasonableness of conduct. *See* *Kline*, 552 Pa. at 142, 713 A.2d at 654* (courts must consider totality of circumstances)*.

---

### V. THE TRIAL COURT FAILED TO ADEQUATELY CONSIDER LESSER SANCTIONS THAT WOULD HAVE BEEN APPROPRIATE

Even assuming some sanction was warranted, the trial court failed to consider the full range of available remedies that would address any actual prejudice without imposing the extreme sanction of default judgment.

Monetary sanctions against the estate would have been appropriate and could have compensated plaintiff for any additional costs incurred due to the discovery disputes. Evidentiary sanctions, such as precluding defendant from presenting certain defenses or requiring the trial court to draw adverse inferences from the failure to testify, would have addressed the prejudice plaintiff claims without entering a complete default judgment. *See* *Butch*, 556 Pa. at 532, 729 A.2d at 1196* (lesser sanctions must be considered)*.

The trial court's finding that lesser sanctions would have been "futile" is undermined by the fact that it never imposed them. Defendant's estate remains intact and subject to monetary sanctions. The estate could have been ordered to pay plaintiff's attorney's fees incurred in connection with the repeated deposition attempts. Such sanctions would have addressed the violation without entering judgment on the merits of the negligence claim.

---

### VI. THE ENTRY OF DEFAULT JUDGMENT EFFECTIVELY DETERMINES THE MERITS OF THE NEGLIGENCE CLAIM WITHOUT A TRIAL

Plaintiff attempts to distinguish the discovery sanction from a merits determination by noting that default judgment as a discovery sanction is "procedural." This distinction is unavailing in practice.

When the trial court entered judgment against defendant, it effectively determined that defendant was negligent and that his negligence caused the fire that killed Tina Marie Plotts. The trial court's opinion acknowledges that "none of the evidence produced during the seven years of litigation in any way suggested that defendant was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire." Yet the court entered judgment anyway.

This result transforms the discovery sanction into a substantive determination of liability—a result that Pennsylvania law expressly prohibits. *See* *Floyd*, 556 Pa. at 80, 726 A.2d at 1057* (sanctions must not become substantive punishment)*. The court cannot impose default judgment when the record contains no evidence of negligence, then defend the sanction as "not a merits determination." The practical effect is precisely that.

---

### VII. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, and remand for further proceedings. The trial court abused its discretion in entering default judgment where: (1) the defendant is deceased and cannot benefit from non-compliance; (2) plaintiff retains substantial alternative evidence to prosecute his claim; (3) defendant's conduct, while regrettable, does not rise to the level of willfulness required for the most severe sanctions; (4) lesser sanctions were available and appropriate; and (5) the default judgment effectively determines the merits of the negligence claim despite the trial court's own finding that no evidence of negligence exists.

The integrity of the judicial process is preserved not by entering default judgments against deceased parties, but by ensuring that sanctions are proportionate to the violations and that they serve the remedial purposes for which they are designed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to Pa. R.A.P. 1928**

**Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, Administrator of the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer v. Joseph Michael Angelichio**

**No.**

**OPINION**

**Before the Court**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on an interlocutory appeal by Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer ("Defendant"), challenging the trial court’s entry of default judgment against him as a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders. The trial court imposed the sanction pursuant to Pa. R.C.P. 4019 following Defendant’s persistent failure to appear for a videotaped deposition. For the reasons that follow, the trial court’s order is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The grant of a default judgment as a discovery sanction is committed to the sound discretion of the trial court. An appellate court will not reverse the decision of the trial court absent a clear abuse of discretion. See *In re: Estate of Kline*, 552 Pa. 132, 713 A.2d 648 (1998). An abuse of discretion is not merely an error in judgment, but rather a misapplication of the law or an unreasonable exercise of judicial power.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Finding Defendant’s Conduct Willful**

The trial court’s determination that Defendant engaged in willful noncompliance is supported by the record. Pennsylvania law is clear that "persistent noncompliance with court-ordered discovery, especially after explicit warnings, demonstrates a level of defiance that justifies severe sanctions." *In re: Estate of Kline*, 552 Pa. 132, 713 A.2d 648 (1998). Here, the record establishes a history of obstruction spanning several years. While Defendant’s counsel initially cited an inability to locate the Defendant as the reason for missed depositions in 2007 and 2008, the nature of the noncompliance shifted in 2009 after Plaintiff successfully located the Defendant.

Following the court’s explicit orders in July 2009 and August 2010 requiring Defendant to appear for a videotaped deposition, Defendant actively refused to comply. The most damning evidence is the January 28, 2011 correspondence from Defendant’s counsel, stating, "obviously, Mr. Diefenderfer will not appear Monday, nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled." This unequivocal statement demonstrates a conscious decision to defy the court’s authority. As held in *Holschuh v. Holland*, 545 Pa. 227, 681 A.2d 707 (1996), "a party’s intentional avoidance of discovery, particularly after judicial orders, constitutes willfulness warranting dismissal." The trial court correctly identified this conduct as willful and in bad faith.

The Defense argues that the initial inability to locate Defendant and his subsequent objections to being videotaped, coupled with alleged health issues, negate the finding of willfulness. However, the record provided does not contain evidence of a terminal illness diagnosis prior to the January 2011 refusal; it only notes Defendant’s death in January 2012. Even assuming health concerns, they do not excuse a categorical refusal to obey a court order. The objection to the method of discovery (videotaping) was resolved by the court’s order limiting the tape’s use. Defendant’s continued refusal thereafter constituted willful noncompliance under *Holschuh*.

**B. The Prejudice to Plaintiff Was Severe and Incurable**

The trial court properly found that Plaintiff suffered severe and incurable prejudice. Prejudice under Rule 4019 exists when the discovery violation "renders the opposing party unable to present its case fully." *D’Ambrosio v. Pennsylvania National Mutual Casualty Insurance Co.*, 494 Pa. 501, 431 A.2d 966 (1981). Defendant was the sole individual present in the residence at the time of the fire other than the decedent. His testimony was critical to establishing the facts surrounding the origin of the fire and the condition of the premises.

The Defense argues that prejudice is curable because the fire marshal’s report, physical evidence, and landlord testimony can establish the cause of the fire. While these sources may establish the physical origin of the fire, they cannot establish Defendant’s state of mind, his actions prior to the fire, or his knowledge regarding the defective kerosene container—elements essential to a negligence claim. The loss of Defendant’s unique testimony, directly resulting from his refusal to comply with court orders while he was alive, constitutes severe prejudice. As noted in *D’Ambrosio*, the inability to secure critical testimony renders the opposing party unable to present its case fully.

Defendant argues that any prejudice resulted from his death in January 2012, rather than his discovery violations. This argument is unpersuasive. The prejudice stems from the inability to obtain testimony. Defendant’s refusal to appear for depositions in 2009, 2010, and 2011—while he was alive—directly caused the absence of sworn testimony. Had Defendant complied with the court orders when they were issued, the testimony would have been secured prior to his death. Because Defendant chose to obstruct discovery until his death, the prejudice is directly attributable to his conduct. The death of the party merely rendered the prejudice incurable; it did not cause it.

**C. Lesser Sanctions Would Have Been Futile**

The trial court acted within its discretion in concluding that lesser sanctions would be futile. Pennsylvania law mandates that "the court must impose the least severe sanction that will accomplish the desired purpose," and dismissal is appropriate only when lesser sanctions would be futile. *Butch v. City of Philadelphia*, 556 Pa. 524, 729 A.2d 1191 (1999). The record reflects that the trial court attempted to accommodate Defendant’s concerns regarding the videotaping of the deposition by restricting the tape’s use in the August 25, 2010 order. Despite this accommodation and multiple court orders, Defendant explicitly refused to appear.

The Defense contends that monetary sanctions or adverse inference instructions would have been appropriate. However, given Defendant’s categorical refusal to participate—"nor any other time"—monetary sanctions or further continuances would not have compelled compliance. The trial court was not required to engage in a futile exercise of imposing fines on a party who had already signaled his intent to permanently defy the court’s orders. The finding of futility is amply supported by the record. See *In re: Estate of Kline*, 552 Pa. 132, 713 A.2d 648 (1998).

**D. The Trial Court Properly Balanced the Equities**

Defendant argues that the trial court failed to balance the equities and that the evidence did not support a finding of negligence. However, the entry of default judgment as a discovery sanction is not a ruling on the merits of the negligence claim, but rather a remedy for the destruction of the adversarial process. As noted in *Floyd v. McCloskey*, 556 Pa. 73, 726 A.2d 1052 (1999), the court must balance the nature of the violation against the severity of the sanction. Here, the violation was total and persistent, effectively preventing Plaintiff from proving his case. The defense’s argument regarding the lack of evidence of negligence is misplaced because the lack of evidence is the direct result of Defendant’s obstruction. The trial court did not presume negligence; it sanctioned Defendant for preventing Plaintiff from discovering the facts necessary to prove or disprove it.

The Defense argues that because the Defendant is deceased, the sanction serves no remedial purpose and is purely punitive. However, the purpose of the sanction is to remedy the prejudice caused by the discovery violations that occurred *prior* to death. The fact that the Defendant is now deceased and cannot testify does not absolve the estate of the consequences of the decedent’s willful flouting of judicial orders. The integrity of the judicial process requires that parties not be allowed to benefit from their own obstruction by delaying until evidence is permanently lost.

Furthermore, the defense’s contention that Plaintiff failed to diligently pursue discovery is contradicted by the record. Plaintiff hired a private investigator to locate Defendant and filed multiple motions for contempt. The delay in securing the deposition was largely driven by Defendant’s evasiveness and subsequent refusal to comply.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court carefully considered the history of noncompliance, the explicit refusal to obey court orders, and the resulting prejudice to Plaintiff. The sanction of default judgment was a proportional response to Defendant’s willful obstruction of the discovery process. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that lesser sanctions would have been futile and that the extreme remedy was necessary to address the egregious conduct.

Accordingly, the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, are affirmed.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY FAILING TO IMPOSE LESSER SANCTIONS

Pennsylvania law is well-established that "the court must impose the least severe sanction that will accomplish the desired purpose" of remedying discovery violations. *Butch v. City of Philadelphia*, 556 Pa. 524, 729 A.2d 1191 (1999). This principle requires the trial court to engage in a meaningful analysis of progressively severe sanctions before resorting to the ultimate remedy of default judgment.

The record in this case demonstrates that the trial court failed to adequately consider or implement lesser sanctions. While the court entered two orders compelling the defendant's deposition, it never imposed monetary sanctions, adverse inference instructions, or other intermediate remedies that might have addressed the plaintiff's concerns without effectively adjudicating the underlying claim. As this Court noted in *Kellerman v. McDonough*, 689 A.2d 273 (Pa. The defense acknowledges that the defendant did not appear for scheduled depositions. However, the appropriate response to non-compliance is not immediate default judgment, but rather the graduated imposition of sanctions designed to compel compliance or compensate for prejudice. The trial court's failure to explore monetary sanctions, evidentiary presumptions, or other intermediate remedies constitutes an abuse of discretion warranting reversal.

The trial court's determination that the defendant's conduct was "willful" and in "bad faith" is not supported by the record when properly analyzed. While the plaintiff characterizes the defendant's conduct as intentional obstruction, a closer examination reveals a more nuanced picture.

Initially, from 2007 to 2008, defendant's counsel genuinely could not locate the defendant. This is not a basis for finding willfulness. Following the defendant's location in 2009, his refusal to submit to a videotaped deposition was based on a legitimate objection to that method of discovery—a concern that this Court has recognized as reasonable in certain circumstances. This Court should distinguish between cases involving genuine bad faith obstruction and cases involving procedural disputes about discovery methods. In *Krechevsky v. **FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to Pa. R.A.P. 1928**

**Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, Administrator of the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer v. Joseph Michael Angelichio**

**No.**

**OPINION**

**Before the Court**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on an interlocutory appeal by Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer ("Defendant"), challenging the trial court’s entry of default judgment against him as a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders. The trial court imposed the sanction pursuant to Pa. R.C.P. 4019 following Defendant’s persistent failure to appear for a videotaped deposition. After careful consideration of the closing arguments submitted by both the Plaintiff and the Defense, the trial court’s order is affirmed.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The grant of a default judgment as a discovery sanction is committed to the sound discretion of the trial court. An appellate court will not reverse the decision of the trial court absent a clear abuse of discretion. See *In re: Estate of Kline*, 552 Pa. 132, 713 A.2d 648 (1998). An abuse of discretion is not merely an error in judgment, but rather a misapplication of the law or an unreasonable exercise of judicial power.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Finding Defendant’s Conduct Willful**

The Defense argues that the trial court erred in finding willfulness, citing *Krechevsky v. Eastern Pennsylvania Psychiatric Institute*, 653 A.2d 73 (Pa. Super. 1995), for the proposition that dilatory conduct does not rise to the level of willfulness required for default judgment. The Defense further contends that Defendant’s refusal to be videotaped was a legitimate objection under *Pennypacker v. Welsh*, 700 A.2d 1303 (Pa. Super. 1997), rather than bad faith obstruction.

These arguments are unpersuasive in light of the record. While the initial failures to appear in 2007 and 2008 were attributed to an inability to locate Defendant, the nature of the noncompliance shifted fundamentally in 2009. After Plaintiff successfully located Defendant, the court entered an order on August 25, 2010, specifically accommodating Defendant’s objection to the videotaping by restricting the tape’s use. Despite this accommodation, Defendant’s counsel communicated in a letter dated January 28, 2011, that Defendant stated, “there is no way I’m getting involved,” and further asserted that “Mr. Diefenderfer will not appear Monday, nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled.”

This categorical refusal to obey a court order, issued after the specific objection to the discovery method had been resolved, constitutes willful noncompliance. As held in *Holschuh v. Holland*, 545 Pa. 227, 681 A.2d 707 (1996), “a party’s intentional avoidance of discovery, particularly after judicial orders, constitutes willfulness warranting dismissal.” The trial court correctly identified this conduct as a conscious decision to defy the court’s authority, distinguishing it from mere dilatory behavior or procedural disputes.

**B. The Prejudice to Plaintiff Was Severe and Incurable**

The Defense argues that the alleged prejudice was curable because the fire marshal’s report and physical evidence established the cause of the fire, rendering Defendant’s testimony unnecessary under the standard set forth in *D’Ambrosio v. Pennsylvania National Mutual Casualty Insurance Co.*, 494 Pa. 501, 431 A.2d 966 (1981). This argument misconstrues the nature of Plaintiff’s negligence claim.

While the fire marshal’s report established the physical origin of the fire—an unattended pot and a defective kerosene container—it could not establish Defendant’s state of mind, his knowledge regarding the defective container, or his actions in the period leading up to the fire. These elements are essential to proving negligence. Defendant was the sole individual present in the residence other than the decedent. His unique testimony was the only source of evidence regarding these critical issues. The loss of this testimony, directly resulting from his refusal to comply with court orders while he was alive, constitutes severe prejudice that renders Plaintiff unable to present his case fully. See *D’Ambrosio*, 494 Pa. at 506, 431 A.2d at 969.

The Defense’s contention that Plaintiff could have relied on landlord testimony or other sources does not cure the prejudice, as those sources cannot speak to Defendant’s specific conduct and state of mind on the night of the fire.

**C. Lesser Sanctions Would Have Been Futile**

The Defense contends that the trial court abused its discretion by failing to impose lesser sanctions, such as monetary fines or adverse inference instructions, before resorting to default judgment. Citing *Butch v. City of Philadelphia*, 556 Pa. 524, 729 A.2d 1191 (1999), and *Kellerman v. McDonough*, 689 A.2d 273 (Pa. Super. 1997), the Defense argues that a graduated approach is required.

However, Pennsylvania law mandates that the court impose the least severe sanction that will accomplish the desired purpose, and dismissal is appropriate when lesser sanctions would be futile. *Butch*, 556 Pa. at 529, 729 A.2d at 1194. The record reflects that the trial court attempted to accommodate Defendant’s concerns. Despite multiple court orders and the accommodation regarding the videotaping, Defendant explicitly refused to participate in any future deposition.

Given Defendant’s categorical statement that he would not appear “nor any other time,” monetary sanctions or further continuances would not have compelled compliance. The trial court was not required to engage in a futile exercise of imposing fines on a party who had already signaled his intent to permanently defy the court’s orders. The finding of futility is supported by the record. See *In re: Estate of Kline*, 552 Pa. 132, 713 A.2d 648 (1998).

**D. The Sanction Is Procedural, Not a Determination on the Merits**

The Defense argues that the default judgment effectively determines the merits of the negligence claim without a trial, violating due process. The Defense asserts that the physical evidence shows the fire was caused by an unattended pot and defective container, implying Defendant was not negligent.

This argument mischaracterizes the nature of discovery sanctions. As noted in *Floyd v. McCloskey*, 556 Pa. 73, 726 A.2d 1052 (1999), the court must balance the nature of the violation against the severity of the sanction. The entry of default judgment here is not a substantive ruling that Defendant was negligent; rather, it is a procedural remedy for Defendant’s destruction of the adversarial process. The lack of evidence regarding Defendant’s negligence is the direct result of his obstruction. The trial court did not presume negligence; it sanctioned Defendant for preventing Plaintiff from discovering the facts necessary to prove or disprove it.

**E. The Defendant’s Death Does Not Render the Sanction Purely Punitive**

The Defense argues that because Defendant died in January 2012, the sanction serves no remedial purpose and is purely punitive. This argument ignores the timeline of events. The prejudice to Plaintiff arose *before* Defendant’s death as a result of his persistent refusal to comply with court orders while he was alive and capable of testifying. The death of the party merely rendered the prejudice incurable; it did not cause it. To accept the Defense’s argument would incentivize parties to obstruct discovery until death, then claim immunity from sanctions. The integrity of the judicial process requires that parties not be allowed to benefit from their own obstruction by delaying until evidence is permanently lost.

**F. Plaintiff’s Diligence**

Finally, the Defense argues that Plaintiff failed to diligently pursue discovery, citing a delay in hiring a private investigator in 2009. The record shows that Plaintiff requested depositions as early as 2007 and filed motions for contempt when Defendant failed to appear. The delay in securing the deposition was largely driven by Defendant’s evasiveness and subsequent refusal to comply. The trial court properly found that Plaintiff’s efforts were sufficient.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court carefully considered the history of noncompliance, the explicit refusal to obey court orders, and the resulting prejudice to Plaintiff. The sanction of default judgment was a proportional response to Defendant’s willful obstruction of the discovery process. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that lesser sanctions would have been futile and that the extreme remedy was necessary to address the egregious conduct. The Defense’s arguments regarding the curability of prejudice, the lack of willfulness, and the punitive nature of the sanction are without merit.

Accordingly, the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, are affirmed.
