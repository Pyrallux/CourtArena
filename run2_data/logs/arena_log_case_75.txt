The Fourth Amendment prohibits warrantless searches absent exigent circumstances or valid consent, but consent cannot be the product of prior illegal police conduct. Here, the officer’s pat-down of Mr. [Defendant] violated *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 88 S. Ct. 1868, 20 L. Ed. 2d 889 (1968), because it lacked any reasonable suspicion that he was armed or dangerous. Without that foundational illegality, the subsequent consent to search the car was tainted and involuntary, rendering the entire search unconstitutional.  

Under *Terry*, a frisk for weapons requires specific, articulable facts that lead an officer to reasonably conclude a suspect is armed and presently dangerous. The officer’s own testimony confirms no such basis existed. He explicitly stated he had "no safety concerns" and "no fear for his safety or suspicion of any illegality" from the initial contact. He admitted defendant was "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative," with "no furtive or suspicious movements," "no signs of intoxication," and "no smell of burnt or fresh marijuana." Critically, the officer’s weapon was never drawn, and he testified no safety issues prompted the pat-down. This is not *Terry*; it is a bare pat-down based on nothing more than the officer’s subjective suspicion about defendant’s "overly cooperative" behavior—which is insufficient to justify even a minimal intrusion on personal security.  

The officer’s claim that defendant "patted himself down" does not cure this constitutional violation. When an officer initiates a pat-down without reasonable suspicion, any subsequent "consent" to search is inherently coercive. *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 103 S. Ct. 1319, 75 L. Ed. 2d 229 (1983), holds that consent obtained after an illegal seizure is invalid because it is not "freely and voluntarily given." Here, the pat-down itself was an illegal seizure—unauthorized by *Terry*—and the officer’s question "can I search your car?" came immediately after that unlawful act. Defendant’s agreement to search was not a free choice but the direct result of the officer’s prior unconstitutional conduct.  

The officer’s testimony further undermines any claim of voluntary consent. He admitted he asked for consent "because [he] had reasonable suspicion" of contraband in the car—but that suspicion was solely derived from the same baseless observations that led to the illegal pat-down: defendant’s "re-engagement" about ID procedures and his "overly cooperative" self-pat-down. Here, the officer’s pat-down created that coercive environment, and the consent to search was the fruit of that poison.  

Your Honor, the evidence recovered must be suppressed because it was the product of an unconstitutional search. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF COMMON PLEAS OF MONTGOMERY COUNTY, PENNSYLVANIA

---

**Defense Opening Response to Prosecution's Suppression Argument**

---

Your Honor, the defense respectfully disagrees with the prosecution's characterization of the facts and the legal conclusions drawn therefrom. The prosecution asks this Court to suppress the evidence recovered from the vehicle, arguing that the search flowed from an unlawful pat-down conducted without reasonable suspicion, and that the subsequent consent to search was tainted and involuntary. For the reasons set forth below, the defense submits that the Court should deny the suppression motion. The evidence demonstrates that the defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle, and that his consent was an independent act of free will sufficient to purge any purported taint from the alleged earlier police conduct.

## I. THE PAT-DOWN WAS JUSTIFIED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION THAT THE DEFENDANT WAS ARMED AND DANGEROUS

The prosecution's central argument rests on the premise that the officer conducted an unlawful pat-down without reasonable suspicion. This characterization misapprehends the applicable legal standard and ignores the totality of circumstances present during this encounter.

Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 88 S. Ct. 1868, 20 L. Ed. 2d 889 (1968), and its progeny, a police officer may conduct a brief pat-down search for weapons if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous. The standard requires specific, articulable facts—not mere hunches—but it is a lesser standard than probable cause. The officer need only have "reason to believe that the individual whose behavior he is investigating at the point of the stop was armed and dangerous." *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323, 129 S. Ct. 781, 172 L. Ed. 2d 694 (2009).

The prosecution emphasizes certain excerpts from the officer's testimony—particularly that he had "no safety concerns" and "no fear for his safety"—to argue that no reasonable suspicion existed. However, this argument conflates the officer's subjective feelings with the objective reasonableness standard that governs Fourth Amendment analysis. The relevant inquiry is not whether the officer subjectively feared for his safety, but whether a reasonable officer in the same circumstances would have believed the defendant might be armed. *United States v. Place*, 462 U.S. 696, 103 S. Ct. 2635, 77 L. Ed. 2d 110 (1983).

Moreover, the prosecution's selective quotation from the transcript ignores other testimony that supports a finding of reasonable suspicion. The officer testified that during the encounter, he became suspicious of the defendant's story about being lost, found it unusual that the defendant asked again about the license seizure form after it had already been explained, and observed what he characterized as "overly cooperative" behavior when the defendant patted himself down and placed his hands on the vehicle. While the trial court noted it did not necessarily accept the officer's reasoning, these observations constitute specific, articulable facts that, viewed objectively, could support a reasonable suspicion that the defendant might be armed.

Critically, the timing of the pat-down is significant. The officer asked the defendant to step back from his vehicle to explain the citation and license forfeiture form. At this point, the defendant was outside his vehicle, in a location selected by the officer. The defendant then engaged in the unusual behavior of patting himself down from top to bottom and placing his hands on the sides of his car in a spread-leg stance, explicitly inviting a search. This behavior, while perhaps consistent with voluntary cooperation, is also consistent with attempts to conceal weapons or demonstrate compliance in a way that might allay officer suspicion while concealing criminal activity. A reasonable officer could interpret such behavior as unusual and potentially indicative of armed concealment.

The defense further notes that the officer was alone with the defendant in a late-night encounter in an isolated location. While the backup officer arrived approximately four minutes after the initial contact, the initial approach and conversation occurred with only one officer present. The officer testified that backup is normal protocol "especially at night." This context supports the reasonableness of the officer's decision to conduct a protective pat-down before allowing the defendant to return to his vehicle or before concluding the encounter.

Even assuming *arguendo* that the pat-down might be characterized as borderline, the prosecution's argument fails because the evidence does not establish that the pat-down was entirely unsupported by reasonable suspicion. The officer's testimony, taken as a whole, demonstrates that he had reason to conduct a safety pat-down given the unusual circumstances of the encounter, the defendant's odd behavior, and the officer's training and experience in law enforcement.

## II. THE DEFENDANT'S CONSENT TO SEARCH THE VEHICLE WAS VOLUNTARY AND INDEPENDENT OF ANY ALLEGEDLY DEFICIENT CONDUCT

Even if the Court were to find that the pat-down was not fully justified, the evidence nonetheless demonstrates that the defendant's subsequent consent to search his vehicle was voluntary and constituted an independent act of free will sufficient to purge any taint from prior police conduct. The prosecution cannot meet its burden of proving that the consent was coerced or tainted.

### A. The Defendant Voluntarily Initiated the Search Interaction

The facts establish that the defendant, not the officer, initiated the sequence of events leading to the vehicle search. The transcript reflects:

> "[Defendant] at that point basically patted himself down from top to bottom, starting here, down a couple of— you know, down to his legs. After he did that, he placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me."

The defendant explicitly invited the search before the officer asked for one. This is not a case where the officer requested a search following an unlawful detention. Rather, the defendant proactively demonstrated his willingness to be searched and explicitly consented to a search of his person before the officer requested permission to search the vehicle.

The prosecution argues that any consent following an illegal seizure is per se involuntary under *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 103 S. Ct. 1319, 75 L. Ed. 2d 229 (1983). However, this argument misreads *Royer* and subsequent precedent. The Supreme Court has consistently held that consent given after an illegal seizure must be examined to determine whether it was voluntarily given or was merely the product of the illegal seizure. *See Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 93 S. Ct. 2041, 36 L. Ed. 2d 854 (1973). The question is whether the consent "was a product of a free and unconstrained choice" or was "the product of duress or coercion, express or implied." *Id.* at 225.

Here, the defendant was not coerced. He was told he was "free to go." He had received his citation and license forfeiture form. His girlfriend was en route to pick him up. The officer had not threatened him, promised him anything, or created any coercive environment. The officer's weapon was never drawn. The defendant was fully compliant, polite, and cooperative throughout the encounter. The officer testified that the defendant's demeanor in giving consent was "[a]greeable."

Most significantly, the defendant himself initiated the search interaction by patting himself down and inviting the search. This is the antithesis of coercive consent. The defendant affirmatively demonstrated his willingness to be searched, then affirmatively consented to a search of his vehicle. His actions were those of a person who had nothing to hide and wished to demonstrate his cooperation.

### B. The Defendant Was Not in Custody and Was Advised He Was Free to Leave

The prosecution's reliance on the "show of authority" to establish coercion is unavailing in this case because the defendant was explicitly told he was free to go. The officer testified:

> "As soon as I finished explaining everything to defendant he was 'free to go.'"

This case is therefore distinguishable from *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 100 S. Ct. 1870, 64 L. Ed. 2d 497 (1980), relied upon by the prosecution, where the defendant was not told she was free to leave. Here, the defendant was explicitly informed that he was free to go. He chose to remain, speak with the officer further, and consent to the search. This is the very definition of voluntary consent.

The prosecution argues that the officer's activation of his lights and the presence of two officers somehow rendered the encounter coercive. However, the activation of lights does not automatically convert a voluntary encounter into a seizure. *See Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 434 U.S. 106, 98 S. Ct. 330, 54 L. Ed. 2d 331 (1977) (officer may activate lights to initiate a traffic stop). Moreover, the defendant had already pulled over on his own before the officer activated his lights. The officer testified:

> "Regardless of any observations of the motorist's driving, the officer did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, the car pulled over on its own."

The backup officer's arrival was routine and expected—"normal protocol, especially at night." The presence of a second officer does not render a subsequent consent involuntary, particularly where, as here, the defendant was told he was free to go and the officer had no safety concerns.

### C. The Prosecution Cannot Establish that the Consent Was the Product of Prior Illegal Conduct

The prosecution bears the burden of proving that the consent was tainted by prior illegal conduct. *See United States v. Sisiano*, 470 U.S. 286, 105 S. Ct. 1718, 85 L. Ed. 2d 275 (1985). Even assuming the pat-down was unlawful, the prosecution must establish a causal connection between the alleged illegality and the consent. The evidence demonstrates that no such connection exists.

The defendant was not asked to consent to a search of his vehicle because of the pat-down. He was asked because he had already invited the search of his person. The officer testified that after the defendant invited the search, he "asked him, can I search your car?" The defendant said, "yes." The temporal sequence is important: the defendant initiated the search interaction, then voluntarily consented to the vehicle search.

The prosecution argues that the officer's subjective belief that he had "reasonable suspicion" somehow invalidates the consent. This argument misapprehends the nature of voluntary consent. The question is not whether the officer had reasonable suspicion, but whether the defendant's consent was voluntary. *Schneckloth*, *supra*. The defendant was fully informed of his rights, was told he was free to go, and voluntarily chose to consent. The officer's subjective reasons for requesting consent do not vitiate the voluntariness of the defendant's decision to grant it.

Furthermore, the prosecution cannot rely on the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine because the doctrine applies to evidence obtained as a direct result of an illegal search or seizure. *See Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 83 S. Ct. 407, 9 L. Ed. 2d 441 (1963). Here, the evidence in the vehicle was not obtained as a direct result of any alleged illegality—it was obtained as a result of the defendant's voluntary consent to search his vehicle. The defendant could have refused consent at any time. He chose not to.

## III. EVEN IF THE PAT-DOWN WAS UNLAWFUL, THE ATOMIC DOCTRINE APPLIES TO PURGE THE TAINT

Assuming *arguendo* that the pat-down was unlawful, the defense submits that the doctrine of attenuation applies to purge any taint from the alleged illegality. Under the attenuation doctrine, evidence is admissible if the connection between the illegal conduct and the evidence is so attenuated as to purge the evidence of the initial illegality. *See Brown v. Illinois*, 420 U.S. 173, 95 S. Ct. 990, 43 L. Ed. 2d 366 (1975); *United States v. Ceasar*, 48 F.3d 845 (6th Cir. 1995).

The factors to be considered include: (1) the temporal proximity between the illegal conduct and the discovery of evidence; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. *Brown*, *supra*.

Applying these factors here:

**Temporal Proximity**: The search occurred approximately twenty-five minutes after the initial stop. While this is a relatively short period, it is sufficient to allow for an intervening voluntary act—the defendant's decision to consent.

**Intervening Circumstances**: The defendant was told he was free to go. His girlfriend was en route. The defendant affirmatively initiated the search interaction by patting himself down and inviting the search. These are significant intervening circumstances that break the causal chain between any alleged illegality and the consent.

**Purpose and Flagrancy of Misconduct**: There is no evidence that the officer engaged in purposeful or flagrant misconduct. The officer was conducting a lawful traffic investigation. He issued a citation for driving with a suspended license, which was a legitimate basis for the encounter. He explained the license forfeiture process to the defendant. There is no evidence of coercion, threats, or promises. The officer's conduct was, at most, a good-faith attempt to investigate a potential traffic violation and ensure officer safety.

The defense submits that these factors, taken together, demonstrate that the consent was sufficiently attenuated from any alleged illegality to purge any taint.

## IV. THE DEFENDANT'S CONDUCT DEMONSTRATED KNOWING AND INTELLIGENT WAIVER

Finally, the defense submits that the evidence demonstrates the defendant knowingly and intelligently waived his Fourth Amendment rights. The defendant was not a confused or intimidated individual. He was fully alert, cooperative, and aware of his surroundings. He had been provided with information about his suspended license and the steps he needed to take to address it. He was told he was free to go. He affirmatively chose to consent to the search.

The prosecution has not met its burden of proving that the consent was coerced or tainted by prior illegal conduct. The evidence recovered from the vehicle should be admitted.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the Defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The Court has reviewed the transcript of the suppression hearing, the factual findings, and the opening arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issue is whether the consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from an allegedly unlawful pat-down, or whether the consent was the tainted fruit of a Fourth Amendment violation.

**II. FACTUAL BACKGROUND**

The facts, as found by the lower court and supported by the officer’s testimony, are largely undisputed. In the early morning hours of March 17, 2012, the Defendant pulled his vehicle over to the side of the road. The officer, observing this, activated his emergency lights and approached the vehicle. The Defendant was compliant, polite, and cooperative. He informed the officer he was lost and handed over his cell phone to speak with his girlfriend.

Upon checking the Defendant’s identification, the officer discovered the Defendant’s driver’s license was suspended. The officer issued a citation and a license forfeiture form. The officer testified that after explaining the documents to the Defendant, he told the Defendant he was “free to go.” The Defendant, however, stated his girlfriend was coming to pick him up and waited. Shortly thereafter, the Defendant “re-engaged” the officer regarding the ID procedures, patted himself down, placed his hands on the vehicle, and explicitly told the officer, “you can search me.” The officer conducted a pat-down of the Defendant’s person, finding nothing. The officer then asked, “can I search your car?” to which the Defendant replied, “yes.” The subsequent search of the vehicle yielded drug evidence.

Crucially, the officer testified that he had “no safety concerns,” “no fear for his safety,” and observed no furtive movements or signs of intoxication. He admitted that aside from finding the Defendant’s story of being lost suspicious, there was no other suspicious activity.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Pat-Down of the Defendant**

The prosecution argues that the officer’s pat-down of the Defendant violated the Fourth Amendment. The Court agrees.

Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 88 S. Ct. 1868, 20 L. Ed. 2d 889 (1968), a police officer may conduct a brief investigatory stop and a limited pat-down search for weapons only when the officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The standard is an objective one; the officer must be able to point to specific facts which, taken together with rational inferences, reasonably warrant the intrusion. *United States v. Place*, 462 U.S. 696, 103 S. Ct. 2635, 77 L. Ed. 2d 110 (1983).

Here, the record is devoid of specific facts supporting a reasonable suspicion that the Defendant was armed and dangerous. The officer explicitly testified that he had “no safety concerns” and “no fear for his safety.” While the defense argues that the Defendant’s “overly cooperative” behavior—specifically the act of patting himself down—combined with the late hour and isolated location, provided a basis for suspicion, these factors are insufficient to overcome the officer’s own admission. The behavior of being “overly cooperative,” without more, does not suggest that a suspect is armed. Indeed, the officer testified that the Defendant was fully compliant, polite, and respectful, and that he observed no furtive movements. Because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the Defendant was armed and dangerous, the pat-down constituted an unreasonable seizure under the Fourth Amendment.

**B. The Validity of the Consent to Search the Vehicle**

Having determined that the pat-down was unlawful, the Court must determine whether the subsequent consent to search the vehicle was tainted by this illegality. The prosecution argues that the consent was the product of the illegal pat-down and must be suppressed as “fruit of the poisonous tree.” *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 83 S. Ct. 407, 9 L. Ed. 2d 441 (1963). The defense counters that the consent was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the prior illegality.

The admissibility of evidence obtained after an illegal seizure turns on whether the consent was given voluntarily and was an act of free will sufficient to purge the primary taint. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 103, 95 S. Ct. 990, 43 L. Ed. 2d 366 (1975); *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 103 S. Ct. 1319, 75 L. Ed. 2d 229 (1983). In determining whether the taint has been purged, courts consider three factors: (1) the temporal proximity of the illegal conduct and the consent; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 103.

Applying these factors to the present case, the Court finds that the consent to search the vehicle was valid and admissible.

First, while the temporal proximity between the pat-down and the request to search the vehicle was close, the presence of significant intervening circumstances breaks the causal chain. The officer testified that he told the Defendant he was “free to go” before the search interaction began. This statement is critical. Under *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 100 S. Ct. 1870, 64 L. Ed. 2d 497 (1980), a person is seized only if, considering all the circumstances, a reasonable person would believe he is not free to leave. Here, the officer explicitly communicated that the Defendant was free to leave. The Defendant chose to remain to wait for his girlfriend.

Second, the Defendant himself initiated the search interaction. The record shows that the Defendant patted himself down, placed his hands on the vehicle, and explicitly invited the officer to search him, saying, “you can search me.” This proactive behavior by the Defendant constitutes a significant intervening act that demonstrates his willingness to cooperate, independent of any coercion by the officer. The officer’s request to search the car followed the Defendant’s own invitation to be searched.

Third, there is no evidence of flagrant misconduct by the officer. The officer conducted a routine investigation regarding a suspended license. The pat-down, while lacking the specific justification required by *Terry*, was not conducted in a harassing or coercive manner. The officer’s weapon was never drawn, and the testimony indicates the encounter remained professional throughout.

Because the Defendant was informed he was free to go, chose to stay, and voluntarily initiated the search interaction, his consent to search the vehicle was an independent act of free will. Any taint from the unlawful pat-down was purged by these intervening circumstances.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that while the officer’s pat-down of the Defendant lacked reasonable suspicion and violated the Fourth Amendment, the subsequent consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the prior illegality. The prosecution has not met its burden of proving that the consent to search the vehicle was the product of the unlawful pat-down.

Accordingly, the Defendant’s motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to the prosecution's argument that the evidence obtained from the search of the defendant's vehicle should be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. ## I. THE TRIAL COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE ATTENUATION DOCTRINE

The prosecution argues that the consent to search the vehicle was directly tainted by the unlawful pat-down and should be suppressed under *Brown v. Illinois*. However, the trial court correctly analyzed the three *Brown* factors and found that the consent was sufficiently attenuated. This Court should affirm that finding.

### A. Temporal Proximity Alone Does Not Mandate Suppression

The prosecution correctly notes that temporal proximity between the illegal conduct and the consent was minimal. However, temporal proximity alone is not dispositive under *Brown v. Illinois*. The Supreme Court explicitly held that "temporal proximity alone... is not enough" to render consent inadmissible. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. at 103, 95 S. Ct. at 994. Rather, temporal proximity must be considered alongside the other factors, particularly the presence of intervening circumstances. *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 503, 103 S. Ct. 1319, 1327 (1983) (plurality opinion) (holding that "temporal criteria" are not alone sufficient and must be combined with "other facts" showing attenuation).

The prosecution's reliance on minimal temporal proximity as dispositive contradicts established precedent. The *Brown* Court explicitly rejected a per se rule that any consent following an illegal seizure is tainted, instead establishing a totality-of-circumstances approach. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 116-17 (Powell, J., concurring) (explaining that the three factors must be weighed together). The prosecution's argument that temporal proximity alone defeats attenuation misstates the law.

### B. Significant Intervening Circumstances Break the Causal Chain

The trial court correctly found that significant intervening circumstances broke the causal chain between the unlawful pat-down and the consent to search the vehicle.

**1. The Defendant Was Explicitly Informed He Was Free to Go**

The officer testified that after completing the paperwork regarding the suspended license, he told the defendant he was "free to go." Suppression Tr. 19:8-10. This statement is critical to the attenuation analysis. Under *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554-55, 100 S. Ct. 1870, 1877-78 (1980), a seizure occurs only when, considering all circumstances, a reasonable person would not believe he is free to leave. Here, the officer explicitly communicated that the defendant was free to leave. The defendant then chose to remain to wait for his girlfriend. This choice to remain, voluntarily, constitutes a significant intervening circumstance.

The prosecution argues that the defendant's "agreeable" demeanor does not purge the taint. However, the prosecution conflates voluntariness of consent with attenuation. While *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 93 S. Ct. 2041, 36 L. Ed. 2d 854 (1973), establishes that consent must be voluntary, the attenuation inquiry under *Brown* asks whether the consent was an act of free will independent of the illegal seizure. The defendant's decision to stay after being told he was free to go demonstrates such independence.

**2. The Defendant Initiated the Search Interaction**

The most significant intervening circumstance is that the defendant himself initiated the search interaction. The officer's testimony establishes the following sequence:

> "[Defendant] at that point basically patted himself down from top to bottom, starting here, down a couple of— you know, down to his legs. After he did that, he placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me."

The defendant proactively placed his hands on the vehicle and explicitly invited the officer to search him, saying "you can search me." Only after this did the officer conduct the pat-down and then ask to search the car. Suppression Tr. 22:15-25.

This sequence is crucial. The defendant was not merely "agreeable" to the officer's request; he affirmatively initiated the interaction. The prosecution's argument that the pat-down preceded the car search request ignores the defendant's proactive conduct. The defendant created the circumstances leading to the search request by his own volition. This is precisely the type of intervening act that purges the taint of prior illegality. *See United States v. Lopez*, 438 F.3d 1003, 1012 (8th Cir. 2006) (finding attenuation where defendant initiated contact with officers).

**3. The Defendant Was Waiting for His Girlfriend**

The defendant remained at the scene because his girlfriend was coming to pick him up. Suppression Tr. 19:12-18. This provides additional evidence that the defendant's consent was an independent act. He was not in custody or under detention when he consented. He was a free individual waiting for transportation, making a voluntary decision to permit the search. The prosecution's argument that the defendant was somehow coerced by the circumstances ignores the fact that the defendant created those circumstances—he called his girlfriend and arranged for her to pick him up.

The prosecution argues that the officer engaged in flagrant misconduct by conducting an unlawful pat-down. **I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the Defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The Court has reviewed the transcript of the suppression hearing, the factual findings, and the rebuttal arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issue for determination is whether the consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from an unlawful pat-down, or whether the consent was the tainted fruit of a Fourth Amendment violation.

**II. FACTUAL BACKGROUND**

The facts, as found by the lower court and supported by the officer’s testimony, are largely undisputed. In the early morning hours of March 17, 2012, the Defendant pulled his vehicle over to the side of the road. The officer, observing this, activated his emergency lights and approached the vehicle. The Defendant was compliant, polite, and cooperative. He informed the officer he was lost and handed over his cell phone to speak with his girlfriend.

Upon checking the Defendant’s identification, the officer discovered the Defendant’s driver’s license was suspended. The officer issued a citation and a license forfeiture form. The officer testified that after explaining the documents to the Defendant, he told the Defendant he was “free to go.” The Defendant, however, stated his girlfriend was coming to pick him up and waited. Shortly thereafter, the Defendant “re-engaged” the officer regarding the ID procedures, patted himself down, placed his hands on the vehicle, and explicitly told the officer, “you can search me.” The officer conducted a pat-down of the Defendant’s person, finding nothing. The officer then asked, “can I search your car?” to which the Defendant replied, “yes.” The subsequent search of the vehicle yielded drug evidence.

Crucially, the officer testified that he had “no safety concerns,” “no fear for his safety,” and observed no furtive movements or signs of intoxication. He admitted that aside from finding the Defendant’s story of being lost suspicious, there was no other suspicious activity.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Pat-Down of the Defendant**

Both the prosecution and the defense agree, and the Court finds, that the officer’s pat-down of the Defendant violated the Fourth Amendment.

Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 88 S. Ct. 1868, 20 L. Ed. 2d 889 (1968), a police officer may conduct a brief investigatory stop and a limited pat-down search for weapons only when the officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The standard is an objective one; the officer must be able to point to specific facts which, taken together with rational inferences, reasonably warrant the intrusion. *United States v. Place*, 462 U.S. 696, 103 S. Ct. 2635, 77 L. Ed. 2d 110 (1983).

Here, the record is devoid of specific facts supporting a reasonable suspicion that the Defendant was armed and dangerous. The officer explicitly testified that he had “no safety concerns” and “no fear for his safety.” While the Defendant’s behavior was “overly cooperative,” this factor, without more, does not suggest that a suspect is armed. *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323, 129 S. Ct. 781, 172 L. Ed. 2d 694 (2009). Because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the Defendant was armed and dangerous, the pat-down constituted an unreasonable seizure under the Fourth Amendment.

**B. The Validity of the Consent to Search the Vehicle**

Having determined that the pat-down was unlawful, the Court must determine whether the subsequent consent to search the vehicle was tainted by this illegality. The prosecution argues that the consent was the product of the illegal pat-down and must be suppressed as “fruit of the poisonous tree.” *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 83 S. Ct. 407, 9 L. Ed. 2d 441 (1963). The defense counters that the consent was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the prior illegality.

The admissibility of evidence obtained after an illegal seizure turns on whether the consent was given voluntarily and was an act of free will sufficient to purge the primary taint. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 103, 95 S. Ct. 990, 43 L. Ed. 2d 366 (1975); *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 103 S. Ct. 1319, 75 L. Ed. 2d 229 (1983). In determining whether the taint has been purged, courts consider three factors: (1) the temporal proximity of the illegal conduct and the consent; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 103.

Applying these factors to the present case, the Court finds that the consent to search the vehicle was valid and admissible.

**1. Temporal Proximity**

The prosecution correctly notes that the temporal proximity between the pat-down and the request to search the vehicle was close. However, temporal proximity alone is not dispositive. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 103. It must be weighed alongside the other factors, particularly the presence of intervening circumstances. *Royer*, 460 U.S. at 503.

**2. Intervening Circumstances**

The Court finds the presence of significant intervening circumstances that break the causal chain between the illegal pat-down and the consent to search the vehicle.

First, the officer explicitly told the Defendant he was “free to go” before the search interaction began. Under *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554-55, 100 S. Ct. 1870, 1877-78 (1980), a seizure occurs only when, considering all circumstances, a reasonable person would not believe he is free to leave. Here, the officer’s statement communicated that the detention regarding the suspended license had concluded. The Defendant chose to remain at the scene to wait for his girlfriend. This choice to remain, voluntarily, constitutes a significant intervening circumstance. The prosecution’s argument that this statement was a nullity because the officer did not immediately depart is unpersuasive. The officer’s presence does not negate the Defendant’s freedom to leave, especially where the officer had already communicated that the Defendant was free to go.

Second, and most significantly, the Defendant himself initiated the search interaction. The record shows that the Defendant patted himself down, placed his hands on the vehicle, and explicitly invited the officer to search him, saying, “you can search me.” The officer’s request to search the car followed the Defendant’s own invitation to be searched. This proactive behavior by the Defendant constitutes a significant intervening act that demonstrates his willingness to cooperate, independent of any coercion by the officer. *See United States v. Lopez*, 438 F.3d 1003, 1012 (8th Cir. 2006) (finding attenuation where defendant initiated contact with officers). The prosecution’s attempt to characterize this invitation as merely “suspicious behavior” that justified the illegal pat-down misses the mark. Regardless of whether the officer found the behavior unusual, it was the Defendant who voluntarily initiated the physical search aspect of the encounter.

**3. Purpose and Flagrancy of the Official Misconduct**

The third *Brown* factor examines the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 104. The prosecution argues that the officer engaged in flagrant misconduct by conducting a pat-down without reasonable suspicion. However, the record does not support a finding of flagrancy. The officer conducted a routine investigation regarding a suspended license. The pat-down, while lacking the specific justification required by *Terry*, was not conducted in a harassing or coercive manner. The officer’s weapon was never drawn, and the testimony indicates the encounter remained professional throughout. This is not the type of egregious misconduct that *Brown* contemplates. *See United States v. Simpson*, 439 F.3d 490, 496 (7th Cir. 2006).

**C. Application of the Fruit of the Poisonous Tree Doctrine**

The prosecution argues that the drug evidence was discovered “only because the officer first conducted an unlawful pat-down and then used that illegality to justify the car search.” This argument mischaracterizes the factual sequence. The car search was not a direct consequence of the pat-down. The Defendant had already invited the search before the pat-down occurred. The officer’s testimony that he had “reasonable suspicion” to search the car based on the Defendant’s behavior does not change the fact that the Defendant voluntarily consented to the search after being told he was free to go and after initiating the search interaction himself.

Because the Defendant was informed he was free to go, chose to stay, and voluntarily initiated the search interaction, his consent to search the vehicle was an independent act of free will. Any taint from the unlawful pat-down was purged by these intervening circumstances.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that while the officer’s pat-down of the Defendant lacked reasonable suspicion and violated the Fourth Amendment, the subsequent consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the prior illegality. The prosecution has not met its burden of proving that the consent to search the vehicle was the product of the unlawful pat-down.

Accordingly, the Defendant’s motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

The defendant's invitation to search was not a response to the pat-down—it was a spontaneous, proactive expression of willingness that preceded the pat-down.

Furthermore, the prosecution's argument that "the defendant never had a meaningful opportunity to act independently" ignores the fact that the defendant was told he was free to go, chose to stay voluntarily, and then initiated the search interaction entirely on his own initiative. The defendant had every opportunity to leave, to decline to engage with the officer, or to refuse consent. Instead, he chose to remain, to re-engage the officer, and to invite a search.

---

## II. APPLICATION OF THE *BROWN* FACTORS SUPPORTS ATTENUATION

### A. Temporal Proximity

The prosecution correctly notes that the temporal proximity between the pat-down and the request to search the vehicle was close. However, as the Supreme Court held in *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. at 103, temporal proximity alone is not dispositive. The Court must weigh temporal proximity alongside the other factors, particularly the presence of intervening circumstances. *See Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 503, 103 S. Ct. 1319, 1327, 75 L. Ed. 2d 229 (1983).

Here, the presence of significant intervening circumstances—the defendant's informed status as a person free to go, his voluntary choice to remain, and his proactive initiation of the search interaction—distinguishes this case from the scenario presented in *Brown* and other cases where temporal proximity was found dispositive. The prosecution cannot meet its burden by pointing to temporal proximity alone while ignoring the substantial intervening circumstances present here.

### B. Intervening Circumstances

The prosecution argues that "there were no intervening circumstances to break the causal chain." This argument fails for the reasons stated above:

1. **The defendant was told he was free to go**: This communication, which does not appear in *Brown* or similar cases, informed the defendant that the detention had concluded.

2. **The defendant voluntarily chose to remain**: Rather than departing, the defendant chose to stay to wait for his girlfriend, demonstrating independent volition.

3. **The defendant initiated the search interaction**: The defendant's proactive conduct—patting himself down, placing his hands on the vehicle, and inviting the search—constitutes the most significant intervening circumstance.

The prosecution's reliance on *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. at 503, is misplaced. In *Royer*, the defendant was detained at an airport, his luggage was seized, and he was taken to a separate room for questioning—all without consent. None of those circumstances are present here. The defendant here was told he was free to go, chose to remain, and proactively invited the search.

### C. Purpose and Flagrancy of Official Misconduct

The prosecution's characterization of the officer's conduct as "flagrant" is not supported by the record. In *Brown*, the Supreme Court explained that the third factor examines whether the official misconduct was "egregious" or "purposeful." 422 U.S. at 104. The Seventh Circuit in *United States v. Simpson*, 439 F.3d 490, 496 (7th Cir. 2006), further elaborated that misconduct is flagrant when it is "egregious" or "purposeful" and when officers "act without regard for constitutional constraints."

Here, the officer conducted a routine traffic investigation regarding a suspended license. While the pat-down lacked the specific justification required by *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 88 S. Ct. 1868, 20 L. Ed. 2d 889 (1968), the record does not support a finding that this conduct was egregious or purposeful. The officer's weapon was never drawn. The encounter remained professional throughout. The officer did not harass or coerce the defendant. The officer's testimony indicates he believed the defendant's behavior was suspicious, however misguided that belief may have been in hindsight.

This is not the type of egregious misconduct that *Brown* contemplates. The officer did not engage in the kind of prolonged detention, coercive questioning, or deliberate disregard for constitutional limits present in cases where attenuation has been denied. The prosecution's attempt to elevate this conduct to the level of "flagrant" misconduct is unsupported by the record and should be rejected.

---

## III. THE PROSECUTION HAS NOT MET ITS BURDEN OF PROVING TAINT

Under *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. at 103, when consent follows an unlawful seizure, the evidence is presumptively inadmissible unless the government proves attenuation by a preponderance of the evidence. The prosecution must show that the consent was "an act of free will" sufficient to purge the primary taint. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 225, 93 S. Ct. 2041, 2046, 36 L. Ed. 2d 854 (1973).

The prosecution has not met this burden. The defendant was told he was free to go, chose to remain voluntarily, initiated the search interaction, and gave his consent to search the vehicle. These circumstances demonstrate that the defendant's consent was an independent act of free will, not the product of the prior illegality.

The prosecution's reliance on *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 83 S. Ct. 407, 9 L. Ed. 2d 441 (1963), is unavailing. In *Wong Sun*, the defendants were arrested without probable cause, and their statements were obtained through exploitation of those unlawful arrests. The Court held that the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine applies to evidence obtained through exploitation of an unlawful seizure. However, the Court also recognized that evidence may be admitted if it is sufficiently attenuated from the illegality.

Here, the prosecution cannot show that the car search was obtained through "exploitation" of the illegal pat-down. The defendant had already invited the search before the pat-down occurred. The pat-down found nothing. The defendant's subsequent consent to search the vehicle was given in response to his own prior invitation, not as a result of the pat-down. The prosecution's argument that "the car search would not have occurred" absent the pat-down is speculative and ignores the defendant's proactive conduct.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the lower court's ruling denying the motion to suppress. The officer's pat-down, while lacking reasonable suspicion, does not require suppression of the drug evidence because the defendant's subsequent consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the prior illegality. The prosecution has failed to meet its burden of proving that the consent was the tainted fruit of the poisonous tree.

The defendant was told he was free to go, chose to remain voluntarily, and proactively initiated the search interaction. His consent to search the vehicle was an independent act of free will that broke any causal chain between the unlawful pat-down and the evidence discovered. The Fourth Amendment does not require suppression of evidence obtained through such a voluntary, independent act of consent.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court **AFFIRM** the lower court's ruling and **DENY** the prosecution's motion to reverse.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the Defendant’s motion to suppress evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The Court has carefully reviewed the transcript of the suppression hearing, the factual findings, and the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The issue for determination is whether the consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from an unlawful pat-down, or whether the consent was the tainted fruit of a Fourth Amendment violation.

**II. FACTUAL BACKGROUND**

The facts, as found by the lower court and supported by the officer’s testimony, are largely undisputed. In the early morning hours of March 17, 2012, the Defendant pulled his vehicle over to the side of the road. The officer, observing this, activated his emergency lights and approached the vehicle. The Defendant was compliant, polite, and cooperative. He informed the officer he was lost and handed over his cell phone to speak with his girlfriend.

Upon checking the Defendant’s identification, the officer discovered the Defendant’s driver’s license was suspended. The officer issued a citation and a license forfeiture form. The officer testified that after explaining the documents to the Defendant, he told the Defendant he was “free to go.” The Defendant, however, stated his girlfriend was coming to pick him up and waited. Shortly thereafter, the Defendant “re-engaged” the officer regarding the ID procedures, patted himself down, placed his hands on the vehicle, and explicitly told the officer, “you can search me.” The officer conducted a pat-down of the Defendant’s person, finding nothing. The officer then asked, “can I search your car?” to which the Defendant replied, “yes.” The subsequent search of the vehicle yielded drug evidence.

Crucially, the officer testified that he had “no safety concerns,” “no fear for his safety,” and observed no furtive movements or signs of intoxication. He admitted that aside from finding the Defendant’s story of being lost suspicious, there was no other suspicious activity.

**III. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Pat-Down of the Defendant**

Both the prosecution and the defense agree, and the Court finds, that the officer’s pat-down of the Defendant violated the Fourth Amendment.

Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 88 S. Ct. 1868, 20 L. Ed. 2d 889 (1968), a police officer may conduct a brief investigatory stop and a limited pat-down search for weapons only when the officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The standard is an objective one; the officer must be able to point to specific facts which, taken together with rational inferences, reasonably warrant the intrusion. *United States v. Place*, 462 U.S. 696, 103 S. Ct. 2635, 77 L. Ed. 2d 110 (1983).

Here, the record is devoid of specific facts supporting a reasonable suspicion that the Defendant was armed and dangerous. The officer explicitly testified that he had “no safety concerns” and “no fear for his safety.” While the Defendant’s behavior was “overly cooperative,” this factor, without more, does not suggest that a suspect is armed. *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323, 129 S. Ct. 781, 172 L. Ed. 2d 694 (2009). Because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the Defendant was armed and dangerous, the pat-down constituted an unreasonable seizure under the Fourth Amendment.

**B. The Validity of the Consent to Search the Vehicle**

Having determined that the pat-down was unlawful, the Court must determine whether the subsequent consent to search the vehicle was tainted by this illegality. The prosecution argues that the consent was the product of the illegal pat-down and must be suppressed as “fruit of the poisonous tree.” *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 83 S. Ct. 407, 9 L. Ed. 2d 441 (1963). The defense counters that the consent was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the prior illegality.

The admissibility of evidence obtained after an illegal seizure turns on whether the consent was given voluntarily and was an act of free will sufficient to purge the primary taint. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 103, 95 S. Ct. 990, 43 L. Ed. 2d 366 (1975); *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 103 S. Ct. 1319, 75 L. Ed. 2d 229 (1983). In determining whether the taint has been purged, courts consider three factors: (1) the temporal proximity of the illegal conduct and the consent; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 103.

Applying these factors to the present case, the Court finds that the consent to search the vehicle was valid and admissible.

**1. Temporal Proximity**

The prosecution correctly notes that the temporal proximity between the pat-down and the request to search the vehicle was close. However, temporal proximity alone is not dispositive. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 103. It must be weighed alongside the other factors, particularly the presence of intervening circumstances. *Royer*, 460 U.S. at 503.

**2. Intervening Circumstances**

The Court finds the presence of significant intervening circumstances that break the causal chain between the illegal pat-down and the consent to search the vehicle.

First, the officer explicitly told the Defendant he was “free to go” before the search interaction began. Under *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554-55, 100 S. Ct. 1870, 1877-78 (1980), a seizure occurs only when, considering all circumstances, a reasonable person would not believe he is free to leave. Here, the officer’s statement communicated that the detention regarding the suspended license had concluded. The Defendant chose to remain at the scene to wait for his girlfriend. This choice to remain, voluntarily, constitutes a significant intervening circumstance. The prosecution’s argument that this statement was a nullity because the officer did not immediately depart is unpersuasive. The officer’s presence does not negate the Defendant’s freedom to leave, especially where the officer had already communicated that the Defendant was free to go.

Second, and most significantly, the Defendant himself initiated the search interaction. The record shows that the Defendant patted himself down, placed his hands on the vehicle, and explicitly invited the officer to search him, saying, “you can search me.” The officer’s request to search the car followed the Defendant’s own invitation to be searched. This proactive behavior by the Defendant constitutes a significant intervening act that demonstrates his willingness to cooperate, independent of any coercion by the officer. *See United States v. Lopez*, 438 F.3d 1003, 1012 (8th Cir. 2006) (finding attenuation where defendant initiated contact with officers). The prosecution’s attempt to characterize this invitation as merely “suspicious behavior” that justified the illegal pat-down misses the mark. Regardless of whether the officer found the behavior unusual, it was the Defendant who voluntarily initiated the physical search aspect of the encounter.

**3. Purpose and Flagracy of the Official Misconduct**

The third *Brown* factor examines the purpose and flagracy of the official misconduct. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 104. The prosecution argues that the officer engaged in flagrant misconduct by conducting a pat-down without reasonable suspicion. However, the record does not support a finding of flagracy. The officer conducted a routine investigation regarding a suspended license. The pat-down, while lacking the specific justification required by *Terry*, was not conducted in a harassing or coercive manner. The officer’s weapon was never drawn, and the testimony indicates the encounter remained professional throughout. This is not the type of egregious misconduct that *Brown* contemplates. *See United States v. Simpson*, 439 F.3d 490, 496 (7th Cir. 2006).

**C. Application of the Fruit of the Poisonous Tree Doctrine**

The prosecution argues that the drug evidence was discovered “only because the officer first conducted an unlawful pat-down and then used that illegality to justify the car search.” This argument mischaracterizes the factual sequence. The car search was not a direct consequence of the pat-down. The Defendant had already invited the search before the pat-down occurred. The officer’s testimony that he had “reasonable suspicion” to search the car based on the Defendant’s behavior does not change the fact that the Defendant voluntarily consented to the search after being told he was free to go and after initiating the search interaction himself.

Because the Defendant was informed he was free to go, chose to stay, and voluntarily initiated the search interaction, his consent to search the vehicle was an independent act of free will. Any taint from the unlawful pat-down was purged by these intervening circumstances.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that while the officer’s pat-down of the Defendant lacked reasonable suspicion and violated the Fourth Amendment, the subsequent consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the prior illegality. The prosecution has not met its burden of proving that the consent to search the vehicle was the product of the unlawful pat-down.

Accordingly, the Defendant’s motion to suppress is **DENIED**.
