The district court held that CBECA "governed all of Joki's claims, as brought against the State Defendants." This broad interpretation of CBECA's scope was error.

CBECA, codified at Idaho Code sections 6-2201 through 6-2216, was enacted to provide a mechanism for addressing constitutional claims *after* a school district has been found to have failed in its obligations. However, CBECA was not designed to—and does not—bar the initial filing of constitutional challenges to the State's overall funding system. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s order dismissing the State Defendants from the action. The Appellant, Joki, challenges the constitutionality of Idaho’s system of funding education. The district court dismissed the action, holding that the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act ("CBECA"), Idaho Code sections 6-2201–2216, provided the exclusive mechanism for such claims and that the Appellant failed to comply with its procedural prerequisites. The district court further held that the Appellant’s claim failed to state a cause of action under existing precedent. Upon review of the opening arguments submitted by the Respondent (State Defendants) and the Appellant (Joki), the Court finds that the district court erred in its application of the CBECA and its interpretation of the scope of *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 155 Idaho 397, 313 P.3d 1110 (2013) ("ISEEO V").

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The CBECA Does Not Bar Systemic Challenges to the State’s Funding System**

The primary issue on appeal is whether the CBECA’s procedural requirements bar the Appellant’s claim against the State Defendants. The Respondent argues that the CBECA is the exclusive mechanism for resolving constitutional claims related to education funding and that the Appellant failed to exhaust the required administrative remedies. Specifically, the Respondent points to *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) ("ISEEO IV"), which established that plaintiffs must strictly adhere to procedural prerequisites—including a bench trial against a local school district, a finding of failure, a remedial order, and continued noncompliance—before naming the State as a defendant.

While the Court acknowledges the holding in *ISEEO IV*, the Respondent’s interpretation of the CBECA’s scope is overly broad. The Appellant correctly argues that the CBECA was designed to address claims where a *local school district* has failed to provide constitutionally required services. The statute’s procedural framework is sequential: it presupposes a finding of failure at the district level before the State can be joined.

The Appellant’s claim, however, is distinct. He challenges the constitutionality of the *State’s overall funding system* itself, not the failure of a specific local district to administer services. As the Appellant contends, applying the CBECA’s exhaustion requirements to a systemic challenge creates an impossible procedural Catch-22. A plaintiff cannot be required to first prove that a local district failed to provide services due to inadequate funding when the plaintiff’s fundamental allegation is that the State’s funding mechanism is constitutionally deficient. To require a plaintiff to exhaust remedies that are predicated on a district-level failure before challenging the systemic source of that potential failure would effectively insulate the State’s funding scheme from judicial review.

The district court erred by holding that the CBECA governs *all* claims against the State Defendants. A proper reading of the CBECA, consistent with the separation of powers principles noted in *Idaho v. Johnson*, 130 Idaho 500, 943 P.2d 946 (1997), suggests that the legislature intended to provide a remedy for district-level noncompliance, not to bar facial challenges to the State’s general educational policy. Therefore, the district court’s dismissal based on the Appellant’s failure to exhaust CBECA remedies was improper.

**B. The Appellant States a Viable Claim Under Article IX, Section 1**

The district court also dismissed the Appellant’s claim on the merits, reasoning that he failed to state a claim to enforce *ISEEO V*. The district court noted that *ISEEO V* addressed the narrow issue of funding for school facilities and that the Appellant did not allege inadequate school facilities.

The Respondent urges this Court to affirm that ruling, arguing that *ISEEO V* is limited to facilities and that the Appellant’s broader challenge to the education funding system is foreclosed. The Court disagrees. While *ISEEO V* specifically addressed the unconstitutionality of the school facility funding system, it did not establish that other aspects of the education funding system are immune from constitutional scrutiny. As the Appellant argues, *ISEEO V* serves as a floor, not a ceiling, for constitutional analysis.

The Appellant seeks a declaratory judgment that the "current system of funding education" violates Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution. This claim is grounded in the broad mandate established in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 128 Idaho 714, 918 P.2d 247 (1996) ("ISEEO I"), which recognizes the State’s obligation to provide a "general, uniform and efficient" system of public education. The district court’s reliance on *ISEEO V* to dismiss a claim that is broader than the specific facts of *ISEEO V* was error. As noted in *State ex rel. Idaho Dep’t of Educ. v. State Bd. of Educ.*, 142 Idaho 564, 130 P.3d 522 (2006), courts must strictly construe prior rulings, but they must not use those rulings to bar legitimate constitutional challenges that fall outside the specific factual context of the precedent.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s order dismissing the State Defendants is reversed. The CBECA’s procedural prerequisites do not apply to the Appellant’s systemic challenge to the State’s funding framework, and the Appellant has stated a viable claim under Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution independent of the specific holding in *ISEEO V*. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

The State Defendants respectfully submit this rebuttal to counter the Plaintiff's mischaracterizations of the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act ("CBECA"), Idaho Code sections 6-2201–2216, and controlling precedent. The Plaintiff's rebuttal fundamentally misreads the statutory framework, ignores the sequential logic of CBECA, and attempts to relitigate issues already resolved by this Court's Preliminary Ruling. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the Preliminary Ruling and reject the Plaintiff's attempt to impose blanket exhaustion requirements on systemic constitutional challenges.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Plaintiff's Misreading of CBECA Ignores the Statute's Textual Structure and Legislative Intent

The Plaintiff contends that CBECA provides the "exclusive mechanism" for all constitutional claims related to education funding, citing *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) ("ISEEO IV"). However, the Plaintiff conflates two distinct concepts: exclusivity of remedy versus applicability to all claims regardless of nature.

In *ISEEO IV*, this Court held that CBECA "provides the exclusive mechanism for resolving constitutional claims related to education funding and services" in the sense that litigants cannot circumvent the statutory framework when seeking relief against the State for district-level failures. 140 Idaho at 597, 97 P.3d at 464. Critically, however, this holding presupposes that the underlying claim involves allegations that a local school district failed to provide constitutionally required services—triggering the sequential process outlined in the statute.

The Plaintiff's argument that CBECA applies to "all constitutional claims related to education funding, including systemic challenges" reads the word "systemic" out of the statute entirely. This interpretation cannot be sustained because:

1. **CBECA's definitional scope is limited**: Idaho Code § 6-2202(3) defines "constitutionally required educational services" as services that "a local school district is required to provide under the Constitution of the State of Idaho." The statute explicitly contemplates district-level obligations, not systemic State funding policies in the abstract.

2. **The remedial structure assumes district-level failure**: Idaho Code § 6-2204 authorizes district courts to issue orders "to assist the local school district in administering constitutionally required educational services." This language confirms the legislature's intent to address failures at the district level, not facial challenges to State funding formulas.

The Plaintiff cites *Idaho v. Johnson*, 130 Idaho 500, 943 P.2d 946 (1997), for the proposition that CBECA balances judicial and legislative prerogatives. However, *Johnson* actually supports the Defense's position. In *Johnson*, this Court recognized that "the judiciary must not intrude upon the legislative prerogative to appropriate funds." 130 Idaho at 504, 943 P.2d at 950. A systemic funding challenge—challenging the State's general funding formula on its face—presents a pure question of constitutional interpretation that does not require the factual predicate of district-level noncompliance. Requiring exhaustion under these circumstances would improperly insulate the State's funding system from any judicial scrutiny, contrary to the separation of powers principles articulated in *Johnson*.

### B. The Plaintiff's "Catch-22" Argument Is Not Baseless—It Is a Legitimate Constitutional Concern

The Plaintiff dismisses the Defense's catch-22 argument as "baseless," but this characterization reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of the pleading at issue. The Plaintiff argues that CBECA's sequential process is "intentional and necessary to ensure that State liability is imposed only after local failures are judicially confirmed."

This reasoning, however, assumes that every constitutional education funding claim arises from a district-level failure. The Plaintiff's complaint does not allege that Meridian Joint District #2 or any other local district failed to provide constitutionally required services. Rather, the Plaintiff challenges the constitutionality of the State's overall funding system itself—arguing that the system, by its design, cannot provide adequate education funding.

As the Preliminary Ruling correctly recognized, requiring a plaintiff to first prove that a local district failed to provide services due to inadequate funding, before being permitted to challenge the systemic source of that potential failure, creates an impossible procedural barrier. The plaintiff cannot be required to satisfy factual predicates that are logically dependent on the very constitutional deficiency he seeks to challenge.

The Plaintiff's reliance on *State ex rel. Idaho Dep't of Educ. v. State Bd. of Educ.*, 142 Idaho 564, 130 P.3d 522 (2006), is misplaced. In that case, this Court cautioned against expanding prior rulings beyond their factual contexts—a principle that cuts in favor of the Defense. *ISEEO IV* addressed claims involving district-level failures; it did not contemplate facial challenges to the State's general funding system. The Plaintiff asks this Court to extend *ISEEO IV* beyond its factual scope, which *State ex rel. Idaho Dep't of Educ.* expressly forbids.

### C. The Plaintiff's Fee Reimbursement Claim Fails to State a Viable Cause of Action

Regarding the claim for reimbursement of certain fees imposed by school districts, the Plaintiff argues that this claim "fell squarely within CBECA's scope." This argument fails for two independent reasons:

1. **The claim is not against the State**: The second amended complaint seeks "reimbursement from Meridian Joint District #2 or the legislature of certain fees imposed by the school districts." The Plaintiff has already obtained judgment against Meridian Joint District #2 in the amount of $85. To the extent the Plaintiff seeks reimbursement from the legislature, this claim presents a non-justiciable political question implicating the separation of powers.

2. **CBECA does not authorize fee reimbursement**: Even assuming CBECA applies, the statute authorizes district courts to issue orders "to assist the local school district in administering constitutionally required educational services." Idaho Code § 6-2204(1). This remedial authority does not encompass retroactive reimbursement of fees previously collected by districts. The Plaintiff's interpretation would transform CBECA into a general tort statute, which the legislature clearly did not intend.

### D. The Preliminary Ruling Correctly Held That ISEEO V Does Not Foreclose Broader Constitutional Challenges

The Plaintiff argues that *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 155 Idaho 397, 313 P.3d 1110 (2013) ("ISEEO V"), is "limited to facilities" and does not preclude other constitutional challenges. Curiously, the Plaintiff then argues that this Court should affirm the district court's dismissal based on the very same reasoning—that Joki's claim "failed to state a claim under ISEEO V."

The Plaintiff's position is internally inconsistent. If *ISEEO V* is truly limited to facility funding, then the district court erred by dismissing claims that fall outside that narrow scope. The Preliminary Ruling correctly recognized this inconsistency:

> "While *ISEEO V* specifically addressed the unconstitutionality of the school facility funding system, it did not establish that other aspects of the education funding system are immune from constitutional scrutiny. As the Appellant argues, *ISEEO V* serves as a floor, not a ceiling, for constitutional analysis."

The Plaintiff argues that this interpretation is "incorrect" because *ISEEO V* was a "narrow ruling limited to facility funding." But this is precisely the Preliminary Ruling's point—the Plaintiff cannot have it both ways. Either *ISEEO V* is narrow (in which case it does not foreclose broader challenges), or it is broad (in which case the Plaintiff should have alleged facility deficiencies). The Plaintiff cannot invoke *ISEEO V* to foreclose claims while simultaneously arguing that *ISEEO V* is too narrow to support those same claims.

The Plaintiff's reliance on *ISEEO V*'s exclusivity is particularly unavailing given that *ISEEO I*—the foundational case establishing the State's obligation to provide a "general, uniform and efficient" system of public education—remains good law. *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. The Plaintiff has cited *ISEEO I* as grounds for his declaratory judgment claim, and the Preliminary Ruling correctly held that this claim is viable independent of *ISEEO V*.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the district court’s order dismissing the State Defendants from the action. The Appellant, Joki, challenges the constitutionality of Idaho’s system of funding education. The district court dismissed the action, holding that the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act ("CBECA"), Idaho Code sections 6-2201–2216, provided the exclusive mechanism for such claims and that the Appellant failed to comply with its procedural prerequisites. The district court further held that the Appellant’s claim failed to state a cause of action under existing precedent. Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by the Respondent (State Defendants) and the Appellant (Joki), the Court adheres to its preliminary finding that the district court erred. The Respondent’s arguments fail to demonstrate that the CBECA’s procedural framework was intended to foreclose facial challenges to the State’s funding system, nor do they establish that the Appellant’s claim is limited to the narrow factual context of *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 155 Idaho 397, 313 P.3d 1110 (2013) ("ISEEO V").

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The CBECA’s Procedural Framework Does Not Bar Facial Challenges to State Funding Policy**

The Respondent argues that the CBECA is the "exclusive mechanism" for all constitutional claims related to education funding, citing *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) ("ISEEO IV"). The Respondent contends that the procedural prerequisites—a bench trial against a local school district, a finding of failure, a remedial order, and continued noncompliance—must be satisfied before the State can be named as a defendant, regardless of the nature of the claim.

The Court is not persuaded that the legislature intended the CBECA to act as a jurisdictional bar to facial challenges against the State’s general funding scheme. As the Appellant correctly notes, the text of the CBECA defines "constitutionally required educational services" in terms of what "a local school district is required to provide." Idaho Code § 6-2202(3). Furthermore, the remedial provisions authorize district courts to issue orders "to assist the local school district in administering constitutionally required educational services." Idaho Code § 6-2204(1). This language indicates a legislative intent to provide a remedial process for *district-level* failures, not to establish a procedural gauntlet for plaintiffs seeking to challenge the *State’s* statutory funding formulas on their face.

The Respondent’s interpretation creates a logical paradox, or "catch-22," which the district court failed to adequately address. If a plaintiff alleges that the State’s funding system is constitutionally inadequate on its face, requiring that plaintiff to first prove a specific local district failed to provide services—due to that allegedly inadequate funding—places the cart before the horse. The Respondent dismisses this concern as "baseless," but the Appellant is correct that such a requirement would insulate the State’s funding policy from judicial review. A plaintiff cannot be expected to exhaust administrative remedies that are predicated on a finding of district-level failure when the core allegation is that the State’s systemic funding mechanism prevents districts from succeeding in the first place.

While *ISEEO IV* held that the CBECA provides the exclusive mechanism for resolving constitutional claims related to education funding, that case involved a challenge to the State's failure to fund specific facilities after a district-level finding of inadequacy. It did not involve a pre-enforcement facial challenge to the general funding system. To extend *ISEEO IV* to bar such facial challenges would be to expand the statute beyond its textual scope, contrary to the principle of strict construction articulated in *State ex rel. Idaho Dep’t of Educ. v. State Bd. of Educ.*, 142 Idaho 564, 130 P.3d 522 (2006). Therefore, the district court erred in applying the CBECA’s exhaustion requirements to the Appellant’s systemic challenge.

**B. The Appellant States a Viable Claim Under Article IX, Section 1 Independent of ISEEO V**

The Respondent argues that the district court correctly dismissed the Appellant’s claim because he failed to allege facts regarding school facilities, which was the specific subject of *ISEEO V*. The Respondent maintains that *ISEEO V* is a narrow ruling and that the Appellant cannot rely on it to support a broader challenge.

The Court agrees with the Appellant that *ISEEO V* is indeed narrow. In *ISEEO V*, this Court specifically held that "Idaho’s system for financing school construction and maintenance violates the Idaho Constitution." 155 Idaho at 402, 313 P.3d at 1115. The Respondent’s assertion that the Appellant’s claim fails because it does not allege facility deficiencies proves the Appellant’s point: the claim is broader than *ISEEO V*. The district court erred by treating *ISEEO V* as the sole avenue for challenging the education funding system.

The Appellant seeks a declaratory judgment based on Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution, which establishes the State’s obligation to provide a "general, uniform and efficient" system of public schools. This obligation was definitively recognized in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 128 Idaho 714, 918 P.2d 247 (1996) ("ISEEO I"). The fact that *ISEEO V* addressed one specific component of that system—facilities—does not foreclose the Appellant from challenging other components or the system as a whole. The Respondent’s attempt to use the narrowness of *ISEEO V* as a shield against the broader constitutional mandate of *ISEEO I* is unavailing.

**C. The Fee Reimbursement Claim**

Regarding the claim for fee reimbursement, the Respondent argues it falls within the CBECA. The Appellant counters that the claim against the legislature presents a non-justiciable political question and that the CBECA does not authorize retroactive reimbursement. The Appellant’s arguments regarding the nature of the relief sought against the State are persuasive. However, because the primary issue on appeal is the systemic challenge to the funding mechanism and the viability of the declaratory judgment action, the Court’s reversal of the dismissal on those grounds renders the specific disposition of the fee claim against the State a matter for further proceedings on remand.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s order dismissing the State Defendants is reversed. The CBECA’s procedural prerequisites do not apply to the Appellant’s facial challenge to the State’s funding framework, and the Appellant has stated a viable claim under Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution independent of the specific holding in *ISEEO V*. The Respondent’s arguments to the contrary misinterpret the scope of the CBECA and the controlling precedent. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the district court’s order dismissing the State Defendants from the action. The Appellant, Joki, challenges the constitutionality of Idaho’s system of funding public schools under Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution. The district court dismissed the action, concluding that the Constitutionally Based Educational Claims Act ("CBECA"), Idaho Code sections 6-2201–2216, provides the exclusive mechanism for such claims and that the Appellant failed to comply with its procedural prerequisites. The district court also held that the Appellant failed to state a claim based on precedent regarding school facilities. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both the State Defendants (Prosecution) and Joki (Defense), the Court affirms its previous determination that the district court erred. The State’s arguments fail to demonstrate that the CBECA was intended to foreclose facial challenges to the State’s funding framework, nor do they establish that the procedural hurdles of the CBECA can be constitutionally applied to a claim seeking declaratory relief on the validity of the funding system itself.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The CBECA Is Not a Jurisdictional Bar to Facial Challenges of State Funding Policy**

The State Defendants argue that the CBECA is the "exclusive mechanism" for all constitutional challenges related to education funding, relying heavily on *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 140 Idaho 586, 97 P.3d 453 (2004) ("ISEEO IV"). They contend that the statutory language defining "constitutionally required educational services" as those a "local school district is required to provide" (Idaho Code § 6-2202(3)) and authorizing orders to "assist the local school district" (Idaho Code § 6-2204(1)) mandates a sequential process where the State cannot be sued until after a local district is found to be noncompliant.

While the Court acknowledges the holding in *ISEEO IV*, that case involved a challenge to the State's failure to fund specific facilities after a district-level finding of inadequacy. It did not address, nor did it purport to address, a facial challenge to the general statutory funding formulas. The State’s interpretation would effectively rewrite the CBECA to act as a jurisdictional shield for the State’s funding policy, insulating it from judicial review unless a plaintiff first navigates a complex, district-level remedial process.

As the Defense correctly argues, applying the CBECA’s procedural prerequisites to a facial challenge creates a logical paradox. If the Appellant alleges that the State’s funding system is constitutionally inadequate on its face, requiring him to first prove a specific local district failed to provide services—due to that allegedly inadequate funding—places the cart before the horse. The State cannot have it both ways; they cannot argue that the funding system is the exclusive province of the legislature while simultaneously arguing that a plaintiff must prove the failure of that system at the local level before questioning the system’s validity in court.

The State relies on *State ex rel. Idaho Dep’t of Educ. v. State Bd. of Educ.*, 142 Idaho 564, 130 P.3d 522 (2006), to argue for strict construction of the statute. However, strict construction does not permit the Court to ignore the constitutional mandate of Article IX, Section 1. The CBECA is a remedial statute designed to assist local districts; it is not a statute designed to strip the judiciary of its authority to review the constitutionality of the State’s legislative enactments. Therefore, the district court erred in holding that the CBECA’s exhaustion requirements barred the Appellant’s facial challenge.

**B. The Procedural Requirements of the CBECA Cannot Be Used to Deny Judicial Review of Constitutional Violations**

The State Defendants argue that the procedural sequence required by the CBECA—bench trial, finding of failure, remedial order, and continued noncompliance—is necessary to respect the separation of powers and prevent courts from intruding on the legislative prerogative to appropriate funds, citing *Idaho v. Johnson*, 130 Idaho 500, 943 P.2d 946 (1997).

The Court agrees with the principle articulated in *Idaho v. Johnson* that the judiciary must not legislate or dictate specific appropriations. However, that principle does not extend to barring a court from hearing a claim that the legislature has violated the Constitution in the exercise of its appropriations power. A declaratory judgment that the current funding system is unconstitutional is not an intrusion into legislative power; it is the fulfillment of the judicial duty to interpret the Constitution.

The State’s argument that these requirements prevent "abstract policy disagreements" is unpersuasive in this context. A facial challenge to the statutory framework is a concrete legal controversy, not an abstract disagreement. By requiring the Appellant to exhaust remedies that are predicated on a finding of district-level failure, the CBECA—as applied by the district court—would require the Appellant to demonstrate the very harm (inadequate services) that is the inevitable result of the allegedly unconstitutional funding scheme before he can even challenge the scheme itself. This is an impermissible burden on the right to seek judicial redress for a violation of Article IX, Section 1.

**C. The Appellant Has Stated a Viable Claim Under Article IX, Section 1 Independent of ISEEO V**

The State argues that the district court correctly dismissed the claim because the Appellant failed to allege facts regarding school facilities, the specific subject of *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 155 Idaho 397, 313 P.3d 1110 (2013) ("ISEEO V"). The State contends that because *ISEEO V* is narrow, the Appellant cannot rely on it to support a broader challenge.

The Court agrees that *ISEEO V* is narrow. However, the State’s argument proves the Appellant’s point: the Appellant’s claim is broader than *ISEEO V*. The district court erred by treating *ISEEO V* as the exclusive avenue for challenging the education funding system. The Appellant seeks a declaratory judgment based on Article IX, Section 1, which establishes the State’s obligation to provide a "general, uniform and efficient" system of public schools. This obligation was definitively recognized in *Idaho Schools for Equal Educational Opportunity v. State*, 128 Idaho 714, 918 P.2d 247 (1996) ("ISEEO I"). The fact that *ISEEO V* addressed one specific component of that system—facilities—does not foreclose the Appellant from challenging other components or the system as a whole under the broader mandate of *ISEEO I*.

**D. The Fee Reimbursement Claim**

Regarding the claim for fee reimbursement, the State argues it falls within the CBECA and that the CBECA does not authorize retroactive reimbursement. The Appellant argues that the claim against the legislature presents a non-justiciable political question. Because the primary issue on appeal is the systemic challenge to the funding mechanism and the viability of the declaratory judgment action, the Court’s reversal of the dismissal on those grounds renders the specific disposition of the fee claim against the State a matter for further proceedings on remand.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s order dismissing the State Defendants is reversed. The CBECA’s procedural prerequisites do not apply to the Appellant’s facial challenge to the State’s funding framework, and the Appellant has stated a viable claim under Article IX, Section 1 of the Idaho Constitution independent of the specific holding in *ISEEO V*. The State’s arguments misinterpret the scope of the CBECA and the controlling precedent, and they seek to impose an impossible procedural burden that would shield the State’s funding system from constitutional scrutiny. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.
