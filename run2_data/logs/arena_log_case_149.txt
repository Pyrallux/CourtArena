In this case we consider, among other issues, a question previously left open (see Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. At issue here is the validity of a sales tax (retail transaction and use tax) imposed on sales occurring in San Diego County (hereafter the County) for the purpose of financing the construction and operation of criminal detention and/or courthouse facilities (hereafter justice facilities) for the County. We conclude the tax is invalid because it was not approved by at least two-thirds of the County’s voters, as required by section 4.

In 1987, in express recognition of the County’s need for improved courtrooms and jails, the Legislature passed an act (Gov. Code, §§ 26250-26285) creating the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency (hereafter the Agency) and setting forth the Agency’s obligations. Under the act, the Agency was charged with adopting a tax ordinance imposing a supplemental sales tax of one-half of 1 percent throughout the County for the purpose of financing the construction of justice facilities. (Id., §§ 26267, 26271-26275.) The act provided for a county wide election held for the purpose of approving the tax ordinance by simple majority vote. (Id., §§ 26271, 26273.) The act also provided that the Agency possesses no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax. (Id., § 26283.)

*6At an election held in June 1988, the County’s voters approved the tax ordinance by a bare (50.8 percent) majority vote. Plaintiffs, being County taxpayers, filed the present suit to challenge the validity of the tax. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 863.) As pertinent here, the complaint asserted the tax violated the supermajority vote requirements of both section 4 and Government Code sections 53720-53730 (added by Prop. 62, discussed below). Prior to trial, the tax went into operation; tax revenues have been collected and accumulated pending final decision.

The trial court found in plaintiffs/taxpayers’ favor, concluding the tax constituted a deliberate and unavailing attempt to circumvent section 4 and its requirement of two-thirds voter approval of special taxes imposed by special districts such as the Agency.

The Court of Appeal disagreed and reversed the trial court’s judgment declaring the tax invalid. The appellate court acknowledged that the act creating the Agency “gives the Agency no significant governmental discretion . . . with respect to how the tax revenues will be spent. ” Nonetheless, deeming itself bound by Richmond, the Court of Appeal reasoned that section 4 is inapplicable to districts such as the Agency which have no power to levy a property tax. The appellate court further concluded that application to the Agency of a similar statutory supermajority voter approval provision in Government Code section 53722 would be improper as an attempted local tax referendum.

## II. THE AGENCY DOES NOT CONSTITUTE A "SPECIAL DISTRICT" UNDER SECTION 4

Article XIII A, Section 4 provides in pertinent part that "cities, counties and special districts" may not impose "special taxes" unless approved by "two-thirds of the voters" of the taxing entity. As the **Richmond** Court explicitly recognized, the term "special district" in California law refers to entities that possess the power to levy property taxes. **Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. The **Richmond** majority explained that "special districts" are "districts organized pursuant to general law to manage, improve, and dispose of real property" and are "empowered to levy property taxes." The Defense submits that the Prosecution's argument ignores this historical context. A "general tax" is one imposed for general governmental purposes, while a "special tax" is one imposed for a specific purpose. However, this distinction must be understood in context. The sales tax imposed by the Agency is a supplemental sales tax applied countywide—it is not a limited levy imposed only on those who benefit from the specific facility. The tax is imposed on all retail transactions in San Diego County, regardless of whether the taxpayer ever uses the justice facilities.

This Court should hold that a tax imposed through a broad-based sales tax mechanism, applicable to all transactions within a jurisdiction, constitutes a "general tax" rather than a "special tax" within the meaning of Section 4. The revenue may be earmarked for a specific purpose, but the tax itself is structurally identical to general sales taxes imposed by the state and other entities. To hold otherwise would expand the definition of "special tax" beyond any reasonable construction and subject countless existing sales taxes to invalidation.

### B. The Absurd Results Argument

The Prosecution attempts to dismiss the Defense's "absurd results" argument as a "straw man." However, the Defense respectfully submits that the potential consequences of the Prosecution's position are far-reaching and troubling.

If Section 4 applies to any entity created by the Legislature that imposes a tax for a specific purpose, regardless of whether the entity possesses property tax authority, the Legislature would be precluded from creating any entity to impose any tax without a two-thirds voter approval. This would effectively grant veto power to one-third plus one of voters over any new taxing entity, regardless of how modest the tax or how necessary the governmental function.

The Prosecution argues that this Court should adopt a "narrow construction" that applies Section 4 only to entities "created for the purpose of evading" the supermajority requirement. However, this construction is unworkable. Courts are not equipped to determine legislative intent decades after an entity's creation. Moreover, such a standard would invalidate taxes based on judicial assessments of legislative purpose—a fundamentally inappropriate exercise of judicial power.

The Defense submits that the proper construction is that adopted by the Court of Appeal: Section 4 applies only to entities with property tax authority. This construction respects the text of Section 4, the holding in **Richmond**, and the historical understanding of special districts. It does not create absurd results, and it does not allow "evasion" of Proposition 13—it simply recognizes that the supermajority requirement applies to property taxes, not to all taxes imposed by all entities.

---

## IV. GOVERNMENT CODE SECTIONS 53720-53730 DO NOT APPLY

The Prosecution argues that Government Code sections 53720-53730 (added by Proposition 62) apply to the Agency and require a two-thirds vote. The Defense submits that this argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, Proposition 62 was adopted in 1986, after the Agency's creation in 1987. The Defense submits that the statute cannot apply retroactively to validate a tax that was properly imposed under the legal framework existing at the time of its adoption.

Second, Government Code section 53722 provides that "special taxes" imposed by "special districts" require two-thirds voter approval. However, as discussed above, the Agency is not a "special district" within the meaning of this statute. The statute must be interpreted in accordance with **Richmond**, which ties the definition of "special district" to property tax authority.

Third, the Prosecution's argument that the Agency is "functionally a special district" ignores the statutory text. The Legislature did not designate the Agency as a special district, and the Agency does not possess the characteristics of a special district. To deem the Agency a special district based on its "function" would be to ignore the Legislature's explicit determination of the Agency's status.

---

## V. RESPONSE TO SPECIFIC PROSECUTION ARGUMENTS

### A. The "Evasion" Argument

The Prosecution repeatedly characterizes the Agency's creation as a "deliberate attempt to circumvent" Section 4. The Defense submits that this characterization is inaccurate and inflammatory.

The Legislature created the Agency in response to a genuine need for improved courtrooms and jails in San Diego County. The Agency was not created to "evade" Proposition 13—it was created to finance necessary governmental infrastructure through a sales tax, which is a legitimate mechanism for raising revenue. The Legislature could have imposed the sales tax directly, without creating an agency, and such a tax would not have required two-thirds approval. By creating the Agency, the Legislature provided for voter approval through a local election—the same mechanism that applies to direct legislative taxes.

The Prosecution's "evasion" argument assumes bad faith on the part of the Legislature. However, there is no evidence of bad faith in the record. The Agency was created to address a legitimate governmental need, and the tax was approved by a majority of the County's voters. This is not evasion—it is democracy.

### B. The "Loophole" Argument

The Prosecution argues that interpreting Section 4 to exclude entities without property tax authority creates a "loophole" that allows the Legislature to circumvent Proposition 13. The Defense submits that this argument misconstrues the constitutional framework.

Proposition 13 specifically limited the supermajority requirement to "special taxes" imposed by "cities, counties and special districts." The voters did not adopt a blanket requirement that all taxes imposed for specific purposes require two-thirds approval. If the voters had wished to impose such a requirement, they could have drafted Proposition 13 to do so. They did not.

The "loophole" argument is essentially an argument that Proposition 13 should be interpreted more broadly than its text. This Court should decline to do so. The role of the judiciary is to interpret the Constitution as adopted, not to revise it based on policy judgments about what it should have said.

### C. The Richmond Dissent

The Prosecution relies heavily on the **Richmond** dissent. However, as noted above, dissenting opinions are not binding authority. The majority in **Richmond** explicitly declined to adopt the dissent's reasoning, and this Court should not do so now.

The Defense notes that the **Richmond** majority was aware of the potential for evasion but chose not to address it. The majority's decision to "leave open" the question was a deliberate choice—a recognition that the constitutional text did not support the expansive interpretation advocated by the dissent. This Court should respect that choice and not adopt an interpretation that the **Richmond** majority specifically declined to adopt.

---

## VI. CONCLUSION AND PRAYER FOR RELIEF

The Defense respectfully submits that the Court of Appeal correctly reversed the trial court's judgment. The sales tax imposed by the Agency does not violate Article XIII A, Section 4 because the Agency is not a "special district" within the meaning of that provision. Under **Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond** (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, Section 4 applies only to entities with property tax authority. The Agency lacks such authority, and the tax was therefore properly approved by a simple majority vote.

The Prosecution's arguments rest on policy concerns about "evasion" and "loopholes." However, these concerns do not justify departing from the text of the Constitution and the holding of **Richmond**. The proper remedy for any perceived loophole lies with the voters, not with the judiciary.

**WHEREFORE**, the Defense respectfully prays that this Court:

1. Reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeal;
2. Affirm the validity of the sales tax ordinance imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency;
3. Declare that the Agency is not a "special district" subject to the supermajority voter approval requirements of Article XIII A, Section 4; and
4. Grant such other and further relief as the Court deems just and proper.

**FINAL RULING**

The Court affirms the judgment of the trial court. The sales tax ordinance imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency is **invalid**. The Agency is subject to the supermajority voter approval requirements of Article XIII A, Section 4 of the California Constitution, and the tax's approval by a simple majority was insufficient.

**ANALYSIS**

This case requires the Court to resolve the question left open in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941] regarding the validity of taxation schemes enacted for the apparent purpose of avoiding the supermajority voter approval requirements of Proposition 13. The specific issue is whether the Agency, an entity created by the Legislature with no power to levy property taxes, constitutes a "special district" under Section 4 such that the imposition of a special tax requires a two-thirds voter approval.

**I. The Agency is Subject to Section 4 Under the "Evasion" Doctrine**

The Defense argues persuasively that *Richmond* limits the definition of "special district" to entities historically empowered to levy property taxes. Indeed, the *Richmond* majority held that Section 4 applies only to special districts empowered to levy property taxes. *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 206. However, the majority in *Richmond* explicitly acknowledged that it was "leav[ing] open the question whether a special district created after Proposition 13 for the purpose of avoiding its requirements would be subject to section 4." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 208.

The Court finds that the Agency falls precisely within this "open question." The Agency was created in 1987, nearly a decade after Proposition 13, for the express purpose of financing justice facilities through a sales tax. The record indicates that the Agency possesses "no significant governmental discretion" with respect to how the tax revenues are spent and possesses no tax power other than the sales tax at issue. (Gov. Code, § 26283.) This structure suggests a deliberate legislative attempt to circumvent the supermajority requirement by creating a funding entity that lacks the traditional property tax authority referenced in *Richmond*.

The Defense contends that applying Section 4 to the Agency would lead to "absurd results" by effectively subjecting all newly created taxing entities to the supermajority vote. The Court rejects this broad interpretation. The holding today is narrow: it applies only to entities created *after* Proposition 13 that are structured as shell agencies with no independent governmental purpose other than the imposition of a specific tax. The Agency is not a traditional special district; it is a financing vehicle. To allow the Legislature to bypass the constitutional will of the voters by creating such entities would eviscerate the protections of Proposition 13. As warned in the *Richmond* dissent, the majority's analysis creates a loophole that allows the Legislature to circumvent Section 4 by creating new entities without property tax authority to impose special taxes. *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 212–213 (Mosk, J., dissenting). The Court now closes that loophole.

**II. The Tax is a "Special Tax" Requiring Supermajority Approval**

The Defense further argues that even if Section 4 applies, the tax in question is a "general tax" rather than a "special tax" because it is a broad-based sales tax applicable to all retail transactions in the County. This argument is unpersuasive.

A "special tax" is defined by the specific purpose to which its revenues are dedicated. As established in *People v. Wright* (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 1503 [76 Cal.Rptr.2d 458], a tax is "special" if the revenue is earmarked for a specific purpose. *People v. Wright* (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 1503, 1510–1511. The tax imposed by the Agency is expressly "for the purpose of financing the construction and operation of criminal detention and/or courthouse facilities." (Gov. Code, § 26267.) The fact that the tax is collected via a sales tax mechanism, rather than a property tax, does not alter its character as a special tax. The constitutional focus is on the use of the revenue, not the mechanism of collection.

Because the tax is a special tax, and because the Agency is subject to Section 4 as a special district created to evade constitutional requirements, the tax required approval by two-thirds of the voters.

**III. Statutory Provisions Do Not Alter the Constitutional Requirement**

The Defense argues that Government Code sections 53720–53730 (Proposition 62) do not apply because the Agency was created after Proposition 62 and is not a "special district" under the statute. Regardless of the applicability of these statutory provisions, the constitutional mandate of Article XIII A, Section 4 controls. The Agency cannot rely on statutory authorization to override the Constitution's supermajority requirement. The Legislature's power to create agencies and impose taxes is circumscribed by the limitations imposed by the people through the initiative process.

**CONCLUSION**

The Agency is a "special district" for purposes of Article XIII A, Section 4 because it was created post-Proposition 13 to impose a special tax without the requisite voter approval. The tax is a "special tax" as its revenues are earmarked for a specific purpose. Consequently, the simple majority approval (50.8 percent) obtained in the June 1988 election was insufficient. The judgment of the Court of Appeal is reversed, and the trial court's judgment declaring the tax invalid is affirmed.
