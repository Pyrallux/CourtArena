## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The District Court Properly Denied Secretary Rumsfeld's Motion to Dismiss the Bivens Claim

#### 1. The Nature of the Bivens Remedy

The Supreme Court established in Bivens that a citizen may bring a damages action against federal officers for violations of constitutional rights. 403 U.S. at 389. While the Court has cautioned against unrestrained expansion of Bivens, it has never categorically foreclosed such actions in all military or national security contexts. To the contrary, the Court has recognized that Bivens serves as a vital remedy where Congress has not provided an alternative and constitutional violations have occurred. See Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14, 18-20 (1980) (recognizing Bivens action against prison officials for Eighth Amendment violations).

#### 2. This Case Does Not Present a "New Context" Barring Bivens

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that this case presents a "new context" for Bivens purposes, invoking Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017). However, this argument misapprehends the nature of the "new context" inquiry. The Supreme Court in Ziglar emphasized that the inquiry focuses on whether the claim is similar to claims previously recognized in Bivens and its progeny. Id. at 1859-60.

Doe's substantive due process claim is not a novel creation. The Supreme Court has long recognized substantive due process claims under the Fifth Amendment for government conduct that "shocks the conscience." See Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952) (establishing the "shock the conscience" standard); see also County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 846 (1998) (confirming the applicability of substantive due process scrutiny to executive action).

The context here—detention and interrogation of an individual by military and law enforcement personnel—is not unprecedented in Bivens jurisprudence. Indeed, the Supreme Court has recognized Bivens claims arising from detention contexts in cases such as Bell v. Wolfish, 441 U.S. 520 (1979), and in the context of prisoner conditions. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678 (1978). While those cases involved prisoners rather than warzone detainees, the constitutional principles at stake are analogous.

#### 3. Special Factors Do Not Counsel Against a Bivens Remedy Here

Even assuming arguendo that this case presents a "new context," the special factors analysis does not foreclose Doe's claim. Secretary Rumsfeld relies heavily on the "military context" and "national security" concerns to argue that courts should not intervene. However, the Supreme Court's precedent establishes that these concerns are not categorical bars.

In Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), the Supreme Court acknowledged the "special nature of military discipline" and the need to avoid interfering with military command decisions. Id. at 304. However, the Court specifically held that this concern does not immunize military officers from all constitutional liability. Rather, the Court established a heightened pleading standard for such claims, not a categorical bar. Id. at 303-04 ("[W]e have recognized [that] the military is, by necessity, a specialized society separate from civilian society... [and] the different treatment of military personnel under the Constitution has been recognized since the earliest days of this Court.").

The Chappell Court explicitly contemplated that Bivens actions might proceed against military officers in appropriate cases, provided the plaintiff meets the heightened pleading standard. 462 U.S. at 304-05. Doe has met that standard here. His complaint alleges specific facts demonstrating that Secretary Rumsfeld personally developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe's harm—allegations sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss.

Moreover, the national security concerns raised by Secretary Rumsfeld, while legitimate, do not automatically foreclose judicial review. As the Supreme Court recognized in Hamdi v. Rumsfeld, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), even in wartime, constitutional protections apply, and courts retain the authority to review the legality of executive detention. Id. at 535-36 ("There is no bar to this Court's acting as a check on the political branches... in a case like this."). The availability of a damages remedy under Bivens is consistent with this constitutional framework.

#### 4. Alternative Remedies Are Inadequate

The absence of alternative remedial mechanisms further supports the recognition of a Bivens remedy here. Doe has no administrative remedy for the constitutional violations alleged. The Detainee Treatment Act, 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd, provides no private right of action, as the district court correctly held. Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F Supp. 2d at 104-05. Without a Bivens remedy, Doe would be left without any federal cause of action for the violations of his constitutional rights—a result that would render those rights illusory.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the absence of alternative remedies is a relevant consideration in the Bivens analysis. See Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. at 18-20 (holding that the availability of a Bivens remedy was appropriate where the Federal Tort Claims Act provided inadequate替代). While the existence of alternative remedies alone does not bar Bivens, their absence supports recognition here.

#### 5. The Nature of the Alleged Violations Supports a Bivens Remedy

The gravity of the constitutional violations alleged further supports the recognition of a Bivens remedy. Doe alleges that he was subjected to prolonged solitary confinement, physical abuse, denial of access to counsel, and detention for nine months without formal charges—all in violation of his Fifth Amendment right to substantive due process. These allegations describe conduct that, if proven, would constitute a fundamental violation of constitutional norms.

The Supreme Court has indicated that the severity of the alleged constitutional violation is relevant to the Bivens inquiry. In Ziglar, the Court emphasized that its concern was with "diffuse" policy decisions rather than direct, individual violations. 137 S. Ct. at 1860. Here, Doe alleges direct violations of his constitutional rights by named individuals pursuant to specific policies—precisely the type of claim Bivens was designed to address.

### B. Secretary Rumsfeld Is Not Entitled to Qualified Immunity

#### 1. The Standard for Qualified Immunity

Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages "insofar as their conduct does not violate clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). To overcome qualified immunity, a plaintiff must allege facts showing (1) that the official violated a constitutional right, and (2) that the constitutional right was clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct. Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194, 201-02 (2001).

#### 2. Doe Has Alleged a Violation of a Clearly Established Constitutional Right

The right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience" was clearly established at the time of Doe's detention in 2005. The Supreme Court has long held that substantive due process protects individuals from executive action that "shocks the conscience." Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952); see also County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 846 (1998) (discussing the "shock the conscience" standard in the context of police pursuit).

The treatment Doe alleges—prolonged solitary confinement, physical abuse, denial of access to counsel, and detention without formal charges for nine months—undoubtedly meets the "shock the conscience" standard. As the district court correctly held, Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F Supp. 2d at 115, a reasonable official in 2005 would have known that such treatment violates constitutional rights.

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that the specific context of military detention in a warzone somehow negates the applicability of these well-established constitutional principles. However, the Supreme Court has rejected the notion that military context provides blanket immunity for constitutional violations. See Hamdi v. Rumsfeld, 542 U.S. at 535 ("[E]ven in wartime, [the Court has] not yet been presented with a case where the President has asserted the power to detain indefinitely a United States citizen captured on an American battlefield."). The Constitution applies even in warzone contexts; only the specific procedures required may differ.

#### 3. Doe Has Alleged Sufficient Facts to Support His Claims

At the motion to dismiss stage, Doe need only allege sufficient facts to state a claim for relief that is plausible on its face. Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009). Doe has done more. His complaint alleges that Secretary Rumsfeld personally developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused his harm. Specifically, Doe alleges that Rumsfeld "developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm." Complaint ¶ 195. These allegations, taken as true, are sufficient to overcome qualified immunity at this stage.

The district court correctly held that Doe "sufficiently pled facts to support a claim that Secretary Rumsfeld violated that right." Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F Supp. 2d at 115. This Court should affirm that holding.

### C. The District Court's Analysis Was Correct

The district court applied the correct legal standards in denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss. The court correctly recognized that while Bivens must be applied with caution in military contexts, it is not categorically barred. The court properly applied the heightened pleading standard from Chappell v. Wallace and found that Doe had met that standard. The court correctly held that Doe had alleged a violation of a clearly established constitutional right and that the factual allegations were sufficient to overcome qualified immunity at the motion to dismiss stage.

Secretary Rumsfeld asks this Court to go further than any previous court in restricting the availability of Bivens remedies. This Court should decline that invitation. The Supreme Court has consistently preserved the availability of constitutional remedies for individuals whose rights have been violated by government officials. While such remedies must be applied with appropriate caution, they remain essential to the enforcement of constitutional guarantees.

**FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court:** Interlocutory appeal from the United States District Court for the District of Columbia challenging the denial of a motion to dismiss.

**Decision:** The order of the district court denying Secretary Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss is **REVERSED**.

**OPINION**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the Appellant, Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld, and the Appellee, John Doe, we adhere to our conclusion that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action in this case. The arguments presented to this Court, including those submitted by the Appellee, confirm that the sensitive context of military detention in a foreign warzone presents "special factors" that foreclose judicial creation of a damages remedy. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity.

**I. Standard of Review**

We review de novo the district court’s denial of a motion to dismiss based on qualified immunity and the existence of a *Bivens* cause of action. See *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008). In doing so, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the non-moving party. See *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009).

**II. The Bivens Remedy is Barred by Special Factors**

The Supreme Court has instructed that implying a *Bivens* remedy is a "disfavored" judicial activity, particularly in new contexts. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). When a plaintiff seeks to expand *Bivens* beyond the three specific contexts recognized by the Court, courts must rigorously analyze whether "special factors counsel hesitation." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. We find that such factors are present here and are dispositive.

**A. The Military Context and National Security Counsel Hesitation**

The Appellee's submission acknowledges that the "specialized society" doctrine of *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296 (1983), rests on the unique structure of the military and the necessity of judicial deference to military judgments. *Chappell*, 462 U.S. at 304. The policies challenged here—detention, interrogation, and intelligence gathering in an active warzone—are quintessential military functions. As argued by both parties, Doe's status as a civilian contractor does not transform these core military operations into civilian matters subject to judicial micromanagement via damages actions against the Secretary of Defense.

Furthermore, the national security implications of this case are undeniable. In *Hernandez v. Mesa*, 140 S. Ct. 735 (2020), the Supreme Court reaffirmed that "special factors" such as "foreign affairs or national security" categorically counsel against extending *Bivens*. *Hernandez*, 140 S. Ct. at 744. The detention of individuals in Iraq during active hostilities involves sensitive strategic decisions committed to the discretion of the Executive Branch. Subjecting these decisions to judicial scrutiny for damages risks interfering with the unique structure of the military and the conduct of foreign policy. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1860.

**B. The Attempt to Distinguish Precedent Fails**

The arguments presented correctly establish that *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), does not authorize a *Bivens* damages action against the Secretary of Defense for the conditions of confinement or the implementation of detention policies. While *Hamdi* recognized a limited right to habeas corpus for enemy combatants, the limited judicial review authorized therein is distinct from the creation of a new monetary remedy that intrudes on military discretion. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1864.

Similarly, the reliance on *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), is misplaced. *Carlson* involved the failure to provide medical care in a federal prison, a context far removed from the battlefield detention and intelligence gathering at issue here. *Ziglar* specifically distinguished *Carlson* when refusing to extend *Bivens* to the detention of illegal immigrants following the 9/11 attacks, noting that "prison conditions cases" are "distinguishable" from "national security operations." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1864-65.

Finally, the absence of an alternative remedy under the Detainee Treatment Act does not justify implying a *Bivens* action. The Supreme Court has explicitly rejected this argument, holding in *Hernandez* that "the fact that there is no alternative remedy does not create a Bivens remedy" when special factors are present. *Hernandez*, 140 S. Ct. at 744.

**III. Qualified Immunity Provides an Alternative Basis for Reversal**

Even if a *Bivens* remedy were theoretically available in this context—a proposition we reject—Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects government officials from civil damages liability unless the official violated a "clearly established" statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known. *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223, 231 (2009).

The Appellee alleges that his treatment "shocked the conscience," violating substantive due process. While the general prohibition against conduct that shocks the conscience is established, the specific contours of this right in the context of military detention of a contractor in a foreign warzone were far from clear in 2005. The Appellee has not pointed to controlling authority that would have put Secretary Rumsfeld on notice that his authorization of detention policies for enemy combatants or suspected threats in Iraq would subject him to personal liability. *Pearson*, 555 U.S. at 243. Given the sensitive national security context and the lack of clearly established law applicable to these specific facts, qualified immunity bars the claim. See *Anderson v. Creighton*, 483 U.S. 635, 640 (1987).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* cause of action. The sensitive nature of military detention in a foreign warzone constitutes a "special factor" that counsels hesitation and forecloses the extension of the *Bivens* remedy. Furthermore, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Accordingly, we reverse the order of the district court denying Secretary Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss.
