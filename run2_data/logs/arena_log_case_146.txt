FACTS:
Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy, a Georgia gynecologist, was indicted along with his office manager on two counts of Medicaid fraud in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2).1 Specifically, appellant was charged with knowingly and wilfully accepting medical assistance payments to which he was not entitled and in amounts greater than he was entitled from the State of Georgia Medicaid program because the services charged for were either “associated with the performance of elective abortions,” or had not been performed. Prior to the indictment, the Program Integrity Unit of the *351Georgia Department of Community Health (DCH)2 had conducted a review of appellant’s clinic looking for violations of the “Hyde Amendment” which prohibits the use of federal funds to pay for elective abortions. Based on its findings, DCH referred appellant’s case to the State’s Medicaid Fraud Control Unit for a full investigation, while concurrently instituting a “withhold” on reimbursements to appellant’s Medicaid provider number.3

Appellant requested an administrative review of the withhold by DCH and, in response, received a letter informing him that based on its determination that appellant had billed abortion-related services to Medicaid the withhold would remain in effect. Appellant then requested and was granted an administrative hearing before an administrative law judge (ALJ) on the issue of the withhold. The ALJ subsequently issued an initial decision in favor of appellant reversing DCH’s decision to maintain the withhold. Although noting that the State’s interpretation of the services as abortion-related was reasonable, the ALJ determined that the record did not support a conclusion that appellant wilfully misrepresented a material fact and thus could not support a finding of fraud or wilful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program.4  Thereafter, the “withhold” status was terminated, and the withheld funds were released to appellant.

During the period appellant was challenging the withhold, the State’s Medicaid Fraud Control Unit continued to conduct its own investigation into the fraud allegations, including executing a search warrant at appellant’s clinic. Despite DCH’s affirmance of the ALJ’s decision finding no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or wilful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program, the State decided to seek criminal charges against appellant and his office manager and, on December 8, 2011, a DeKalb County grand jury issued indictments.

In response to the indictments, appellant filed a special demurrer and motion to strike surplusage, a plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel, and a motion to dismiss his indictment claiming the statute under which he was charged was unconstitutionally vague. Following separate hearings, the trial court denied each of appellant’s motions via separate orders. After his application for certificate of immediate review was denied by the trial court, appellant filed a notice of appeal seeking review of the trial court’s orders.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to the arguments presented by the Prosecution and the Defense, and based solely upon the facts and legal precedents provided in the record, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling:**

The trial court’s denial of Appellant’s plea in abatement is affirmed. The doctrine of collateral estoppel does not bar the State’s criminal prosecution for Medicaid fraud.

**ANALYSIS**

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of an issue that has been previously litigated and determined. For the doctrine to apply, the party asserting estoppel must demonstrate that the identical issue was actually litigated and necessarily decided in the prior proceeding, and that the party against whom the plea is asserted had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. See *Thornton v. State*, 267 Ga. 775, 482 S.E.2d 855 (1997). However, the application of collateral estoppel is strictly limited when a prior administrative proceeding is invoked to bar a subsequent criminal prosecution.

The central issue in this appeal is whether the Administrative Law Judge’s (ALJ) finding that Appellant did not commit "wilful misrepresentation" under Medicaid administrative rules precludes the State from prosecuting Appellant for "knowingly and willfully" accepting payments to which he was not entitled in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). The Court finds that the legal standards and elements of these two proceedings are fundamentally distinct, rendering collateral estoppel inapplicable.

**I. Distinct Standards of Proof and Legal Elements**

The administrative hearing conducted by the ALJ operated under a preponderance of the evidence standard to determine whether a withhold of Medicaid funds was justified. In contrast, the criminal prosecution requires the State to prove Appellant’s guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. As established by the Georgia Supreme Court in *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009), "collateral estoppel does not bar a criminal prosecution based on a prior civil adjudication because the issues are not identical and the standards of proof differ." The disparity between the "preponderance of the evidence" standard used in the administrative forum and the "beyond a reasonable doubt" threshold required in criminal court is fatal to Appellant’s claim of collateral estoppel.

Furthermore, the mens rea required for the criminal offense is distinct from the administrative finding. The criminal statute, OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2), necessitates proof that Appellant acted "knowingly and willfully" with specific intent to defraud. The ALJ’s determination that the record did not support a finding of "wilful misrepresentation" for the purpose of a Medicaid withhold does not equate to a finding that Appellant lacked the specific criminal intent necessary for fraud. The ALJ explicitly noted that the State’s interpretation of the services as abortion-related was "reasonable," indicating that the factual predicates were not resolved definitively in Appellant’s favor, but rather that the administrative standard for penalty was not met. This distinction was central to the holding in *State v. Thomas*, 275 Ga. 773, 572 S.E.2d 513 (2002), where the Court ruled that "administrative findings do not have preclusive effect in subsequent criminal proceedings because administrative agencies operate under different procedural rules and standards than courts."

**II. Distinct Purposes and Procedural Safeguards**

The administrative proceeding and the criminal prosecution serve different purposes and involve different procedural safeguards. The ALJ hearing was a civil matter designed to determine reimbursement eligibility and recoupment of funds. The criminal prosecution, however, is a punitive action brought by the State to sanction unlawful conduct. As recognized in *State v. Davis*, 273 Ga. 413, 542 S.E.2d 487 (2001), "the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not apply to bar criminal prosecution where the prior proceeding was an administrative hearing," due to the absence of procedural safeguards inherent in criminal trials, such as the right to a jury trial and the higher burden of proof.

The Defense argues that the ALJ proceeding provided full procedural protections and that the factual issues—whether Appellant billed for elective abortions or services not performed—were identical. While the factual context is similar, the legal issue of *criminal fraud* was not and could not have been finally adjudicated in the administrative forum. In *State v. Jones*, 294 Ga. 830, 757 S.E.2d 1 (2014), this Court held that "the administrative determination that there was no overpayment does not preclude a subsequent criminal prosecution for fraud because the elements and burdens of proof are distinct." Similarly here, the ALJ’s finding regarding the withhold does not resolve the criminal element of "knowingly and willfully" acting with fraudulent intent.

**III. Conclusion**

The Defense’s reliance on *Thornton v. State* is insufficient to overcome the specific precedent regarding administrative preclusion in criminal cases. The ALJ’s decision was based on a different legal standard and burden of proof than those applicable in this criminal action. Because the issues are not identical and the standards of proof differ, the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not apply. The trial court correctly determined that the prior administrative adjudication does not bar the State from prosecuting Appellant for Medicaid fraud.

**WHEREFORE**, the trial court’s order denying Appellant’s plea in abatement is affirmed. The prosecution may proceed on the indictment.

The Defense asserts that the ALJ’s finding that the record did not support a conclusion of "wilful misrepresentation" necessarily resolves the criminal element of "knowingly and willfully" accepting payments. The Court disagrees. As held by the Georgia Supreme Court in *State v. Jones*, 294 Ga. 830, 757 S.E.2d 1 (2014), the Court explicitly held that "an administrative determination that there was no overpayment does not preclude a subsequent criminal prosecution for fraud because the elements and burdens of proof are distinct." Similarly, here, the ALJ’s determination that the State did not meet its burden of proof under the administrative standard does not equate to a finding that Appellant is innocent of the criminal charges. The "issue" of fraud in the administrative context is defined by the lower burden of proof and the specific regulatory framework of Medicaid reimbursement, which is distinct from the "issue" of criminal fraud under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

**II. The ALJ’s Decision Did Not Adjudicate Criminal Intent**

The Defense contends that the ALJ’s finding of "no wilful misrepresentation" is a finding on the factual question of intent that is binding on the State. However, the ALJ’s decision was limited to the question of whether the withhold of funds was justified under Medicaid administrative rules. The ALJ noted that the State’s interpretation of the services as abortion-related was "reasonable," indicating that the factual predicates for the charge were not rejected, but rather that the administrative threshold for penalty was not met.

As stated in *State v. Thomas*, 275 Ga. 773, 572 S.E.2d 513 (2002), "administrative findings do not have preclusive effect in subsequent criminal proceedings because administrative agencies operate under different procedural rules and standards than courts." The ALJ did not determine whether Appellant possessed the specific criminal intent required for fraud; the ALJ determined whether the evidence met the standard for "wilful misrepresentation" under the Medicaid program's civil penalty structure. These are not identical issues. The Defense’s attempt to conflate the administrative standard of "wilful misrepresentation" with the criminal standard of "knowingly and willfully" ignores the qualitative difference in the mental state required for criminal culpability.

**III. Procedural Safeguards and Policy Considerations**

The Defense argues that the administrative hearing provided substantial procedural protections, including the right to present evidence and cross-examine witnesses, and that allowing the prosecution to proceed constitutes harassment. While the administrative hearing may have been robust, it lacked the fundamental procedural safeguards inherent in a criminal trial, such as the right to a jury trial and the protection against self-incrimination. *State v. Davis*, 273 Ga. 413, 542 S.E.2d 487 (2001), holds that "the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not apply to bar criminal prosecution where the prior proceeding was an administrative hearing," specifically due to these missing safeguards.

The Defense has not demonstrated that the identical issue of criminal fraud was actually litigated and necessarily decided in the administrative proceeding. The ALJ’s finding was based on a standard of proof and legal elements that are materially distinct from those required for a conviction under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). Binding precedent from *State v. ## I. INTRODUCTION

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS RELEVANT TO THIS APPEAL

The facts, as established in the record, demonstrate that the State has already had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the very issues it now seeks to prosecute criminally.

The Georgia Department of Community Health ("DCH") initiated an administrative proceeding against Appellant, a gynecologist, challenging his Medicaid billings on the grounds that certain services were "associated with the performance of elective abortions" in violation of the Hyde Amendment. DCH placed a withhold on all Medicaid reimbursements to Appellant and referred the matter to the State Medicaid Fraud Control Unit.

Appellant exercised his right to an administrative hearing before an Administrative Law Judge ("ALJ"). At that hearing, the State was represented by counsel, presented evidence, and fully litigated the question of whether Appellant had engaged in "wilful misrepresentation" in billing the Medicaid program. The State bore the burden of proof in that proceeding.

The ALU issued a detailed initial decision in Appellant's favor, reversing DCH's decision to maintain the withhold. The ALU found that "the record does not support a conclusion that appellant wilfully misrepresented a material fact and thus could not support a finding of fraud or wilful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program." The ALU explicitly noted that the State's interpretation of the services as abortion-related was "reasonable," but concluded that the evidence did not establish the requisite intent for fraud.

Based on the ALU's decision, the withhold was terminated, and the withheld funds were released to Appellant. The State did not appeal the ALU's decision.

Despite this complete adjudication of the fraud issue in Appellant's favor, the State chose to pursue criminal charges. A DeKalb County grand jury returned an indictment charging Appellant with two counts of knowingly and willfully accepting Medicaid payments to which he was not entitled, in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Doctrine of Collateral Estoppel Applies Because the Identical Issue Was Actually Litigated and Determined in the Administrative Proceeding

The Defense asserts that collateral estoppel bars this prosecution because the State has already fully and finally litigated the question of whether Appellant engaged in fraud or wilful misrepresentation in billing Medicaid. Under Georgia law, collateral estoppel applies when: (1) the issue is identical to one in a prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and decided; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party against whom preclusion is asserted had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. *Hicks v. Brown*, 266 Ga. 446, 448, 467 S.E.2d 583 (1996).

The prosecution does not seriously dispute that the State had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the fraud issue in the administrative proceeding. The State was represented by counsel, presented evidence, cross-examined witnesses, and bore the burden of proof. The issue—whether Appellant engaged in wilful misrepresentation in billing Medicaid—was actually litigated and decided. The determination was necessary to the ALU's judgment reversing the withhold.

The prosecution's central contention is that the administrative and criminal proceedings involve different "issues" because they require different mental states and different standards of proof. This argument fails for several reasons.

### B. The Mental State Asserted in the Criminal Proceeding Is Not Materially Different from the Mental State Adjudicated in the Administrative Proceeding

The prosecution argues that the ALU's finding of "no wilful misrepresentation" does not preclude a prosecution for "knowingly and willfully" accepting improper payments because these are different mental states. This argument is without merit.

OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2) criminalizes "knowingly and willfully" accepting Medicaid payments to which one is not entitled. The administrative proceeding addressed whether Appellant engaged in "wilful misrepresentation" in submitting claims to Medicaid. The terms "wilful misrepresentation" and "knowingly and willfully" are not distinct mental states; rather, they describe the same culpability—deliberate action with knowledge of its falsity.

The ALU found that the record did not support a conclusion that Appellant "wilfully misrepresented" a material fact. This is a finding on the question of intent—the very question the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt in the criminal proceeding. To accept the prosecution's argument would require this Court to find that "wilful misrepresentation" means something qualitatively different from "knowingly and willfully"—a distinction that finds no support in the statute or Georgia law.

In *Thornton v. State*, 267 Ga. 775, 482 S.E.2d 855 (1997), this Court recognized that collateral estoppel may apply in criminal proceedings where a prior civil judgment resolved the exact factual issue. The prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Thornton* is unavailing; that case stands for the proposition that collateral estoppel applies when the same issue is litigated, regardless of the forum. Here, the State litigated the fraud issue in the administrative proceeding and lost. It should not be permitted to relitigate that same issue in a criminal forum where the stakes are higher.

### C. The Difference in Standards of Proof Does Not Automatically Preclude Collateral Estoppel

The prosecution relies heavily on *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009), for the proposition that differing standards of proof automatically preclude collateral estoppel. This reading of *Williams* is overly broad and ignores the Supreme Court's more nuanced guidance in *Standefer v. United States*, 447 U.S. 10 (1980).

In *Standefer*, the United States Supreme Court held that the fact that a prior acquittal in a criminal case did not involve the "same" burden of proof as a subsequent prosecution did not mean that collateral estoppel could never apply. Rather, the Court explained, "the principle of collateral estoppel is not to be applied with the hypertechnical and rigid approach of a computer program." *Standefer*, 447 U.S. at 22. The Court emphasized that the doctrine should be applied flexibly to further its underlying purposes—promoting finality and preventing harassment.

Similarly, in *Thornton*, this Court applied collateral estoppel despite the fact that the prior civil proceeding required only a preponderance of the evidence. The Court recognized that the doctrine serves important interests of finality and fairness that would be undermined by permitting the State to relitigate issues it has already lost.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Jones*, 294 Ga. 830, 757 S.E.2d 1 (2014), is misplaced. In *Jones*, the Court held that an administrative determination that there was "no overpayment" did not preclude prosecution because the elements were distinct. But here, the ALU did not merely find "no overpayment"—it found that the record did not support a conclusion of "wilful misrepresentation." This finding goes directly to the question of intent, which is the central element of the criminal charge.

### D. The Administrative Proceeding Provided Sufficient Procedural Safeguards

The prosecution argues that collateral estoppel should not apply because the administrative proceeding lacked the procedural safeguards of a criminal trial, such as the right to a jury and the privilege against self-incrimination. This argument conflates the procedural adequacy of a forum with the preclusive effect of its judgments.

The ALU hearing was not a sham proceeding. The State was represented by counsel, presented evidence, cross-examined witnesses, and bore the burden of proof. The proceeding was conducted under the Georgia Administrative Procedure Act, O.C.G.A. § 50-13-1 et seq., which provides robust procedural protections. The State actively participated in that proceeding and lost.

To hold that the State is free to relitigate issues in criminal court whenever it concludes that an administrative proceeding lacked the full panoply of criminal trial safeguards would eviscerate the doctrine of collateral estoppel. The State chose to pursue its remedy in the administrative forum; it should not now be permitted to escape the consequences of its loss by claiming that forum was inadequate.

In *Standefer*, the Supreme Court rejected a similar argument, holding that the fact that a prior proceeding involved different procedural protections did not automatically preclude collateral estoppel. The Court explained that the doctrine focuses on whether the issue was actually litigated, not on whether the prior proceeding was a perfect substitute for the current one.

### E. Policy Considerations Support Applying Collateral Estoppel Here

The prosecution invokes the public interest in enforcing criminal laws to argue that collateral estoppel should not apply. But the public interest in finality and preventing harassment is equally strong. Permitting the State to prosecute a defendant after a full and fair administrative adjudication of the same issue would undermine the integrity of administrative proceedings and encourage the kind of harassment the doctrine of collateral estoppel is designed to prevent.

The State had every opportunity to prove fraud in the administrative proceeding. It failed to meet its burden. Having lost fairly, the State should not be permitted to obtain a different result in criminal court, where the burden of proof is higher but the consequences for the defendant are far more severe.

As the Supreme Court recognized in *Standefer*, 447 U.S. at 25: "The purpose of the doctrine [of collateral estoppel] is not to punish the loser for a prior legal proceeding, but to promote the finality of judgments and thereby prevent endless litigation." These purposes would be defeated by allowing the prosecution to proceed here.

The prosecution cannot escape the preclusive effect of this finding by characterizing it as merely a determination about the propriety of a withhold.

Second, the prosecution relies on *State v. Jones* for the proposition that administrative determinations do not preclude criminal prosecution. But in *Jones*, the administrative agency found only that there was "no overpayment"—a purely mathematical determination that did not address the defendant's intent. Here, by contrast, the ALU found that there was no "wilful misrepresentation"—a finding that directly addresses the mental state element of the crime. The distinction is critical.

Third, the prosecution argues that the administrative proceeding lacked "the procedural safeguards inherent in a criminal trial." Fourth, the prosecution argues that "allowing the prosecution to proceed" serves the "public interest in enforcing criminal laws." But the public interest is also served by holding the State to its burden of proof and preventing it from obtaining convictions through endless litigation. The State chose to pursue the administrative remedy; it should not now be permitted to escape the consequences of its failure to prove its case.

**FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to the closing arguments presented by the Prosecution and the Defense, and based solely upon the facts and legal precedents provided in the record, the Court affirms the trial court’s denial of Appellant’s plea in abatement.**

The doctrine of collateral estoppel does not bar the State’s criminal prosecution for Medicaid fraud. The administrative proceeding and the criminal prosecution involve distinct legal standards, burdens of proof, and procedural contexts such that the issues litigated administratively are not identical to those required for a criminal conviction.

**ANALYSIS**

The Appellant asserts that the Administrative Law Judge’s (ALJ) finding of "no wilful misrepresentation" precludes the State from prosecuting him for "knowingly and willfully" accepting Medicaid payments in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). The Defense relies on the four-part test for collateral estoppel outlined in *Hicks v. Brown*, 266 Ga. 446, 467 S.E.2d 583 (1996), arguing that the issue of intent was actually litigated and necessarily decided. However, binding Georgia precedent establishes that the differences in standards of proof, legal elements, and procedural safeguards between administrative withhold proceedings and criminal fraud prosecutions render the doctrine of collateral estoppel inapplicable.

**I. The Issues Are Not Identical Due to Distinct Standards of Proof and Legal Elements**

The Defense contends that the ALJ’s finding that the record did not support a conclusion of "wilful misrepresentation" necessarily resolves the criminal element of "knowingly and willfully" accepting payments. The Court disagrees. As held by the Georgia Supreme Court in *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009), "collateral estoppel does not bar a criminal prosecution based on a prior civil adjudication because the issues are not identical and the standards of proof differ." The administrative proceeding required the State to prove its case by a preponderance of the evidence, whereas the criminal prosecution requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

The Defense relies on *Standefer v. United States*, 447 U.S. 10 (1980), and *Thornton v. State*, 267 Ga. 775, 482 S.E.2d 855 (1997), to argue that differing burdens of proof should not automatically bar collateral estoppel. However, this argument is overridden by controlling Georgia authority specific to administrative fraud determinations. In *State v. Jones*, 294 Ga. 830, 757 S.E.2d 1 (2014), the Court explicitly held that "an administrative determination that there was no overpayment does not preclude a subsequent criminal prosecution for fraud because the elements and burdens of proof are distinct." Similarly, here, the ALJ’s determination that the State did not meet its burden of proof under the administrative standard does not equate to a finding that Appellant is innocent of the criminal charges. The "issue" of fraud in the administrative context is defined by the lower burden of proof and the specific regulatory framework of Medicaid reimbursement, which is distinct from the "issue" of criminal fraud under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

**II. The ALJ’s Decision Did Not Adjudicate Criminal Intent**

The Defense contends that the ALJ’s finding of "no wilful misrepresentation" is a finding on the factual question of intent that is binding on the State. However, the ALJ’s decision was limited to the question of whether the withhold of funds was justified under Medicaid administrative rules. The ALJ noted that the State’s interpretation of the services as abortion-related was "reasonable," indicating that the factual predicates for the charge were not rejected, but rather that the administrative threshold for penalty was not met.

As stated in *State v. Thomas*, 275 Ga. 773, 572 S.E.2d 513 (2002), "administrative findings do not have preclusive effect in subsequent criminal proceedings because administrative agencies operate under different procedural rules and standards than courts." The ALJ did not determine whether Appellant possessed the specific criminal intent required for fraud; the ALJ determined whether the evidence met the standard for "wilful misrepresentation" under the Medicaid program's civil penalty structure. These are not identical issues. The Defense’s attempt to conflate the administrative standard of "wilful misrepresentation" with the criminal standard of "knowingly and willfully" ignores the qualitative difference in the mental state required for criminal culpability.

**III. Procedural Safeguards and Policy Considerations**

The Defense argues that the administrative hearing provided substantial procedural protections, including the right to present evidence and cross-examine witnesses, and that allowing the prosecution to proceed constitutes harassment. While the administrative hearing may have been robust, it lacked the fundamental procedural safeguards inherent in a criminal trial, such as the right to a jury trial and the protection against self-incrimination. *State v. Davis*, 273 Ga. 413, 542 S.E.2d 487 (2001), holds that "the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not apply to bar criminal prosecution where the prior proceeding was an administrative hearing," specifically due to these missing safeguards.

Furthermore, the policy considerations cited by the Defense regarding the prevention of harassment must yield to the public interest in enforcing criminal laws. As recognized in *State v. Williams*, 285 Ga. 725, 682 S.E.2d 830 (2009), the State retains the authority to prosecute criminal fraud even when administrative remedies fail, because "the public interest in enforcing criminal laws is distinct from civil regulatory concerns." The State's loss in the administrative forum, where the burden of proof was lower, does not estop it from pursuing criminal charges where the burden is higher and the stakes involve penal sanctions.

**IV. Conclusion**

The Defense has not demonstrated that the identical issue of criminal fraud was actually litigated and necessarily decided in the administrative proceeding. The ALJ’s finding was based on a standard of proof and legal elements that are materially distinct from those required for a conviction under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). Binding precedent from *State v. Williams*, *State v. Jones*, *State v. Thomas*, and *State v. Davis* confirms that collateral estoppel does not apply in this context.

**WHEREFORE**, the trial court’s order denying Appellant’s plea in abatement is affirmed. The prosecution may proceed on the indictment.
