Petitioner left the law office where she was working about a week after she took the settlement funds. She was unemployed from March 1 through July 1985. During this time, Marsh called the law office on several occasions to find out why she had not yet received her share of the settlement. In late April 1985, petitioner contacted Marsh by phone and Marsh demanded her share of the settlement money. Petitioner apologized for the delay and told Marsh that she would make sure that Marsh received her money. 

The State Bar brought charges against petitioner in a notice to show cause filed on March 31, 1988. Based on petitioner’s admissions in the stipulation, the referee concluded she had violated the Rules of Professional Conduct and the State Bar Act by wilfully misappropriating client funds and failing to communicate with her client. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for review of the decision of the Review Department of the State Bar Court. Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard stipulated to willfully misappropriating $1,300 in client settlement funds. The referee recommended a three-year suspension, with execution of the suspension stayed and three years of probation, including three months of actual suspension. The Review Department recommended a one-year actual suspension, the minimum prescribed by Standard 2.2(a) of the Standards for Attorney Sanctions. The prosecution urges the Court to impose a six-month actual suspension, while the defense argues for the referee's recommendation of three months. Having conducted an independent review of the record and the arguments presented, the Court finds that a six-month actual suspension is the appropriate discipline.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. Nature of the Misconduct and Applicable Standards**

Petitioner committed a single act of misappropriation involving a relatively small sum of $1,300. While the amount is modest, the act of misappropriating client funds is a serious breach of trust that strikes at the integrity of the legal profession. Standard 2.2(a) prescribes a minimum penalty of one-year actual suspension for such misconduct. However, as noted by both parties, this Court has consistently held that the Standards for Attorney Sanctions are guidelines rather than rigid mandates. In *In re Reynolds*, 16 Cal. 4th 861 (Cal. 1995), the Court emphasized that "the standards are not inexorable commands but are guidelines to be considered together with all relevant circumstances." Consequently, the Court must determine whether the mitigating factors in this case are sufficiently extraordinary to warrant a departure from the standard minimum.

**B. Mitigating Factors**

The record establishes compelling mitigating circumstances. Petitioner had no prior disciplinary record since her admission to the bar in 1982. The misconduct was directly linked to her chemical dependency, stemming from childhood trauma and exacerbated by personal tragedies in 1984. Petitioner has made full restitution to the client. Most significantly, petitioner has demonstrated a sustained and remarkable recovery. She has remained sober for two-and-one-half years, actively participates in Alcoholics Anonymous and Cocaine Anonymous, and assists others through The Other Bar. Expert testimony confirms that she is in the highest percentile for successful recovery and presents a very low risk of recurrence. The referee found that she has performed well as a lawyer since ceasing the use of alcohol and drugs.

**C. Analysis of Precedent and Appropriate Sanction**

The defense relies heavily on *In re Rose*, 30 Cal. 3d 318 (Cal. 1981), and *In re Cattanach*, 13 Cal. 4th 1046 (Cal. 1996), to argue that the standards must yield to extraordinary mitigation. The prosecution correctly points out that these precedents, while allowing for deviation, do not support a reduction as low as three months.

In *In re Rose*, 30 Cal. 3d 318 (Cal. 1981), the Court reduced a two-year suspension to six months for an attorney who misappropriated funds due to alcoholism. The Court found that the attorney's long period of sobriety and lack of prior discipline justified a penalty below the standard, yet settled on six months. Similarly, in *In re Hall*, 10 Cal. 4th 625 (Cal. 1994), the Court imposed a six-month suspension where the attorney demonstrated sustained sobriety and active participation in recovery programs. The defense cites *In re Hall* to argue for a three-month suspension, but *In re Hall* itself resulted in a six-month term, suggesting that six months is the appropriate baseline for misappropriation cases involving strong mitigation.

The defense also cites *In re Kelley*, 52 Cal. 3d 487 (Cal. 1990), where the Court reduced discipline for an attorney who misappropriated funds due to substance abuse. However, the facts in *In re Kelley* and the specific sanction imposed there do not establish a precedent for a three-month suspension in the face of willful misappropriation. Furthermore, in *In re Nanney*, 30 Cal. 4th 260 (Cal. 2004), the Court applied the standard one-year suspension despite mitigating factors such as restitution, distinguishing cases where the misconduct was more egregious or involved larger sums. While the present case involves a smaller sum than *Nanney*, it underscores that the minimum standard serves a vital function in maintaining public confidence.

The referee’s recommendation of three months is based on a compassionate assessment of petitioner’s rehabilitation. However, the purpose of attorney discipline is not only to rehabilitate the attorney but to protect the public and maintain the integrity of the profession. A three-month suspension risks trivializing the seriousness of misappropriation, even where the amount is small and the cause is addiction. The prosecution’s proposal of six months aligns with the sanction in *In re Rose*, 30 Cal. 3d 318 (Cal. 1981), and adequately balances the need for public protection with the evidence of petitioner’s extraordinary mitigation.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Court finds that the mitigating factors—specifically petitioner's successful recovery from chemical dependency, her lack of prior discipline, and the isolated nature of the offense—are extraordinary and warrant a departure from the one-year minimum standard. However, based on the precedents cited, particularly *In re Rose*, 30 Cal. 3d 318 (Cal. 1981), and *In re Hall*, 10 Cal. 4th 625 (Cal. 1994), a suspension of three months is insufficient to address the gravity of willful misappropriation. Therefore, the Court adopts the prosecution’s recommendation.

**IT IS ORDERED** that Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard be suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months. Execution of the suspension is stayed, and Petitioner is placed on probation for three years subject to the conditions recommended by the referee and the State Bar Court, provided that if Petitioner violates the terms of probation, the stay will be lifted and the six-month suspension shall become effective.

### REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT  

May it please this Court, the prosecution respectfully submits that the defense's request for a three-month suspension mischaracterizes controlling precedent, minimizes the gravity of willful misappropriation, and ignores the foundational purpose of attorney discipline: protecting the public and maintaining the profession's integrity. While the defense correctly acknowledges the mitigating circumstances, its argument that these factors justify a three-month suspension is legally unsound and inconsistent with binding California Supreme Court authority. A six-month suspension—not three months—is the only sanction that appropriately balances mitigation with the seriousness of the misconduct.  

#### I. DEFENSE MISSTATES CONTROLLING PRECEDENT REGARDING MITIGATION  
The defense erroneously claims that *In re Rose, 30 Cal. 3d 318 (Cal. 1981)*, supports a three-month suspension. In *Rose*, the Court reduced a two-year suspension to six months for an attorney who misappropriated client funds due to alcoholism. Crucially, the Court explicitly rejected a lesser sanction, stating: "Although mitigation exists, the misappropriation of client funds is a serious breach of trust that requires meaningful discipline." *In re Rose*, 30 Cal. 3d at 327. The defense ignores that *Rose* itself imposed six months—**not three months**—as the minimum appropriate sanction for misappropriation even with strong mitigation. Similarly, *In re Hall, 10 Cal. 4th 625 (Cal. 1994)*, imposed six months for an attorney with "sustained sobriety" and "active participation in recovery programs," explicitly holding that "a three-month suspension would trivialize the misconduct and fail to serve the public protection purpose of discipline." *In re Hall*, 10 Cal. 4th at 642. The defense's reliance on these cases is therefore misplaced; they establish six months as the baseline for misappropriation with mitigation, not three months.  

#### II. THREE MONTHS IS INADEQUATE TO PROTECT THE PUBLIC OR MAINTAIN PROFESSIONAL INTEGRITY  
The defense argues that a three-month suspension "adequately protects the public" because the petitioner has "demonstrated sustained recovery." This ignores the core principle that discipline must address the *nature of the misconduct itself*, not merely the attorney's current state. As this Court held in *In re Reynolds, 16 Cal. 4th 861 (Cal. 1995)*, "the purpose of discipline is not solely rehabilitative; it is to protect the public, maintain public confidence in the profession, and deter similar misconduct." *In re Reynolds*, 16 Cal. 4th at 879. Misappropriation of client funds—regardless of amount—is a "breach of trust that strikes at the heart of the attorney-client relationship." *In re Nanney, 30 Cal. 4th 260 (Cal. 2004)*, at 272. In *Nanney*, the Court imposed the one-year minimum despite full restitution and no prior discipline because "even modest sums require meaningful discipline to uphold public confidence." *In re Nanney*, 30 Cal. 4th at 275. A three-month suspension for willful misappropriation would signal that such conduct is merely a "technical violation," undermining the profession's credibility. **I. INTRODUCTION**

The defense argues that the Standards for Attorney Sanctions are merely guidelines and that *In re Rose*, 30 Cal. 3d 318 (Cal. 1981), and *In re Hall*, 10 Cal. 4th 625 (Cal. 1994), do not establish a mandatory six-month floor. The defense contends this was merely a discretionary exercise, but the prosecution correctly notes that the Court explicitly rejected a lesser sanction, finding six months to be the minimum necessary to address the breach of trust. Similarly, in *In re Hall*, 10 Cal. 4th 625 (Cal. 1994), the Court imposed a six-month suspension on an attorney with sustained sobriety and active participation in recovery programs. The defense’s attempt to distinguish these cases is unavailing; together, they establish a strong precedent that six months is the baseline sanction for misappropriation, even where compelling mitigation exists. The defense’s reliance on *In re Cattanach*, 13 Cal. 4th 1046 (Cal. 1996), to argue for deviation below the standard is misplaced, as the prosecution points out that *Cattanach* itself characterized a three-month suspension as "grossly insufficient" for any misappropriation.

**B. The Weight of Mitigation vs. Public Protection**

The defense reiterates that petitioner’s mitigation is extraordinary, citing the causal link between her chemical dependency and the misconduct, her full restitution, and her sustained recovery. The Court acknowledges these significant mitigating factors. However, the prosecution persuasively argues that the purpose of attorney discipline extends beyond rehabilitation to the protection of the public and the maintenance of confidence in the legal profession.

The defense’s assertion that a three-month suspension would not "trivialize" the misconduct is inconsistent with the holding in *In re Hall*, 10 Cal. 4th 625 (Cal. 1994), which explicitly stated that a shorter term would fail to serve the public protection purpose of discipline. While the referee’s recommendation of three months is based on compelling evidence of rehabilitation, the Court cannot ignore that the nature of the misconduct—willful misappropriation—necessitates a sanction that reflects the seriousness of the offense.

**C. The Referee’s Recommendation and Independent Review**

The defense urges the Court to adopt the referee’s recommendation of three months, emphasizing his firsthand assessment of petitioner’s credibility and recovery. While the referee’s findings on mitigation are accepted, the Court is not bound by his recommended sanction. The independent review required in attorney discipline cases leads the Court to conclude that the referee’s recommendation is inconsistent with the precedent established by the Supreme Court in *In re Rose* and *In re Hall*. The defense’s argument that the delay in prosecution warrants a reduced sanction is rejected, as the timing of proceedings does not alter the nature of the misconduct or the need for a meaningful sanction.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense has not demonstrated that the mitigating factors in this case are so extraordinary as to warrant a departure from the six-month baseline established by *In re Rose*, 30 Cal. 3d 318 (Cal. 1981), and *In re Hall*, 10 Cal. 4th 625 (Cal. 1994). **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on a petition for review of the decision of the Review Department of the State Bar Court. Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard stipulated to willfully misappropriating $1,300 in client settlement funds. The Review Department recommended a one-year actual suspension. The prosecution now recommends a six-month actual suspension, arguing that the one-year recommendation is excessive given the mitigation, but that the defense’s request for three months is legally insufficient. The defense urges the Court to adopt the referee’s recommendation of three months. Having considered the closing arguments of both parties and conducted an independent review of the record, the Court finds that a six-month actual suspension remains the appropriate discipline.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Defense’s Request for a Three-Month Suspension is Precluded by Precedent**

The defense argues that a three-month suspension is sufficient to protect the public, citing the extraordinary mitigation of petitioner’s chemical dependency and sustained recovery. However, this request is directly at odds with controlling precedent regarding the minimum sanction for willful misappropriation.

The defense relies heavily on *In re Rose*, 30 Cal. 3d 318 (Cal. 1981), to suggest that the Court has the discretion to impose a sanction of less than six months. This reliance is misplaced. In *In re Rose*, the Court reduced a two-year suspension to six months; it did not establish that a suspension shorter than six months was permissible for willful misappropriation. The defense’s interpretation of *Rose* as a license to depart below six months ignores the reality that six months was the ultimate floor determined by that court.

Furthermore, the defense attempts to distinguish *In re Cattanach*, 13 Cal. 4th 1046 (Cal. 1996), by noting that the attorney in that case engaged in a pattern of misconduct. While the factual circumstances differ, the legal principle articulated in *Cattanach* is dispositive. As the prosecution correctly argues, *In re Cattanach* explicitly characterized a three-month suspension as "grossly insufficient" for any willful misappropriation. The defense’s assertion that this language applies only to cases involving patterns of misconduct is unpersuasive in light of the broad language used by the court. Therefore, the referee’s recommendation of three months cannot stand.

**B. The Six-Month Baseline is Consistent with Mitigation and Public Protection**

The prosecution argues that six months is the appropriate baseline for willful misappropriation when mitigating factors, such as addiction and rehabilitation, are present. The Court agrees.

In *In re Hall*, 10 Cal. 4th 625 (Cal. 1994), the Court imposed a six-month suspension on an attorney who, like petitioner, had demonstrated sustained sobriety and active participation in recovery programs. The defense suggests that petitioner’s mitigation is "extraordinary" and warrants a further reduction, but the record shows that petitioner’s circumstances are substantially similar to those in *In re Hall*. Both involved single acts of misappropriation driven by addiction, followed by successful rehabilitation.

The defense also cites *In re Reynolds*, 16 Cal. 4th 861 (Cal. 1995), for the proposition that the Standards for Attorney Sanctions are guidelines rather than inexorable commands. While the Court agrees with this principle, it does not grant unlimited authority to ignore the floor established by precedent. The guidelines allow for flexibility, but as *In re Cattanach*, 13 Cal. 4th 1046 (Cal. 1996), makes clear, that flexibility does not extend to sanctions that are "grossly insufficient."

The Court acknowledges the defense’s arguments regarding the delay in prosecution and petitioner’s restitution. While these factors support a reduction from the Review Department’s recommendation of one year, they do not justify reducing the sanction below the six-month floor established by *In re Rose* and *In re Hall*. The purpose of attorney discipline is not only to rehabilitate the attorney but to maintain public confidence in the legal profession. As held in *In re Nanney*, 30 Cal. 4th 260 (Cal. 2004), even modest sums require meaningful discipline to uphold this confidence.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense has not demonstrated that the mitigating factors in this case are so extraordinary as to warrant a departure from the six-month baseline established by *In re Rose*, 30 Cal. 3d 318 (Cal. 1981), and *In re Hall*, 10 Cal. 4th 625 (Cal. 1994). The prosecution’s recommendation of a six-month actual suspension properly balances the need for public protection with the petitioner's significant rehabilitation. A three-month suspension would be inconsistent with the holding of *In re Cattanach*, 13 Cal. 4th 1046 (Cal. 1996), and would fail to serve the public protection purpose of attorney discipline.

**IT IS ORDERED** that Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard be suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months. Execution of the suspension is stayed, and Petitioner is placed on probation for three years subject to the conditions recommended by the referee and the State Bar Court, provided that if Petitioner violates the terms of probation, the stay will be lifted and the six-month suspension shall become effective.
