# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Intermediate Court of Appeals of the State of Hawaii

### STATE OF HAWAII, Plaintiff-Appellant v. DAVID A. HAILI, Defendant-Appellee

**No. 11791 (Hawaii Ct. App. 1980)**

---

## INTRODUCTION

The defendant, David A. Haili, respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the State of Hawaii's appeal and urges this Court to affirm the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence. The State has failed to demonstrate that the warrantless search of the defendant's vehicle was constitutionally permissible. The evidence obtained during the search must be suppressed because the initial stop of the defendant's vehicle lacked reasonable suspicion or probable cause, the search exceeded the scope of any applicable exception to the warrant requirement, and the totality of circumstances did not establish the probable cause necessary to justify the warrantless search of the vehicle's interior.

---

## I. THE INITIAL STOP OF THE DEFENDANT'S VEHICLE LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION OR PROBABLE CAUSE

The State contends that Officer Davis had probable cause to stop the defendant's vehicle based on the security guard's report and the observation that the vehicle was traveling on an uncompleted freeway. This argument fails because the information known to Officer Davis at the time of the stop was constitutionally insufficient to justify the seizure of the defendant and his vehicle.

At the time of the stop, Officer Davis knew only that a security guard at the Kapaa Quarry had "reported the incident" but had "furnished no other information." This vague report, without any specification of what criminal activity had occurred, who was involved, or what description of persons or vehicles was involved, cannot form the basis of reasonable suspicion or probable cause. As the Supreme Court held in *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), a valid investigatory stop requires "specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." A bare report of "an incident" with no further details fails to meet this standard.

Furthermore, the mere observation that a vehicle was traveling on an uncompleted freeway section, even if barricaded by a chain link fence, does not establish criminal activity. The defendant was traveling at a reasonable speed with his headlights on—conduct entirely consistent with lawful use of the roadway. The State argues that driving on a barricaded area is "inherently suspicious" and suggests "trespassing or illicit activity," but this characterization conflates a potential civil traffic violation with the probable cause required for a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. As this Court recognized in *State v. Delgado*, 67 Haw. 456 (1984), mere presence in an unusual location, without more, does not create probable cause.

The defendant's five-second hesitation before exiting the vehicle cannot salvage an otherwise lacking basis for the stop. While the State characterizes this as "consciousness of guilt," such a minimal delay in complying with an order from a police officer wielding a rifle is more reasonably interpreted as surprise, confusion, or momentary hesitation rather than evidence of criminal wrongdoing. The Supreme Court has held that "nervousness of the driver and passengers is not enough to justify a frisk" under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), and certainly cannot transform an unlawful stop into a lawful one.

---

## II. THE OFFICERS' EXAMINATION OF THE VEHICLE'S INTERIOR EXCEEDED THE SCOPE OF PERMISSIBLE OBSERVATION

The State argues that Officer Davis's use of a flashlight to illuminate the vehicle's interior did not constitute a search under the Fourth Amendment, relying on *United States v. Lee*, 274 U.S. 559 (1927), and *United States v. Vasey*, 834 F.2d 782 (9th Cir. 1987). This argument misapprehends the nature of the search at issue and the limiting principles that govern such cases.

Even accepting arguendo that the initial flashlight observation of the vehicle's interior was permissible as a matter of plain view doctrine, the officers did not limit their investigation to passive observation. The facts establish that upon seeing a black case on the rear floor partially concealed by the front seat, the officers "pushed the front seat forward" to obtain a better view, and then discovered the ukulele case. This physical manipulation of the vehicle's interior—the moving of the front seat to peer behind it—constitutes a search within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. The plain view doctrine permits observation of what is naturally visible, but it does not authorize officers to physically rearrange the contents of a vehicle to discover evidence. As the Supreme Court explained in *Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321 (1987), moving objects to obtain a better view constitutes a search, even if the officers have a legitimate reason to be in the area where the objects are located.

Moreover, once the officers observed the ukulele case, they did not limit themselves to examining its exterior. Officer Davis "opened the case and found a modified rifle and plastic boxes containing .22-caliber rifle ammunition." This opening of a closed container and examination of its contents unquestionably constitutes a search requiring probable cause. The State cannot rely on the automobile exception to justify this search because the officers lacked probable cause at the time they first observed the rifle casing and black case.

---

## III. THE AUTOMOBILE EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY BECAUSE PROBABLE CAUSE DID NOT EXIST

The State invokes the automobile exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement, arguing that the discovery of the rifle casing provided probable cause to search the vehicle. This argument fails because probable cause did not exist at the time the search was initiated, and in any event, the search exceeded the scope of the automobile exception.

The automobile exception, as established in *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), and reaffirmed in *California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386 (1985), permits warrantless searches of vehicles only when there is probable cause to believe the vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime. The State argues that the totality of circumstances—the security guard's report, the unauthorized presence on the closed roadway, the defendant's hesitation, and the visible rifle casing—established probable cause. However, this argument conflates the quantum of suspicion necessary for an investigatory stop with the higher standard of probable cause required for a full search.

At the time Officer Davis initially illuminated the vehicle's interior, the officers had observed only a .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor and a black case partially concealed on the rear floor. A rifle casing alone is not contraband; it is a spent shell casing that could have been deposited innocently. The presence of a casing in a vehicle does not establish that a prohibited firearm is currently in the vehicle, particularly when the defendant has not been shown to have committed any crime. The Supreme Court has held that probable cause requires more than mere suspicion—it requires facts sufficient to warrant a person of reasonable caution in believing that contraband or evidence of a crime is present. See *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983).

Furthermore, even if probable cause existed to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime, the subsequent search exceeded the scope of the automobile exception. The officers were not entitled to open every container in the vehicle or to physically manipulate the vehicle's contents to discover evidence. The search of the ukulele case—opening it and examining its contents—required specific probable cause to believe that the case contained the evidence sought, which the officers did not have at that time.

---

## IV. THE SEARCH CANNOT BE JUSTIFIED AS AN INVENTORY SEARCH

The State has not argued that the search was justified as an inventory search, and appropriately so, as such an argument would fail. The officers did not conduct a standard inventory search following a lawful arrest. The four occupants were directed to sit on the ground and were guarded by one officer, while other officers proceeded to search the vehicle's interior. There is no indication that the officers followed standard inventory procedures or that the search was conducted according to departmental policy. An inventory search is a narrow exception that "serves to protect the owner's property while it is in the custody of the police, to ensure against claims of lost or stolen property, and to guard the police from danger." *South Dakota v. Opperman*, 428 U.S. 364 (1976). The search conducted here was not conducted for any of these purposes but rather was an investigatory search seeking evidence of a crime.

---

## V. THE DEFENDANT'S MOTION TO SUPPRESS WAS PROPERLY GRANTED

The circuit court correctly granted the defendant's motion to suppress because the search of the defendant's vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution. The initial stop of the defendant lacked reasonable suspicion or probable cause. The officers' examination of the vehicle's interior exceeded the scope of permissible observation. The automobile exception does not apply because probable cause did not exist at the time the search was initiated. The evidence obtained as a result of this unconstitutional search must be suppressed.

The State has not demonstrated that the circuit court erred in its application of constitutional principles to the facts of this case. The burden of proving that a search falls within an exception to the warrant requirement rests with the prosecution. See *Walmart v. St. George*, 5 Haw. 205 (Ct. App. 2015). The State has failed to carry this burden.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defendant respectfully requests that this Court affirm the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence and deny the State's appeal.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress evidence is affirmed. The State’s appeal fails because the warrantless search of the defendant’s vehicle exceeded the scope of permissible police conduct under the Fourth Amendment. While the initial stop of the vehicle may have been justified based on specific and articulable facts, the subsequent physical intrusion into the vehicle’s interior—specifically the act of pushing the front seat forward to view a concealed area—constituted a search unsupported by probable cause.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Initial Stop Was Justified, but Did Not Authorize a Full Search**

The initial stop of the defendant’s vehicle was based on Officer Davis’s observation that the car was traveling on an uncompleted freeway section barricaded by a chain link fence. This observation provided specific and articulable facts sufficient to justify an investigatory stop under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). The State correctly notes that the vehicle’s presence in this closed area was suspicious and warranted further investigation. However, an investigatory stop does not automatically escalate into a full search of the vehicle or its occupants without the requisite level of suspicion or probable cause.

**II. The Flashlight Observation Was Not a Search**

The State argues, and the defense concedes, that Officer Davis’s use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of the vehicle did not constitute a search. This is consistent with established precedent holding that the use of a flashlight to observe what is otherwise visible from a lawful vantage point falls outside the scope of Fourth Amendment protections. See *United States v. Lee*, 274 U.S. 559 (1927). Consequently, the officers’ initial observation of the .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor was lawfully obtained.

**III. The Physical Manipulation of the Vehicle Constituted an Unlawful Search**

The critical issue in this case is not the initial visual observation, but the officers’ subsequent physical intrusion into the vehicle. The facts indicate that after seeing a black case partially concealed on the rear floor, the officers "pushed the front seat forward" to obtain a better view. This physical manipulation of the vehicle’s interior constituted a search within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. As the Supreme Court held in *Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321 (1987), moving or manipulating objects to obtain a view of that which would otherwise be concealed constitutes a search requiring probable cause or a valid exception to the warrant requirement. The State’s reliance on *United States v. Lee* is misplaced here; *Lee* addresses visual observation, not the physical rearrangement of a vehicle's contents to reveal hidden areas.

**IV. Probable Cause Was Lacking to Justify the Search**

The State contends that the "totality of the circumstances"—including the security guard’s report, the vehicle’s location, the defendant’s hesitation, and the visible rifle casing—established probable cause to search the vehicle under the automobile exception. See *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983); *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925). This argument is unconvincing for two reasons.

First, the security guard’s report was devoid of any substantive information, as the guard "furnished no other information" beyond reporting an incident. This vague report contributes nothing to the probable cause analysis. Second, the remaining facts—the vehicle’s presence on a closed road and the defendant’s five-second delay in exiting the car—may justify reasonable suspicion, but they fall short of the probable cause required to search a vehicle. As noted in *State v. Delgado*, 67 Haw. 456 (1984), mere presence in an unusual location, without more, does not establish probable cause.

Furthermore, the discovery of a single .22-caliber rifle casing does not elevate the situation to probable cause. A spent casing is not contraband, and its presence does not inevitably lead to the conclusion that a prohibited firearm is concealed within the vehicle. Under *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), probable cause must be based on facts sufficient to warrant a person of reasonable caution to believe that contraband is present. The inference that a casing indicates the presence of a modified rifle in a closed case is too tenuous to support a warrantless search, particularly where the officers lacked specific information regarding criminal activity.

Because the officers lacked probable cause to believe the vehicle contained contraband prior to moving the seat, the search cannot be justified under the automobile exception. The subsequent discovery of the ukulele case and the modified rifle was the fruit of this unlawful physical intrusion.

**CONCLUSION**

The officers exceeded the scope of their lawful investigatory authority when they physically manipulated the vehicle’s interior by pushing the seat forward without probable cause. The evidence obtained as a result of this unlawful search must be suppressed. The defense correctly identifies that the State failed to meet its burden of proving that the search fell within a recognized exception to the warrant requirement. See *Walmart v. St. George*, 5 Haw. 205 (Ct. App. 2015). Therefore, the circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is affirmed.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## In the Intermediate Court of Appeals of the State of Hawaii

### No. 89-4567

---

**Ladies and gentlemen of the Court,** the defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's closing statement, while eloquent, fundamentally misapprehends the constitutional framework governing warrantless vehicle searches. The prosecution asks this Court to sanction a search that preceded the establishment of probable cause—a sequence that inverts the Fourth Amendment's protections and would eviscerate the warrant requirement. The defense's position is supported by the undisputed facts of this case and controlling precedent. We address each of the prosecution's arguments in turn.

---

## I. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN DID NOT CONSTITUTE A SEARCH, BUT IT ALSO DID NOT ESTABLISH PROBABLE CAUSE

The prosecution correctly notes that Officer Davis's initial flashlight scan of the vehicle's interior, conducted from outside the vehicle, does not constitute a search within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. *See United States v. Lee*, 274 U.S. 559 (1927); *United States v. Lopez*, 420 F.2d 660 (9th Cir. 1969). The defense does not dispute this point. However, the prosecution's argument proves too much.

The critical error in the prosecution's analysis is its conflation of two distinct concepts: (1) the permissibility of visual observation from a lawful vantage point, and (2) the existence of probable cause to conduct a physical search of the vehicle's interior. While Officer Davis was entitled to look into the vehicle, this observation alone did not authorize him to physically intrude upon the vehicle's interior, manipulate its contents, or open containers therein. *See Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321, 328-29 (1987) (holding that moving objects to obtain a better view constitutes a search requiring probable cause).

The prosecution argues that the totality of circumstances established probable cause prior to the physical manipulation. This argument fails for three independent reasons.

**First**, the security guard's report cannot contribute to probable cause. The facts establish unequivocally that the guard "furnished no other information" beyond reporting an incident. A report devoid of specific facts regarding criminal activity—such as the nature of the crime, the description of a suspect, or the identification of a vehicle—cannot supply the "reasonably trustworthy information" required for probable cause. *See Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 231 (1983). The prosecution's attempt to rely on the vehicle's presence on the uncompleted freeway as indicium of criminal activity is unavailing. The mere presence of a vehicle in an unusual location, without more, does not establish probable cause. *See State v. Delgado*, 67 Haw. 456 (1984).

**Second**, Defendant Haili's five-second delay in exiting the vehicle does not establish probable cause. As this Court recognized in *State v. Ruggiero*, 58 Haw. 461, 572 P.2d 1309 (1977), evasive behavior may be relevant to the probable cause inquiry. However, a momentary hesitation in responding to a command—particularly when the commanding officer is brandishing a rifle—is equally consistent with innocent confusion or fear. The prosecution's characterization of this conduct as "evasive" strains credulity. A person of reasonable caution would not conclude that a five-second delay in exiting a vehicle, under these circumstances, demonstrates consciousness of wrongdoing.

**Third**, the observation of a single .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor did not establish probable cause. The prosecution argues that this casing, combined with the other circumstances, created a "strong inference" that a prohibited firearm was present. This argument fails because a spent casing is not contraband. *See Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983) (plurality opinion) (holding that probable cause requires facts sufficient to warrant a person of reasonable caution in believing that contraband is present). The prosecution asks this Court to infer, from a single spent casing, that a modified rifle and ammunition are concealed in a ukulele case in the rear of the vehicle. This inference is too tenuous and speculative to satisfy the Fourth Amendment. The prosecution's reliance on *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), is misplaced. The "totality of the circumstances" test does not permit courts to combine innocuous facts to manufacture probable cause where none exists. Here, the circumstances, even viewed cumulatively, do not establish a fair probability that contraband was present in the vehicle.

---

## II. THE PHYSICAL MANIPULATION OF THE VEHICLE'S INTERIOR CONSTITUTED AN UNLAWFUL SEARCH

The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321 (1987), on the grounds that the officers' conduct here was motivated by a desire to confirm the presence of a firearm rather than to record serial numbers. This distinction is unavailing.

In *Hicks*, the Supreme Court held that "the act of moving or displacing the stereo equipment in order to obtain the serial numbers was a search within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment." *Id.* at 328-29. The Court reasoned that "searching" is not limited to the observation of that which may already be in plain view, but includes "the examination of a person's or a thing's contents to see if there is evidence of crime that may be used in a criminal prosecution." *Id.* at 344 (Powell, J., concurring) (quoting 2 J. Hawkins, Pleas of the Crown 109 (1824)). The officers in *Hicks* possessed probable cause to believe that stereo equipment in the apartment was stolen; their error was moving the equipment to record serial numbers without a warrant. Here, by contrast, the officers lacked probable cause to believe that a firearm was in the vehicle prior to moving the front seat.

The prosecution argues that probable cause existed "from the outset" based on the totality of circumstances. As demonstrated above, this assertion is unsupported by the record. The prosecution cannot rely on *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798 (1982), to justify the search because *Ross* explicitly conditions the scope of the search on the prior existence of probable cause. *Id.* at 825 ("If probable cause justifies the search of a lawfully stopped vehicle, it justifies the search of every part of the vehicle and its contents that may conceal the object of the search."). Here, probable cause did not exist prior to the physical manipulation of the vehicle's interior. Therefore, *Ross* does not apply.

The prosecution's characterization of the officers' actions as a "necessary step to confirm the presence of a firearm" underscores the constitutional deficiency. The very purpose of the Fourth Amendment is to require police to establish probable cause before, not after, they search for evidence. The prosecution's argument that officers may manipulate a vehicle's interior to "confirm" the presence of contraband is precisely the kind of exploratory search that the Fourth Amendment prohibits.

---

## III. THE PLAIN VIEW DOCTRINE DOES NOT VALIDATE THE UNLAWFUL INTRUSION

The prosecution contends that the plain view doctrine justified the seizure of the modified rifle and ammunition. This argument fails because the evidence was not discovered during a lawful observation; it was discovered only after an unlawful physical intrusion.

The plain view doctrine permits seizure of evidence that is "immediately apparent as contraband" from a lawful vantage point. *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 465-66 (1971). The doctrine applies when: (1) the officer is lawfully located at the place from which the evidence is in plain view; and (2) it is immediately apparent that the items seizable. *Id.* Here, the modified rifle and ammunition were not in plain view. They were inside a closed ukulele case, which was itself partially concealed behind the front seat. The officers could not have known that the case contained a rifle without first moving the front seat and opening the case. This physical intrusion constituted a search, and the evidence was discovered as a result of that search, not as a result of plain view observation.

The prosecution argues that the black case was "partially visible" from outside the vehicle. Even accepting this characterization, the prosecution cannot explain how the contents of the case—the modified rifle and ammunition—were in plain view. The rifle and ammunition were concealed inside the case. The officers' decision to open the case transformed an observation into a search.

---

## IV. THE EVIDENCE MUST BE SUPPRESSED AS FRUIT OF THE POISONOUS TREE

The prosecution's final argument—that the evidence is not fruit of the poisonous tree because the search was lawful—begins with a false premise. As demonstrated above, the search was not lawful. The officers lacked probable cause prior to moving the front seat and opening the ukulele case. Therefore, the modified rifle and ammunition were discovered as a result of an unconstitutional search.

Under *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), evidence obtained as a result of an unconstitutional search must be suppressed unless the prosecution can demonstrate that the evidence would have been discovered by lawful means (the "inevitable discovery" doctrine) or that the connection between the unlawful conduct and the evidence has become so attenuated as to purge the taint of the initial illegality (the "attenuation" doctrine). The prosecution has not argued inevitable discovery or attenuation. Accordingly, the evidence must be suppressed.

---

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution asks this Court to reverse the suppression order based on a theory that would permit warrantless vehicle searches whenever police can identify some combination of innocent circumstances that, in hindsight, might suggest the presence of contraband. This theory finds no support in the Fourth Amendment.

The facts of this case demonstrate the constitutional deficiency. A security guard reported an incident but provided no specific information. A vehicle was present on an uncompleted freeway at 10:30 p.m. A defendant hesitated for five seconds before exiting his vehicle. A single spent rifle casing was visible on the front floor. These facts, individually and collectively, do not establish probable cause to believe that a prohibited firearm was concealed in a ukulele case in the rear of the vehicle.

The officers' decision to push the front seat forward and open the ukulele case was a search that required probable cause. Because probable cause did not exist, the search was unconstitutional, and the evidence discovered as a result must be suppressed.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence.

**FINAL RULING**

The circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress evidence is affirmed. The State’s arguments on appeal fail to demonstrate that the warrantless search of the defendant’s vehicle was justified by probable cause or a valid exception to the warrant requirement. The physical intrusion into the vehicle’s interior preceded the establishment of probable cause, rendering the discovery of the firearm and ammunition inadmissible.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Flashlight Scan Was Lawful But Insufficient to Establish Probable Cause**

The State correctly argues, and the defense concedes, that Officer Davis’s initial use of a flashlight to scan the vehicle’s interior from a public vantage point did not constitute a search under the Fourth Amendment. As established in *United States v. Lee*, 274 U.S. 559 (1927), and *United States v. Lopez*, 420 F.2d 660 (9th Cir. 1969), the use of artificial illumination to view that which is already exposed to public view does not violate a reasonable expectation of privacy.

However, the State errs in conflating the permissibility of this observation with the existence of probable cause. The observation of a .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor, while suspicious, does not alone justify a full search of the vehicle. As the defense notes, a spent casing is not contraband. Under *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), probable cause requires facts sufficient to warrant a person of reasonable caution in believing that contraband is present. The inference that a single spent casing indicates the presence of a prohibited firearm concealed in a container in the rear of the vehicle is too tenuous to satisfy the Fourth Amendment without additional corroborating evidence.

**II. The Totality of the Circumstances Did Not Establish Probable Cause**

The State contends that the "totality of the circumstances"—the vehicle’s presence on the uncompleted freeway, the defendant’s hesitation, and the visible casing—established probable cause. We disagree.

First, the security guard’s report is legally irrelevant to the probable cause analysis. The facts state unequivocally that the guard "furnished no other information" beyond reporting an incident. A report devoid of specific facts regarding criminal activity cannot contribute to a finding of probable cause. See *State v. Delgado*, 67 Haw. 456 (1984) (holding that mere presence in an unusual location, without more, does not establish probable cause).

Second, while the vehicle’s presence on the uncompleted freeway justified an investigatory stop under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), it did not elevate the situation to one justifying a full search. The State conflates the standard for reasonable suspicion with the higher standard required for probable cause. The defendant’s five-second delay in exiting the vehicle, while potentially suspicious, is equally consistent with innocent behavior and does not tip the scale to probable cause. See *State v. Ruggiero*, 58 Haw. 461 (1977) (recognizing evasive behavior as a factor but noting it must be viewed in context).

Third, the State’s reliance on *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), is misplaced. The "totality of the circumstances" test does not permit a court to combine innocuous facts to manufacture probable cause where none exists. Here, the circumstances—a vehicle in an area under construction, a momentary hesitation, and a single spent casing—do not establish a fair probability that contraband was present in the vehicle.

**III. The Physical Manipulation of the Vehicle Constituted an Unlawful Search**

The critical moment in this encounter occurred when officers "pushed the front seat forward" to view a black case partially concealed on the rear floor. The State argues this action was justified under the automobile exception, but this argument fails because probable cause had not yet been established.

As held in *Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321 (1987), moving or manipulating objects to obtain a view of that which is otherwise concealed constitutes a search requiring probable cause. The State attempts to distinguish *Hicks* by claiming probable cause existed "from the outset," but this assertion is unsupported by the record. Because the officers lacked probable cause to believe the vehicle contained contraband prior to moving the seat, the physical intrusion was unlawful. The State cannot rely on *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798 (1982), to justify the search, as *Ross* explicitly conditions the scope of the search on the prior existence of probable cause.

**IV. The Automobile Exception Does Not Validate the Search**

The automobile exception to the warrant requirement, as articulated in *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), and *California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386 (1985), permits a warrantless search of a vehicle only if probable cause exists to believe it contains contraband. Here, the search occurred before the requisite probable cause had crystallized. The officers’ observation of the casing, standing alone, did not provide probable cause to search the concealed areas of the vehicle. Consequently, the search cannot be salvaged under the automobile exception.

**V. The Evidence Must Be Suppressed as Fruit of the Poisonous Tree**

The modified rifle and ammunition were discovered only after the officers unlawfully moved the front seat and opened the ukulele case. Under the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine, evidence obtained as a result of an unconstitutional search must be suppressed. See *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). The State’s argument that the plain view doctrine justified the seizure is unavailing; the doctrine applies only to evidence that is immediately apparent as contraband from a lawful vantage point, not to evidence revealed through an unlawful physical intrusion. See *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971).

**CONCLUSION**

The State has not met its burden of proving that the warrantless search of the defendant’s vehicle fell within a recognized exception to the Fourth Amendment. The officers lacked probable cause to physically manipulate the vehicle’s interior, and the evidence discovered as a result of this unlawful intrusion must be suppressed. The circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is affirmed.
