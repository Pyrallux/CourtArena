### I. THE COMMISSION'S DECISION IS SUPPORTED BY THE RECORD AND CONSISTENT WITH ESTABLISHED WISCONSIN LAW

The Court in *Johnson* explicitly held that "the mere fact that the employee is traveling to or from the place of performance of his contract of employment does not bring him within the coverage of the Workmen's Compensation Act." *Johnson*, 108 Wis. 2d at 596, 322 N.W.2d at 524.

#### B. The "Coming and Going" Rule Bars Recovery

Wisconsin has consistently adhered to the "coming and going" rule, which excludes from worker's compensation coverage injuries sustained by employees during their ordinary commute to and from work. This rule is grounded in the fundamental principle that an employee is not rendering service to the employer during the commute, as the commute serves the employee's own personal interest in reaching the place where he will perform his duties. *See General Teamsters Local No. 662 v. McNeil*, 156 Wis. 2d 544, 551, 457 N.W.2d 367, 371 (Ct. App. 1990) (holding that the "going and coming" rule is "deeply embedded in the common law of workers' compensation").

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish his case by invoking his union employment and the occasional practice of picking up supplies at headquarters. He was driving directly from his home to a job site, exactly as he would on any normal workday. The Commission specifically found that "there was no business-related purpose to the applicant's commute, he was simply going to work."

This Court in *McNeil* recognized that the going and coming rule applies even when the employer provides transportation or when the commute involves special circumstances, unless there exists a "special errand" or "special mission" that transforms the commute into compensable travel. *McNeil*, 156 Wis. 2d at 552, 457 N.W.2d at 371. The plaintiff has alleged no such special errand here. The Commission's finding that McRae was "not performing any special errand" is amply supported by the record.

#### C. The Traveling Employee Provision Does Not Apply

The plaintiff claimed below that he was a "traveling employee" under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f). This provision, however, applies to employees whose work requires them to travel away from the employer's premises, not to employees who simply commute from home to job sites. As this Court recognized in *McNeil*, the traveling employee exception applies "where the employee is engaged in a special mission or errand for the employer, or where the employee is required to travel as part of the employment." *McNeil*, 156 Wis. 2d at 552, 457 N.W.2d at 371.

McRae's employment as a painter did not require him to travel as a condition of his employment. He was assigned to particular job sites and drove from his home to those sites in the same manner as any other construction worker. His travel was not "away from the employer's premises" in any meaningful sense; rather, he was traveling to a location where he would perform his regular job duties. The Commission correctly determined that § 102.03(1)(f) does not apply.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**McRae v. Labor and Industry Review Commission**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from a decision of the Labor and Industry Review Commission (Commission) which affirmed the denial of worker's compensation benefits to the petitioner, McRae. The issue presented is whether McRae was "performing services growing out of and incidental to [his] employment" under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) at the time of his injury. Specifically, the Court must determine whether McRae's travel from his residence to a job site constitutes a compensable activity or falls under the "coming and going" rule exclusion.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The prosecution argues that the Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03 is entitled to great weight deference pursuant to *State ex rel. Kalal v. Circuit Court for Dane County*, 2004 WI 58, 272 Wis. 2d 146, 680 N.W.2d 110 (2004). Under *Kalal*, great weight deference is accorded to an agency's interpretation of a statute it administers when the interpretation is reasonable, the agency has expertise, and the statute involves technical or specialized subject matter. The defense counters that the Commission's decision involves a straightforward application of settled legal principles to undisputed facts, warranting de novo review.

Regardless of the standard applied, the outcome remains unchanged. The Commission's findings of fact are supported by the record, and its application of the "coming and going" rule to those facts is consistent with established Wisconsin jurisprudence.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Coming and Going" Rule**

Wisconsin law is clear that injuries sustained by an employee while traveling to and from work are generally not compensable. In *Johnson v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 108 Wis. 2d 589, 322 N.W.2d 520 (1982), the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that "the mere fact that the employee is traveling to or from the place of performance of his contract of employment does not bring him within the coverage of the Workmen's Compensation Act." *Johnson*, 108 Wis. 2d at 596, 322 N.W.2d at 524. The rationale for this rule is that the commute serves the employee's personal interest in reaching the workplace, rather than the employer's interest.

The undisputed facts in this case establish that McRae was driving his personal vehicle from his home in Twin Lakes to a job site at Aldrich Chemical Company in Milwaukee. He received no reimbursement for this travel. This scenario fits squarely within the "coming and going" rule articulated in *Johnson*. McRae was not rendering service to his employer during the commute; he was merely transporting himself to the location where his work would begin.

**B. Absence of a "Special Errand" or "Special Mission"**

The defense correctly notes that the "coming and going" rule is not absolute. An exception exists where the employee is engaged in a "special errand" or "special mission" for the employer. *General Teamsters Local No. 662 v. McNeil*, 156 Wis. 2d 544, 551, 457 N.W.2d 367, 371 (Ct. App. 1990). However, the record is devoid of any evidence suggesting McRae was performing a special errand at the time of the accident.

The Commission found that "there was no business-related purpose to the applicant's commute, he was simply going to work" and that he was "not performing any special errand." These findings are supported by the facts: McRae was driving directly from his home to the job site. Although the background indicates he occasionally stopped at the employer's headquarters to pick up supplies, he did not do so on the date of the injury. Without a deviation for a business purpose, the travel remains a personal commute. As stated in *McNeil*, the "going and coming" rule is "deeply embedded in the common law of workers' compensation," and absent a special mission, the rule bars recovery. *McNeil*, 156 Wis. 2d at 551, 457 N.W.2d at 371.

**C. The "Traveling Employee" Doctrine**

McRae also claimed status as a "traveling employee" under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f). The defense argues, and the law supports, that this provision applies to employees whose work requires them to travel away from the employer's premises as a condition of employment, not to employees commuting from home to a fixed job site. *McNeil*, 156 Wis. 2d at 552, 457 N.W.2d at 371. McRae's travel was not a condition of his employment; it was the means by which he reported to work. Therefore, the traveling employee doctrine does not apply.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Commission's determination that McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment at the time of the accident is correct. The undisputed facts establish that McRae was engaged in a routine commute without a business-related purpose or special errand. This falls squarely within the "coming and going" rule established in *Johnson* and affirmed in *McNeil*. Consequently, the Commission's decision denying worker's compensation benefits is affirmed.

In **General Teamsters Local No. 662 v. McNeil**, 156 Wis. 2d 544, 551, 457 N.W.2d 367, 371 (Ct. App. 1990), the court emphasized that the "going and coming" rule is "deeply embedded in the common law of workers' compensation," and absent a special mission, the rule bars recovery.

The prosecution's reliance on the **occasional** possibility of stopping for supplies does not transform this commute into a special errand. ### IV. THE TRAVELING EMPLOYEE DOCTRINE DOES NOT APPLY

The prosecution has not invoked the traveling employee doctrine, but the defense addresses it briefly for completeness. Under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f), traveling employees are those whose work requires them to travel away from the employer's premises as a condition of employment.

As explained in **McNeil**, 156 Wis. 2d at 552, 457 N.W.2d at 371, this provision applies to employees whose work inherently involves travel—not to employees commuting from home to a fixed job site. McRae's travel was not a condition of his employment; it was merely the means by which he reported to work. The traveling employee doctrine is inapplicable.

The prosecution argues that great weight deference requires this Court to uphold the Commission's interpretation. While the defense acknowledges the general principle of deference to agency expertise, the defense submits that:

**FINAL RULING**

**McRae v. Labor and Industry Review Commission**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from a decision of the Labor and Industry Review Commission (Commission) which affirmed the denial of worker's compensation benefits to the petitioner, McRae. The issue on appeal is whether McRae was "performing services growing out of and incidental to [his] employment" under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) at the time of his injury. Specifically, the Court must determine if McRae's commute from his residence to a job site constitutes a compensable activity or falls under the "coming and going" rule exclusion. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The prosecution argues that the Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03 is entitled to great weight deference pursuant to *State ex rel. Kalal v. Circuit Court for Dane County*, 2004 WI 58, 272 Wis. 2d 146, 680 N.W.2d 110 (2004). The prosecution contends that because the Commission administers the Worker’s Compensation Act, its interpretation of statutory exceptions to the "coming and going" rule must be upheld if reasonable. *Estate of Smith v. LIRC*, 2007 WI 12, 299 Wis. 2d 1, 727 N.W.2d 192 (2007).

The defense counters that while deference may be appropriate, the outcome is dictated by the undisputed facts and settled law, rendering the deference analysis ancillary. The defense argues that the "coming and going" rule is a matter of common law, and its application here is straightforward.

The Court agrees with the defense that the Commission's decision is correct regardless of the standard of review. However, the Court acknowledges the principle established in *Kalal* that an agency's interpretation of a statute it administers is entitled to great weight deference if it is reasonable. *Kalal*, 2004 WI 58, ¶ 46, 272 Wis. 2d 146, 680 N.W.2d 110. Here, the Commission's determination that McRae was not engaged in a special errand is a reasonable application of the law to the undisputed facts.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Coming and Going" Rule**

Wisconsin law is clear that injuries sustained by an employee while traveling to and from work are generally not compensable. In *Johnson v. Department of Industry, Labor & Human Relations*, 108 Wis. 2d 589, 322 N.W.2d 520 (1982), the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that "the mere fact that the employee is traveling to or from the place of performance of his contract of employment does not bring him within the coverage of the Workmen's Compensation Act." *Johnson*, 108 Wis. 2d at 596, 322 N.W.2d at 524.

The undisputed facts establish that McRae was driving his personal vehicle from his home in Twin Lakes to a job site at Aldrich Chemical Company in Milwaukee. He received no reimbursement for this travel. This scenario fits squarely within the "coming and going" rule articulated in *Johnson*. McRae was not rendering service to his employer during the commute; he was merely transporting himself to the location where his work would begin.

**B. Absence of a "Special Errand" or "Special Mission"**

The prosecution argues that the Commission's expertise in determining whether the "special errand" exception applies warrants deference. However, the prosecution's closing argument contains a fundamental contradiction. The prosecution urges the Court to reverse the Commission's decision, yet simultaneously argues that the Commission's interpretation should be deferred to because it is reasonable. Specifically, the prosecution asserts that "His commute... was reasonably interpreted by the Commission as serving a business purpose under § 102.03(1)." This statement is directly contrary to the record, which indicates the Commission found "no business-related purpose to the applicant's commute."

The defense correctly points out that the Commission's finding of "no business-related purpose" is a straightforward factual finding supported by the record. McRae was driving directly from his home to the job site. Although the background indicates he occasionally stopped at the employer's headquarters to pick up supplies, he did not do so on the date of the injury. The possibility of an occasional stop does not transform a routine commute into a special errand. As stated in *General Teamsters Local No. 662 v. McNeil*, 156 Wis. 2d 544, 457 N.W.2d 367 (Ct. App. 1990), the "going and coming" rule is "deeply embedded in the common law of workers' compensation," and absent a special mission, the rule bars recovery. *McNeil*, 156 Wis. 2d at 551, 457 N.W.2d at 371. The Commission's determination that no special errand existed is a reasonable application of the law to the undisputed facts.

**C. The "Traveling Employee" Doctrine**

The defense correctly argues that the "traveling employee" doctrine does not apply. This provision applies to employees whose work requires them to travel away from the employer's premises as a condition of employment, not to employees commuting from home to a fixed job site. *McNeil*, 156 Wis. 2d at 552, 457 N.W.2d at 371. McRae's travel was not a condition of his employment; it was the means by which he reported to work. Therefore, the traveling employee doctrine does not apply.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Commission's determination that McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment at the time of the accident is correct. The undisputed facts establish that McRae was engaged in a routine commute without a business-related purpose or special errand. This falls squarely within the "coming and going" rule established in *Johnson* and affirmed in *McNeil*. The prosecution's arguments regarding deference do not alter this outcome, particularly where the prosecution mischaracterizes the Commission's findings. The Commission's decision denying worker's compensation benefits is reasonable and supported by the substantive law.
