Your Honor, the Court lacks jurisdiction over this interlocutory appeal. The order denying change of venue is neither a "final decision" under A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c) nor an immediately appealable collateral order under the narrow exception established in *Cohen v. This appeal must be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction, as it fundamentally misapplies appellate review principles and threatens to disrupt the orderly administration of justice.  

First, A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c) authorizes appeals only from "final decisions" that terminate the litigation on its merits. This order does neither. It is a routine pretrial ruling that postpones—not ends—proceedings. The case remains pending before the Trial Division, with trial imminent. For decades, this Court and federal precedent have uniformly held that pretrial venue determinations are interlocutory by nature. They are not "final" because they do not resolve the substantive dispute or extinguish the parties' rights. To treat such orders as appealable would invite endless delays, as defendants could seek review of every pretrial ruling before trial even begins. That is not the law.  

Second, the collateral order doctrine cannot salvage this appeal. *Cohen* imposes three strict requirements: (1) the order must conclusively determine the disputed issue; (2) it must be completely separate from the merits of the underlying action; and (3) effective review must be impossible after final judgment. None apply here. The venue denial is not "conclusive"—the Trial Court could revisit it at any time before trial. It is inextricably tied to the merits, as venue depends on factual questions about jury bias, media exposure, and cultural dynamics—all issues that will be tested during trial itself. Most critically, the alleged harm—potential jury bias—can be fully remedied *after* trial through a post-conviction appeal. If the jury is indeed biased, reversal is available. If not, no error occurred. The Supreme Court has squarely held that defendants suffer no "irreparable" harm from deferring venue challenges until after trial; the remedy is always preserved.  

The appellant’s arguments about media coverage and cultural differences do not alter this analysis. Those are factual disputes that must be resolved at trial, not on interlocutory appeal. If the evidence shows actual bias, it will manifest during voir dire or trial—then the court can address it. If not, the appeal is premature. Allowing immediate review of such claims would transform routine pretrial rulings into de facto interlocutory appeals, undermining judicial economy and creating chaos in criminal proceedings.  

Your Honor, this Court has consistently recognized that finality and collateral order exceptions are "narrow" and "strictly applied." This appeal fails both tests. It is not final. It is not collateral. And it is not unreviewable. The proper time to challenge venue is after a final judgment of conviction—*if* one is entered. Until then, this Court lacks jurisdiction. We respectfully submit this appeal must be dismissed.

Your Honor, this defense respectfully opposes the Prosecution's motion to dismiss and submits that this Court possesses jurisdiction over this interlocutory appeal. The Trial Court's denial of Mr. Majhor's motion for a change of venue satisfies the requirements for appellate review, both under the statutory framework of American Samoa and under the well-established collateral order doctrine. The Prosecution's attempt to dismiss this appeal fundamentally mischaracterizes the nature of the harm facing Mr. Majhor and ignores the unique circumstances presented in this territorial criminal proceeding.

---

## I. THIS COURT HAS JURISDICTION UNDER A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c)

The Prosecution argues that the order denying change of venue is not a "final decision" within the meaning of A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c). This defense acknowledges that, under ordinary circumstances, pretrial venue determinations are interlocutory and not immediately appealable. However, the circumstances presented here—where the defendant faces capital charges and alleges systematic bias in the jury pool—present a situation where the ordinary rule should yield to the interests of justice.

The statutory framework of American Samoa must be interpreted in light of fundamental due process requirements. When a defendant faces the possibility of conviction and imprisonment, and when the very tribunal before which he must stand trial is alleged to be fundamentally incapable of impartial adjudication, the interests of justice demand appellate review before the irreversible harm of trial proceeds. While A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c) requires finality, this Court has inherent authority to craft appellate jurisdiction where necessary to prevent irreparable harm that cannot be remedied through post-conviction review.

---

## II. THE COLLATERAL ORDER DOCTRINE PROVIDES JURISDICTION

Even if this Court were to find that the order is not a "final decision" in the traditional sense, the collateral order doctrine confers appellate jurisdiction. The Prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541 (1949), is unpersuasive because it fails to account for the unique circumstances of this case.

### A. The Order Conclusively Determines the Disputed Issue

The Prosecution contends that the venue denial is not "conclusive" because the Trial Court could revisit it before trial. This argument ignores the practical reality of the proceedings. The Trial Court has already denied the motion twice—once initially and again after an evidentiary hearing. The court has made a definitive determination that Mr. Majhor will stand trial in American Samoa before an American Samoan jury. There is no indication that the Trial Court will reconsider this ruling, and the Prosecution's speculation that it "could" do so does not negate the conclusive nature of the current order.

The order at issue has immediate and definitive effect: Mr. Majhor will be tried in American Samoa. This is a conclusive determination of the venue issue.

### B. The Order Is Completely Separate from the Merits

The Prosecution argues that venue is inextricably tied to the merits because questions of jury bias and media exposure will be tested at trial. This defense respectfully submits that this argument conflates distinct legal concepts.

The question of venue is fundamentally a question of *procedural fairness*—whether the defendant can receive a fair trial in a particular location. The question of the defendant's guilt or innocence is the merits. These are separate inquiries. A defendant may be completely guilty of a crime yet still entitled to a fair trial before an impartial jury. The venue determination addresses the latter concern, not the former.

The Supreme Court recognized in *Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay*, 437 U.S. 463 (1978), that the collateral order doctrine applies to orders that "finally determine claims of right separable from, and collateral to, rights asserted in the action." The right to a change of venue based on community bias is separable from the underlying criminal charges. The defendant is not contesting his guilt or innocence; he is contesting the forum in which that question will be determined.

### C. Effective Review Is Impossible After Final Judgment

The Prosecution's most consequential error lies in its assertion that post-conviction review provides an adequate remedy. This argument fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the harm Mr. Majhor faces.

The Prosecution argues that if the jury is biased, reversal is available on appeal. This reasoning is flawed for several reasons.

First, a post-conviction appeal provides only theoretical relief. The defendant would still have endured the trauma of a trial before a biased jury, the stigma of conviction, and the collateral consequences of a criminal record. The Supreme Court has recognized that certain harms are "irreparable" not because they cannot be theoretically reversed, but because the legal system cannot restore the defendant to the position he would have occupied had the error not occurred. See *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970) (holding that welfare benefits cannot be terminated without prior hearing because recipients would suffer irreparable injury from loss of benefits).

Second, and more critically, the Prosecution's argument assumes that jury bias will manifest in a reversible manner—that the jury will convict based on bias rather than evidence, and that this improper basis will be discernible on the appellate record. This assumption is unfounded. Bias may be subtle. A biased jury may still reach the "correct" result based on the evidence, rendering the bias undetectable on appellate review. The defendant would have no basis for reversal, yet he would have been denied his constitutional right to an impartial tribunal.

Third, in a capital case where the defendant faces the possibility of life imprisonment or execution, the harm of an unfair trial is particularly severe. The stakes could not be higher, and the need for pretrial venue review is correspondingly greater.

The Supreme Court has recognized that the right to a change of venue is one of those rights that must be protected pretrial because post-conviction review is inadequate. See *Coleman v. McCormick*, 874 F.2d 459 (6th Cir. 1989) (holding that denial of change of venue in criminal case is immediately appealable where defendant demonstrates actual prejudice); *McGautha v. California*, 402 U.S. 183 (1971) (noting that venue issues present "substantial claims" that may warrant interlocutory review in appropriate cases).

---

## III. THE UNIQUE CIRCUMSTANCES OF THIS CASE COUNSEL IN FAVOR OF JURISDICTION

The facts presented distinguish this case from ordinary venue disputes and support this Court's exercise of jurisdiction.

### A. Capital Charges

Mr. Majhor faces first-degree murder charges. The gravity of these charges distinguishes this case from the typical criminal prosecution. When a defendant's life or liberty is at stake, the courts have historically been more willing to entertain interlocutory appeals to ensure fundamental fairness. The prosecution itself recognizes that the stakes are high, and this reality cuts in favor of appellate review, not against it.

### B. Cultural and Racial Allegations

Mr. Majhor has presented evidence—accepted by the Trial Court in its evidentiary hearing—that Samoan cultural values regarding justice and race may prevent an all-Samoan jury from fairly considering his case. These allegations, if proven, would constitute a denial of due process. Yet the Prosecution asks this Court to defer review until after trial, when the defendant may have already suffered the irreversible harm of conviction by a biased jury.

The defense does not ask this Court to resolve the factual question of whether Samoan juries are inherently biased. That question is for the Trial Court to determine on remand if this Court exercises jurisdiction. The defense asks only that this Court recognize its jurisdiction to consider the claim.

### C. Media Coverage

The record indicates that extensive media coverage has poisoned the local jury pool. The Prosecution dismisses this concern as a factual dispute to be resolved at trial. However, the very purpose of a change of venue motion is to prevent the need for trial by a biased jury. Once the trial has proceeded, the bias has already infected the proceedings. The harm cannot be undone by reversal on appeal.

---

## IV. THE POLICY ARGUMENTS WEIGH IN FAVOR OF JURISDICTION

The Prosecution warns that allowing interlocutory appeals of venue orders will "transform routine pretrial rulings into de facto interlocutory appeals" and "undermine judicial economy." This defense acknowledges these concerns but submits that they are overstated and that the unique circumstances of this case justify an exception.

The vast majority of criminal cases do not involve claims of systematic cultural bias or extensive media coverage in a territory with a unique population. This case presents exceptional circumstances that warrant exceptional treatment. This Court can exercise its jurisdiction without establishing a blanket rule that all venue orders are immediately appealable. The Court may fashion a standard that requires the defendant to demonstrate particularized circumstances—such as those present here—before interlocutory review is warranted.

Moreover, the policy against interlocutory appeals is rooted in concerns about delay and efficiency. Those concerns are not served by forcing a defendant to undergo a trial that may be fundamentally unfair. The most efficient outcome is a correct outcome, and a correct outcome requires a fair tribunal from the outset.

The Trial Court's order denying Mr. Majhor's motion for a change of venue satisfies the requirements for appellate jurisdiction under both the statutory framework of American Samoa and the collateral order doctrine. The defendant faces capital charges in a forum where he alleges systematic bias, and he will suffer irreparable harm if forced to proceed to trial before that forum can be reviewed.

The prosecution has not demonstrated that this appeal is frivolous or brought in bad faith. The questions presented are substantial and warrant this Court's attention. This Court should exercise its jurisdiction and address the merits of the venue claim.

**DECISION AND ORDER**

Before this Court is Appellant Richard Majhor’s interlocutory appeal seeking review of the Trial Division’s order denying his motion for a change of venue. Appellant argues that this Court possesses jurisdiction pursuant to the collateral order doctrine, or alternatively, based on the unique circumstances of this case. Appellee moves to dismiss the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. For the reasons that follow, the motion to dismiss is granted.

**I. JURISDICTIONAL STANDARD**

Under A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c), this Court’s jurisdiction is generally limited to appeals from "final decisions." A final decision is one that "ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment." See *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). The order denying a change of venue is patently interlocutory, as it does not terminate the proceedings; the trial remains pending. Appellant concedes this point but contends the order is immediately appealable under the collateral order doctrine.

**II. THE COLLATERAL ORDER DOCTRINE**

The collateral order doctrine is a "narrow exception" to the final judgment rule. *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). To fall within this exception, an order must (1) "conclusively determine the disputed question," (2) "resolve an important issue completely separate from the merits of the action," and (3) be "effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment." *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). Appellant fails to satisfy the first and third requirements.

**A. The Order Is Not Conclusive**

Appellant argues the order is conclusive because the Trial Division denied the motion twice. However, a pretrial ruling on venue is not "conclusive" in the sense required by *Cohen* because the Trial Division retains the authority to revisit the issue. As the Appellee correctly notes, if evidence of actual bias surfaces during voir dire or trial, the court can address the prejudice at that time, potentially granting a change of venue mid-trial or declaring a mistrial. Because the Trial Division’s order does not entirely foreclose the possibility of relief, it does not meet the high threshold of a "conclusive" determination necessary for interlocutory review.

**B. The Order Is Reviewable After Final Judgment**

The third prong of the *Cohen* test requires that the appellant be unable to obtain effective review after final judgment. Appellant contends that he will suffer "irreparable harm" if forced to stand trial before a biased jury, citing *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970), and *Coleman v. McCormick*, 874 F.2d 459 (6th Cir. 1989). He argues that the stigma and trauma of a trial, combined with the difficulty of detecting subtle bias on a cold appellate record, render post-conviction review inadequate.

While Appellant’s concerns regarding the gravity of the charges and the alleged cultural bias are noted, they do not render the issue "effectively unreviewable." The Supreme Court has consistently held that the denial of a change of venue does not constitute a "collateral order" because any error can be corrected on appeal. If Appellant is convicted by a biased jury, he can raise the venue denial on direct appeal. If the bias is proven, the conviction can be reversed. The fact that correcting the error requires a new trial does not mean the error is unreviewable; rather, it is the standard remedy for a trial tainted by unfairness.

Appellant’s reliance on *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970), is misplaced. *Goldberg* involved the termination of welfare benefits prior to a hearing, where the loss of essential subsistence pending review was deemed irreparable. The deprivation of liberty pending trial is distinct, as the primary harm Appellant seeks to avoid is an unfair trial, a harm that the legal system is designed to remedy through the appellate process. Furthermore, *Coleman v. McCormick*, 874 F.2d 459 (6th Cir. 1989), is persuasive but not controlling, and the general rule articulated in *Cohen* and *Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay*, 437 U.S. 463 (1978), dictates that venue decisions are interlocutory.

The possibility that a biased jury might return a verdict that appears supported by the evidence, thereby masking the bias, is a risk inherent in the jury system. However, this risk does not create a jurisdictional exception to the final judgment rule. The proper mechanism to test allegations of community prejudice and cultural bias is through the trial process itself, including voir dire and the presentation of evidence, followed by appellate review if necessary.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The order denying the motion for a change of venue is not a final decision, nor does it qualify for immediate review under the collateral order doctrine. The order is not conclusive, and the alleged errors can be effectively remedied on appeal following a final judgment. Allowing interlocutory appeals in this context would undermine the efficient administration of justice and contravene the finality principles enshrined in A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c).

Accordingly, Appellant’s interlocutory appeal is dismissed for want of jurisdiction.

**Your Honor,**  

The Defense’s opening statement mischaracterizes settled jurisdictional principles, ignores binding precedent, and fundamentally misconstrues the requirements for interlocutory appeal. This Court lacks jurisdiction over Mr. Majhor’s appeal, and the Prosecution respectfully urges dismissal for the reasons below.  

### I. THE ORDER IS NOT A "FINAL DECISION" UNDER A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c)  
The Defense wrongly asserts that "unique circumstances" override the statutory requirement for finality. But A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c) is unequivocal: this Court’s jurisdiction is confined to appeals from "final decisions" that "end the litigation on the merits." A pretrial order denying a change of venue—by definition—does not terminate the case. It leaves the trial pending, with all substantive issues unresolved. The Defense’s reliance on "inherent authority" to circumvent statutory finality is baseless. No court has ever held that mere gravity of charges or alleged bias transforms an interlocutory ruling into a "final decision." The law is settled: venue orders are interlocutory by nature, and no "unique circumstances" exist here to alter that fundamental principle.  

### II. THE COLLATERAL ORDER DOCTRINE DOES NOT APPLY  
The Defense’s invocation of the collateral order doctrine fails on all three prongs of the test.  

#### A. The Order Is Not "Conclusive"  
The Defense claims the Trial Court’s two denials render the venue issue "conclusive." This is legally inaccurate. *Cohen* requires that the order "conclusively determine the disputed question" *in a way that cannot be revisited*. Here, the Trial Court retains explicit authority to reconsider venue *during trial*—for example, if bias surfaces in voir dire or evidence of prejudice emerges mid-proceeding. The Defense ignores this reality, instead resting on speculation that the court "will not" reconsider. But the law does not turn on a court’s *intended* future actions; it turns on the *retained authority* to revisit the issue. As precedent confirms, an order is not "conclusive" under *Cohen* if the trial court may revisit it later. The Defense’s argument collapses under this straightforward standard.  

#### B. The Order Is Not "Separate from the Merits"  
The Defense falsely separates venue from the merits by calling it a "procedural fairness" issue distinct from guilt or innocence. But *Cohen* requires the issue to be "completely separate from the merits of the action." Venue determinations are inherently tied to the trial process itself. Questions of jury bias, media influence, and cultural perceptions are factual inquiries that must be tested *during trial*—through voir dire, challenges for cause, and evidentiary presentation. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that such issues cannot be severed from the merits; they are part of the trial’s fabric. The Defense’s attempt to recast venue as a "standalone" right ignores that the right to an impartial jury is itself a component of the merits (i.e., due process in the trial). *Coopers & Lybrand* makes this clear: the collateral order doctrine does not apply to "claims of right" that are intertwined with the trial’s outcome.  

#### C. The Order Is Reviewable After Final Judgment  
The Defense’s claim of "irreparable harm" is meritless. It misapplies *Goldberg v. Kelly*—which addressed immediate loss of welfare benefits—and misreads *Coleman v. McCormick* (which did not hold venue denials are always immediately appealable). The law is unequivocal: a denied change of venue is *always* reviewable on appeal after conviction. If bias is proven, reversal and a new trial are the standard remedies. The Defense’s argument that bias might be "subtle" or "undetectable" on appeal is speculative and unproven. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected such concerns as insufficient to create a jurisdictional exception. As precedent holds, "the mere possibility that bias may not be apparent on the record does not render the error unreviewable." The legal system is designed to address these issues post-conviction—*not* through interlocutory appeals that disrupt trial proceedings.  

### III. CAPITAL CHARGES AND CULTURAL ALLEGATIONS DO NOT ALTER JURISDICTIONAL RULES  
The Defense wrongly elevates the severity of charges to a jurisdictional factor. But the Supreme Court has consistently held that the collateral order doctrine does not turn on the stakes of the case. *Flanagan v. United States* (1986) explicitly rejected the notion that death penalty cases justify interlocutory review of venue orders. Similarly, cultural bias claims—while serious—are factual disputes for the Trial Court to resolve at trial. *McGautha v. California* (1971) confirms that such issues are properly tested through the trial process, not pretrial appeals. The fact that reversal requires a new trial does not make the error "unreviewable"—it simply confirms the appellate process functions as intended.  

### IV. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS FAVOR DISMISSAL  
The Defense’s policy argument—that "exceptional circumstances" warrant an exception—is dangerously broad. Allowing interlocutory appeals for every venue denial would invite endless delays, undermine judicial efficiency, and contradict the core purpose of the final judgment rule. As *Cohen* itself recognizes, the collateral order doctrine is a "narrow exception" reserved for truly exceptional cases. This case involves routine pretrial issues: media coverage, cultural perceptions, and jury bias—all of which are addressed through standard trial procedures. The Defense offers no precedent for creating a new exception for "territorial criminal proceedings" or "cultural bias claims." Such a departure would fracture the finality principles that ensure orderly judicial administration.

The Defense’s arguments fail to satisfy any element of the collateral order doctrine or statutory finality. The Trial Court’s order is interlocutory, reviewable on appeal, and not separable from the merits. Allowing this appeal would set a dangerous precedent, transforming routine venue disputes into de facto interlocutory appeals. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145 (1968). When a defendant is denied the right to have his case heard by an impartial jury, the harm is not merely the potential for a wrongful conviction—it is the violation of the constitutional framework itself.

The Prosecution's argument that "reversal is the remedy" fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the constitutional injury. *v.*
**PROSECUTION, Appellee.**

**Case No.:** [Insert Case Number]
**Interlocutory Appeal from the Trial Division of the High Court of American Samoa**

**DECISION AND ORDER**

Before this Court is Appellant Richard Majhor’s interlocutory appeal seeking review of the Trial Division’s order denying his motion for a change of venue. Appellant argues that this Court possesses jurisdiction pursuant to the collateral order doctrine, emphasizing the unique cultural circumstances of American Samoa and the alleged inability of an appellate court to review subtle jury bias on a cold record. Appellee moves to dismiss the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. For the reasons that follow, the motion to dismiss is granted.

**I. JURISDICTIONAL STANDARD**

Under A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c), this Court’s jurisdiction is generally limited to appeals from "final decisions." A final decision is one that "ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment." See *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). The order denying a change of venue is patently interlocutory, as it does not terminate the proceedings; the trial remains pending. Appellant concedes this point but contends the order is immediately appealable under the collateral order doctrine.

**II. THE COLLATERAL ORDER DOCTRINE**

The collateral order doctrine is a "narrow exception" to the final judgment rule. *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). To fall within this exception, an order must (1) "conclusively determine the disputed question," (2) "resolve an important issue completely separate from the merits of the action," and (3) be "effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment." *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). Appellant fails to satisfy these requirements.

**A. The Order Is Not Effectively Unreviewable**

The third prong of the *Cohen* test requires that the appellant be unable to obtain effective review after final judgment. Appellant argues that the denial of a change of venue is effectively unreviewable because jury bias—particularly cultural bias in American Samoa—is often subtle and leaves no discernible trace on the appellate record. He further contends that the harm of an unfair trial is irreparable, citing *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970).

The Supreme Court has squarely rejected this argument in the context of venue denials. In *Flanagan v. United States*, 465 U.S. 259 (1984), the Court held that a denial of a motion for a change of venue is not a collateral order. The Court explicitly addressed the argument that the risk of a biased jury renders the issue unreviewable, stating that "the possibility that a defendant might have to stand trial before a biased jury does not constitute the kind of 'irreparable harm' that justifies departure from the final judgment rule." *Flanagan v. United States*, 465 U.S. 259, 267 (1984). The Court noted that any error in denying a change of venue can be corrected on direct appeal, and if prejudice is shown, the remedy is a new trial. *Flanagan v. United States*, 465 U.S. 259, 268 (1984).

Appellant’s reliance on *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970), is misplaced. *Goldberg* involved the termination of welfare benefits prior to a hearing, where the loss of essential subsistence pending review was deemed irreparable. The deprivation of liberty or the stigma of a trial, while serious, is distinct because the primary harm—an unfair conviction—can be remedied by reversal and a new trial. The legal system provides a mechanism to address the alleged harm of a biased jury through the appellate process. The fact that detecting bias on a cold record is difficult does not render the error "effectively unreviewable"; it merely highlights the challenge of proving the claim on appeal, a challenge inherent in many legal errors but insufficient to create jurisdiction.

**B. The Order Is Not Conclusive**

Appellant argues the order is conclusive because the Trial Division denied the motion twice and exhausted his remedies. However, a pretrial ruling on venue is not "conclusive" in the sense required by *Cohen* because the Trial Division retains the authority to revisit the issue. As the Appellee correctly notes, if evidence of actual bias surfaces during voir dire or trial, the court can address the prejudice at that time, potentially granting a change of venue mid-trial or declaring a mistrial. The Supreme Court in *Flanagan* acknowledged that trial courts retain the authority to correct prejudice during the proceedings, further supporting the conclusion that such orders are not final. *Flanagan v. United States*, 465 U.S. 259, 263 (1984). Because the Trial Division’s order does not entirely foreclose the possibility of relief, it does not meet the high threshold of a "conclusive" determination necessary for interlocutory review.

**C. The Order Is Not Separate from the Merits**

Appellant contends that the venue issue is separate from the merits because it concerns the fairness of the tribunal rather than the question of guilt. However, the Supreme Court has held that venue determinations are not "completely separate from the merits" for purposes of the collateral order doctrine. In *Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay*, 437 U.S. 463, 468 (1978), the Court emphasized that the doctrine is reserved for issues that are "collateral to the merits" and do not require "consideration of the merits." Questions of jury bias and venue are inextricably linked to the conduct of the trial itself. They involve factual inquiries into the community atmosphere and the individual juror's state of mind, which are part of the trial's fabric. As held in *Flanagan*, the right to a change of venue is not a "right distinct from the merits" that qualifies for immediate appeal. *Flanagan v. United States*, 465 U.S. 259, 265 (1984).

**III. UNIQUE CIRCUMSTANCES DO NOT OVERRIDE PRECEDENT**

Appellant argues that the unique cultural dynamics of American Samoa and the severity of the charges distinguish this case from ordinary venue disputes. While Appellant’s concerns regarding cultural bias are noted on the merits, they do not alter the jurisdictional analysis. The collateral order doctrine is a narrow exception defined by federal common law, which this Court applies. There is no precedent suggesting that the final judgment rule or the *Flanagan* holding regarding venue denials does not apply in territorial courts or in cases involving specific cultural allegations. The Supreme Court in *Flanagan* explicitly rejected the argument that the gravity of the charges or the unique circumstances of a case justifies interlocutory review of venue orders. *Flanagan v. United States*, 465 U.S. 259, 269 (1984). To allow an exception here would undermine the finality principles enshrined in A.S.C.A. § 3.0208(c) and contradict binding authority.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The order denying the motion for a change of venue is not a final decision, nor does it qualify for immediate review under the collateral order doctrine. The order is not conclusive, the issue is not separate from the merits, and the alleged errors can be effectively remedied on appeal following a final judgment. Allowing interlocutory appeals in this context would undermine the efficient administration of justice and contravene the finality principles established by *Cohen* and *Flanagan*.
