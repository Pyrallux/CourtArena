The Court affirms the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. **ANALYSIS**

**I. Classification of Property**

The primary issue on review is whether the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits received by Loretta Angell constitute marital or nonmarital property. Both the prosecution and the defense agree in their opening arguments that these funds should be classified as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. The Court finds this position well-supported by Minnesota law.

Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3, defines nonmarital property to include property acquired by "gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse." The undisputed facts establish that Levi Angell, the parties' son, designated Loretta Angell as the sole beneficiary of his military life-insurance policy and the federal death-gratuity program. The prosecution correctly argues that these proceeds constitute a bequest from a third party. This Court has consistently held that life insurance proceeds payable to a spouse as a beneficiary are nonmarital property because they are acquired by bequest. In *In re Marriage of Kjelland*, 355 N.W.2d 504 (Minn. Ct. App. 1984), the Court held that "life insurance proceeds payable to a spouse as beneficiary are nonmarital property because they are acquired by bequest." This principle was reaffirmed in *In re Marriage of Kuntz*, 382 N.W.2d 847 (Minn. Ct. App. 1986), where the Court stated that such proceeds are nonmarital "regardless of when acquired during marriage," as the statutory exception for bequests rebuts the presumption of marital property. Furthermore, in *In re Marriage of Sorenson*, 447 N.W.2d 38 (Minn. Ct. App. 1989), the Court declared that "property acquired by bequest or inheritance is nonmarital even if received during marriage."

The defense agrees with this classification, noting that the funds are traceable exclusively to Levi’s beneficiary designations. As stated in *In re Marriage of Hahn*, 449 N.W.2d 849 (Minn. Ct. Here, the funds are traceable to Levi Angell, a third party, and were acquired by bequest. Therefore, the district court properly classified the benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property.

**II. Division of Benefits**

The second issue is whether the district court had the authority to divide Loretta Angell’s nonmarital benefits to prevent an unfair hardship to Gordon Angell. The district court relied on Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, to award Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property.

The defense argues that the district court properly exercised its authority under this state statute. However, the record indicates that Loretta Angell objected to this award on the grounds that it violated federal anti-attachment statutes protecting military death benefits. The defense acknowledges "the complex interplay between Minnesota state law and federal anti-attachment statutes" but fails to provide sufficient legal authority to demonstrate that state law can override the federal protections governing these specific funds.

The benefits at issue were paid pursuant to 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-80 (2000 & Supp. IV 2004) and the Emergency Supplemental Appropriations Act for Defense, the Global War on Terror, and Tsunami Relief, 2005, Pub. L. No. 109-13, § 1013(b), 119 Stat. 231, 247 (2005). These federal laws govern the payment of death gratuities and life insurance to active-duty service members. Where federal law provides specific protections for military benefits, state laws regarding the division of property must yield if they conflict with the federal scheme. The defense’s request for "clear guidance on remand" regarding this interplay effectively concedes that the district court’s reliance on state law alone may have been insufficient.

Because the benefits are federal in nature and subject to federal anti-attachment statutes, the district court erred by relying solely on Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, to divide them. The defense has not demonstrated that state law permits the division of these federal funds in the face of the federal protections cited by Loretta Angell. Consequently, the award of $150,000 to Gordon Angell from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property must be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is affirmed. The district court’s order dividing those benefits and awarding $150,000 to Gordon Angell is reversed. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

## Introduction and Statement of Position

This defense rebuttal is submitted on behalf of Gordon Angell ("Gordon") in response to the plaintiff's (Loretta Angell's) rebuttal brief. While the defense agrees with the classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell's nonmarital property—a position consistently maintained throughout these proceedings—the defense respectfully submits that the appellate court erred in reversing the district court's award of $150,000 from those benefits under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2. The plaintiff erroneously characterizes the division of these benefits as improper simply because they constitute nonmarital property. However, Minnesota law expressly authorizes the division of a spouse's nonmarital property in situations involving unfair hardship, and the plaintiff has failed to demonstrate that federal anti-attachment statutes preempt this well-established state authority. This defense rebuttal will address the plaintiff's mischaracterizations and establish that the district court properly exercised its statutory authority.

## I. The District Court Properly Exercised Its Authority Under Minnesota Statutes Section 518.58, Subdivision 2

### A. The Plain Language of Minnesota Law Authorizes the Award

The plaintiff contends that the district court lacked authority to award any portion of the death benefits to Gordon. This argument ignores the plain language of Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, which explicitly provides that "the court may apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to the other spouse" when necessary "to prevent an unfair hardship." The legislature enacted this provision precisely to address situations where rigid adherence to the marital/nonmarital classification would produce unjust results.

The district court found that an unfair hardship existed in this case, noting the 27-year duration of the marriage and the significant contributions made by Gordon to the family economic unit throughout that period. This factual determination is entitled to deference and should not be disturbed on appeal unless clearly erroneous. The plaintiff has not challenged the factual basis for the district court's hardship finding, instead attempting to circumvent the statute entirely by arguing that federal law prohibits any division of these specific funds.

### B. Federal Anti-Attachment Statutes Do Not Preempt State Hardship Authority

The plaintiff's argument that federal law prohibits the division of military death benefits rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the anti-attachment provisions. The relevant statutes, 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-1480, protect these benefits from execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process by creditors. However, the division of property in a dissolution proceeding is distinct from creditor garnishment.

As this Court recognized in its preliminary ruling, the question is not whether these benefits are subject to creditor claims—they clearly are not—but rather whether a state's equitable authority to divide property between spouses conflicts with federal law. The defense submits that it does not.

The anti-attachment provisions were designed to protect surviving service members and their families from predatory creditors and financial hardship following the death of a service member. See H.R. Rep. No. 93-1665 (1974), reprinted in 1974 U.S.C.C.A.N. 5531, 5539 (stating the purpose of the death gratuity is "to provide immediate cash to the survivor to meet expenses resulting from the member's death"). This protective purpose is not implicated when a court, acting within its equitable authority, allocates a portion of these funds to the surviving spouse who shared a long-term marital relationship with the deceased service member's parent.

The plaintiff has cited no controlling authority holding that the federal anti-attachment statutes preempt state property division statutes in dissolution proceedings. Indeed, the legislative history suggests the opposite: Congress enacted these provisions to protect benefits from outside creditors, not to insulate them from equitable division between spouses who jointly built the marital estate.

### C. The Plaintiff's Reliance on Federal Preemption Is Misplaced

The plaintiff argues that "federal law does not preempt state property classification rules" but fails to address the critical distinction between classification and division. Even accepting that the benefits retain their character as nonmarital property under Minnesota law—which the defense does not accept without qualification—the question of whether they may be divided presents a separate legal issue.

The plaintiff incorrectly assumes that classification as nonmarital property automatically precludes any division. Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, explicitly refutes this assumption by creating an exception for unfair hardship situations. The question before this Court is whether the legislature, in enacting the anti-attachment provisions, intended to override this state-law exception.

Nothing in the text of 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-1480 or the Emergency Supplemental Appropriations Act suggests Congress contemplated displacing state family-law dissolution procedures. The statutes address the mechanics of payment and the protections against creditors—they do not address the allocation of benefits between spouses in dissolution proceedings. This Court should decline to infer preemption where Congress has not clearly expressed an intent to override state domestic-relations law.

## II. Even If Classification as Nonmarital Property Is Sustained, the District Court's Award Should Be Affirmed

### A. The Presumption of Marital Property Was Not Adequately Rebutted

While the defense agrees with the classification outcome in this case, the defense reserves the right to challenge the legal analysis underlying that classification in other contexts. The plaintiff argues that the beneficiary designations alone are sufficient to establish that these funds constitute a "bequest" under Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3. However, this analysis overlooks the unique nature of military life insurance and death-gratuity benefits.

Unlike traditional life insurance, which a policyholder purchases with private funds, military life insurance is provided as a benefit of military service, and the death gratuity is a statutory payment made by the federal government. The question of whether these benefits represent a "bequest" from the deceased service member—as opposed to a payment from the government triggered by the service member's death—is not as straightforward as the plaintiff suggests.

In typical beneficiary-designation cases, the deceased policyholder has paid premiums with marital funds, creating a traceable connection to the marital estate. Here, however, Levi Angell was only 20 years old at the time of his death and had been in military service for a limited period. It is entirely possible that the premiums for Levi's military life insurance were deducted from his military pay, which itself may have been contributed to the family support during the marriage. The district court should have conducted a more thorough inquiry into the source of the funds used to acquire these benefits before classifying them as nonmarital.

The defense submits that even if the classification is affirmed, the district court's award to Gordon was appropriate as an alternative basis for achieving an equitable result.

### B. The Hardship Finding Was Supported by the Record

The district court found that an unfair hardship would result if Gordon received nothing from these benefits. This finding was based on the following undisputed facts: (1) the marriage lasted 27 years; (2) Gordon contributed to the family economy throughout the marriage; (3) the parties accumulated significant marital assets during the marriage; and (4) Gordon would be left without adequate support if the entire death-benefit package were awarded to Loretta.

The plaintiff has not challenged these factual findings as clearly erroneous. Instead, she argues that hardship alone is insufficient to overcome the nonmarital character of the property. However, this misreads Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, which expressly authorizes the court to apportion nonmarital property "to prevent an unfair hardship"—without requiring any additional justification.

The legislature clearly contemplated that hardship-based awards would be appropriate in some cases, and this case presents exactly such a situation. The district court did not abuse its discretion in making this award.

## III. The Plaintiff's Policy Arguments Do Not Support Reversal

The plaintiff urges this Court to affirm the reversal of the district court's award based on policy considerations, arguing that allowing division of military death benefits would undermine the purpose of these benefits and discourage military service. This argument is unpersuasive for several reasons.

First, the plaintiff conflates the purpose of the anti-attachment provisions—which is to protect benefits from creditors—with the entirely different question of whether these benefits may be equitably divided between spouses. The legislative history indicates that Congress was concerned with protecting surviving family members from predatory lending and financial exploitation, not with insulating death benefits from all forms of allocation in family-law proceedings.

Second, the plaintiff's policy argument proves too much. If accepted, it would mean that military death benefits could never be considered in dissolution proceedings, regardless of the circumstances. This would create an irrational result where a spouse could receive a windfall of hundreds of thousands of dollars simply because the funds came from a particular source, while the other spouse—who contributed to the marriage throughout a decades-long relationship—receives nothing. The legislature did not intend such an absurd result.

Third, the district court's award was not excessive. Of the total $500,352 in benefits, Gordon received only $150,000—approximately 30 percent. This is consistent with the statute's authorization of up to a 50 percent apportionment and reflects a reasonable balancing of the interests involved.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court:

The central issue on review is whether the district court erred in awarding Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2. The preliminary ruling reversed this award based on the conclusion that federal anti-attachment statutes prohibited the division. **A. Authority Under Minnesota Statutes Section 518.58, Subdivision 2**

Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, expressly authorizes a district court to "apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to the other spouse" when necessary "to prevent an unfair hardship." **B. Federal Preemption and Anti-Attachment Statutes**

The prosecution argues that the award violated federal anti-attachment statutes, specifically 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-1480 (2000 & Supp. IV 2004) and the Emergency Supplemental Appropriations Act for Defense, the Global War on Terror, and Tsunami Relief, 2005, Pub. However, the defense persuasively distinguishes between the protection of benefits from creditor claims and the equitable division of property in a dissolution proceeding.

The federal anti-attachment provisions protect military death benefits from "execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process by creditors." 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-1480. The legislative history confirms that the purpose of these statutes is "to provide immediate cash to the survivor to meet expenses resulting from the member's death" and to protect families from predatory creditors. The Court affirms the district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. The Court reverses the district court’s award of $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property to Gordon Angell.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Classification of Property**

The classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is affirmed. Both the prosecution and the defense agree that the funds acquired by Loretta Angell pursuant to her son Levi Angell’s beneficiary designations constitute nonmarital property. Minnesota Statutes section 518.003, subdivision 3, defines nonmarital property to include property acquired by "gift, bequest, devise, or inheritance from a third party to one but not the other spouse."

The parties correctly identify that Levi Angell’s designation of his mother as the sole beneficiary constitutes a bequest from a third party. This Court has previously held that "life insurance proceeds payable to a spouse as beneficiary are nonmarital property because they are acquired by bequest." *In re Marriage of Kjelland, 355 N.W.2d 504 (Minn. Ct. App. 1984)*. Furthermore, "property acquired by bequest or inheritance is nonmarital even if received during marriage." *In re Marriage of Sorenson, 447 N.W.2d 38 (Minn. Ct. App. 1989)*. The funds are traceable to Levi Angell’s designations, a third-party source, and thus fall squarely within the statutory exception. *In re Marriage of Hahn, 449 N.W.2d 849 (Minn. Ct. App. 1990)*. Therefore, the district court properly classified the benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property.

**II. Division of Benefits**

The central issue on review is whether the district court erred in awarding Gordon Angell $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property under Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2. The defense argues that this award was without legal authority and violated federal anti-attachment statutes. The Court finds the defense’s arguments persuasive and reverses the award.

**A. Federal Preemption and Anti-Attachment Statutes**

The defense argues that the federal anti-attachment statutes protecting military death benefits prohibit their division in a dissolution proceeding. The federal anti-attachment provisions protect military death benefits from "execution, levy, attachment, garnishment, or other legal process by creditors." 10 U.S.C. §§ 1475-1480 (2000 & Supp. IV 2004).

While the prosecution argues that the classification of the property is dispositive, it failed to address the defense’s contention that the award violates federal law. The defense correctly points out that the legislative history confirms the purpose of these statutes is "to provide immediate cash to the survivor to meet expenses resulting from the member's death" and to protect families from predatory creditors. H.R. Rep. No. 93-1665 (1974), reprinted in 1974 U.S.C.C.A.N. 5531, 5539.

The defense persuasively contends that permitting the division of these protected benefits in a dissolution proceeding undermines this protective purpose. The district court’s order requiring Loretta Angell to pay $150,000 to Gordon Angell effectively attaches her interest in the death benefits to satisfy a claim arising from the dissolution. This constitutes a "legal process" that diverts funds intended for the survivor’s immediate needs. Because the prosecution did not rebut this interpretation of the federal statute or demonstrate that Congress intended to allow such division in domestic relations proceedings, the Court finds that the district court lacked the authority to divide these federal benefits.

**B. Authority Under Minnesota Statutes Section 518.58, Subdivision 2**

Minnesota Statutes section 518.58, subdivision 2, authorizes a district court to "apportion up to one-half of a spouse's nonmarital property to the other spouse" only when necessary "to prevent an unfair hardship." The defense argues that the prosecution failed to establish the prerequisites for such an award.

The district court found an unfair hardship based on the duration of the marriage and Gordon Angell’s contributions. However, the defense correctly notes that the prosecution, in its closing statement, did not defend this finding or articulate specific, compelling circumstances of hardship warranting the appropriation of Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property. Given the protection afforded to these benefits by federal law, and the prosecution’s failure to demonstrate that the statutory prerequisites for an unfair hardship award were met, the district court’s reliance on section 518.58, subdivision 2, was in error.

The district court’s classification of the life-insurance and death-gratuity benefits as Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property is affirmed. The district court’s award of $150,000 from Loretta Angell’s nonmarital property to Gordon Angell is reversed. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
