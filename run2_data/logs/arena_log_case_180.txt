The Supreme Court has held that a pat-down search is permissible only when an officer has "a reasonable suspicion that the person stopped is armed and dangerous," as established in *Terry v. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS

---

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this opening response to rebut the prosecution's arguments and to affirm the lower court's denial of the suppression motion. The prosecution's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the encounter and ignores well-established precedent supporting the validity of the search in this case. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the ruling of the trial court.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

The prosecution attempts to frame this case as a simple Fourth Amendment violation involving an unlawful pat-down. However, the facts establish a far more nuanced encounter that involved a lawful investigative detention based on the defendant's suspended license, followed by a consensual search of the vehicle. The Defense acknowledges that the initial encounter was non-traditional—the defendant pulled his vehicle over on his own, and the officer did not initiate a traffic stop. Nevertheless, once the officer approached the defendant and obtained his license and registration, the officer was entitled to run a license check, which revealed that the defendant's license was suspended. This provided the officer with lawful authority to detain the defendant, issue a citation, and complete the license forfeiture paperwork. *See Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 434 U.S. 106, 111 (1977) (holding that once a lawful detention occurs, an officer may take steps necessary to effectuate the stop safely).

---

## II. THE ENCOUNTER WAS LAWFUL UNDER THE FOURTH AMENDMENT

### A. The Officer Had Lawful Authority to Approach the Defendant

The prosecution emphasizes that the defendant "pulled over on his own" and that "the officer did not initiate a traffic stop." This fact, while true, does not render the subsequent encounter unlawful. The Fourth Amendment is not implicated by a mere approach and conversation between a police officer and a citizen. *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 497-98 (1983). An officer may approach an individual in a public place, ask questions, and request identification without triggering Fourth Amendment protections, provided the officer does not coerce the individual into compliance. *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 200 (2002).

Here, the defendant was stopped at an intersection in the early morning hours, his vehicle positioned improperly in the center lane "well past the marked stop line, and cocked to the right." (Suppression Tr. 11-12.) The officer observed this traffic violation and had authority to investigate. Even assuming the defendant technically "pulled over on his own," the officer's decision to approach and investigate was objectively reasonable under the circumstances. *See Pennsylvania v. Primack*, 727 A.2d 563, 567 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1998) (officer may approach vehicle stopped for traffic violation to investigate).

### B. The Discovery of the Suspended License Justified Continued Detention

Once the officer obtained the defendant's license and registration and ran a check, learning that the license was suspended provided the officer with probable cause to believe a crime was being committed—driving while operating privilege is suspended or revoked. *75 Pa. C.S. § 1543(a)*. At that point, the officer was authorized to detain the defendant, issue a citation, and complete the license forfeiture form. *See Mimms*, 434 U.S. at 111 ("The driver is being detained pursuant to the police officer's exercise of his statutory authority to enforce the traffic laws."). The officer's actions in writing the citation and explaining the license seizure procedure were entirely lawful and consistent with his authority.

---

## III. THE SEARCH OF THE VEHICLE WAS VALID BASED ON CONSENT

### A. The Defendant Voluntarily Consented to the Search

The prosecution's primary argument focuses on the alleged "pat-down," but the facts establish that the search at issue was of the *vehicle*, not the defendant's person. The defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle after being told he was "free to go." The sequence of events is critical:

1. The officer completed the citation and explained the license forfeiture form.
2. The officer told the defendant he was "free to go" and that his girlfriend was coming to pick him up.
3. The defendant confirmed he was not going to drive.
4. The officer began walking back to his patrol car.
5. The defendant then "engaged me again in conversation" and asked about the license seizure form.
6. The officer explained the procedures again.
7. The officer asked if he could "ask [defendant] a question."
8. Defendant agreed.
9. The officer made a statement about "people bringing things out of Philadelphia" and asked if defendant "had anything that would concern me."
10. The defendant "offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle" and stated "I can search him." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.)

This sequence demonstrates that the search was consensual. The defendant was told he was free to leave, his girlfriend was en route, and he was not under arrest. The defendant then voluntarily agreed to the search. *See Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 40 (1996) (holding that consent to search is valid when given after a lawful detention where the totality of circumstances demonstrates that a reasonable person would feel free to leave).

### B. The Defendant's Consent Was Voluntary Under the Totality of Circumstances

The prosecution argues that the consent was invalid because the officer did not advise the defendant that he could decline. However, the Supreme Court has never required such warnings as a prerequisite to valid consent. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 232 (1973). Rather, voluntariness is determined by examining the totality of circumstances, including: (1) the defendant's age and education; (2) whether the defendant was informed of his right to refuse; (3) whether the defendant was in custody or under arrest; (4) whether the defendant was told a warrant was needed; (5) whether the officer used physical coercion; and (6) the duration of the encounter. *Id.* at 226-27.

Here, the totality of circumstances strongly supports voluntariness:

- The defendant was an adult who appeared fully capable of understanding the nature of his actions.
- The defendant was not handcuffed or under arrest.
- The officer had no weapon drawn. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5.)
- The defendant was described as "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative." (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19.)
- The defendant was told he was "free to go" before the consent was requested.
- The officer asked "if I could ask him a question," giving the defendant the option to decline. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25.)
- The defendant responded that "yes, I can — he would answer my questions." (Suppression Tr. 22:24-25.)

The prosecution's reliance on the officer's subjective belief that the defendant was being "deceptive" does not negate the objective fact of voluntary consent. *See United States v. Salander*, 59 F. Supp. 2d 1090, 1099 (D. Mont. 1999) (officer's subjective beliefs about defendant's truthfulness do not affect validity of consent).

### C. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Nature of the Search

The prosecution repeatedly refers to an "unlawful pat-down" but the record does not support this characterization. The officer testified that he asked to search the *vehicle*, and the defendant placed his hands on the vehicle to indicate consent. The officer searched "first in the compartment of the vehicle, then moved into the trunk" where he discovered drug evidence. (Suppression Tr. 24:10-15.) This was a search of the vehicle, not a frisk of the defendant's person.

Even if the prosecution were correct that a pat-down occurred, *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), authorizes a pat-down for weapons when an officer has reasonable suspicion that a person is armed and dangerous. The prosecution argues no such suspicion existed. However, the officer's subjective belief that the defendant was engaged in criminal activity—specifically, bringing drugs from Philadelphia—combined with the late hour, the defendant's inconsistent story about being lost, and his "overly cooperative" demeanor in placing his hands on the vehicle, provided specific, articulable facts supporting reasonable suspicion. *See United States v. Arvizu*, 534 U.S. 266, 273 (2002) (reasonable suspicion is a "commonsense, nontechnical" standard that allows officers to draw on their experience and expertise).

---

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON FLORIDA V. ROYER IS MISPLACED

The prosecution cites *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), for the proposition that a frisk is unconstitutional where the defendant is cooperative and exhibits no objective signs of danger. However, *Royer* is distinguishable.

In *Royer*, the defendant was detained in an airport terminal based on police suspicion that he was transporting drugs. The officers seized his luggage without his consent and without probable cause, and they detained him in a small room for an extended period. The Supreme Court held that the detention was unlawful because the officers lacked probable cause to believe Royer was engaged in criminal activity. *Id.* at 503-04.

Here, by contrast, the officer had probable cause to believe the defendant was driving with a suspended license. The defendant was not seized in a coercive manner—he was told he was free to go, and his ride was en route. The search was of the vehicle, not of the defendant's person, and it was conducted only after the defendant voluntarily consented.

Moreover, the prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1 (1989), is similarly misplaced. In *Sokolow*, the Court held that reasonable suspicion existed based on specific facts: the defendant paid $2,000 in cash for a one-way airline ticket, was traveling under an alias, had no luggage, appeared nervous, and was traveling to a source city for drugs. *Id.* at 8-9. Here, the prosecution attempts to equate the defendant's being "lost" with the defendant in *Sokolow*, but this conflates innocent behavior with suspicious behavior. The key difference is that in *Sokolow*, the combination of factors created reasonable suspicion; here, the prosecution asks this Court to find that a single factor—being lost—is insufficient. That is precisely what *Sokolow* holds, and it supports the Defense's position that the officer's suspicion, while perhaps insufficient standing alone, combined with other factors to create reasonable suspicion.

---

## V. ALTERNATIVELY, THE SEARCH WAS JUSTIFIED BY THE AUTOMBILE EXCEPTION

Under *Carroll v. # DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes the facts and misapplies the law. The motion to suppress should be denied because the search of the defendant's vehicle was conducted pursuant to valid voluntary consent given after the lawful seizure had concluded. The prosecution's arguments rest on a flawed interpretation of the encounter and ignore critical facts that establish the constitutionality of the search.

## II. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S "PAT-DOWN" ARGUMENT

### A. No Terry Pat-Down Occurred

The prosecution erroneously characterizes the encounter as involving an unlawful "pat-down" under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). This characterization ignores the actual facts established at the suppression hearing.

The officer's testimony establishes that the defendant *voluntarily offered* to be searched. The transcript specifically states:

> "He — without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.)

The defendant placed his hands on the vehicle and stated "I can search him" *before* any pat-down of his person occurred. (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.) This was an affirmative, voluntary offer to consent to a search of his person and vehicle—not a *Terry* frisk initiated by the officer.

The prosecution conflates a consensual self-search with an officer-initiated pat-down. However, when a defendant voluntarily places his hands on a vehicle and offers to be searched, this constitutes the *initiation* of a consensual encounter, not a *Terry* frisk. The officer did not conduct a pat-down search; he merely accepted the defendant's offer to search. The prosecution's reliance on *Terry* is therefore misplaced because *Terry* applies only to searches initiated by officers without consent. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991) (distinguishing between consensual searches and those conducted pursuant to *Terry*).

### B. Even Assuming Arguendo a Pat-Down Occurred, It Was Consensual

Even if this Court were to characterize the defendant's placement of his hands on the vehicle as a "pat-down" of his own person, such action was wholly voluntary and initiated by the defendant, not the officer. The officer testified that "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.)

The prosecution cannot transform a consensual act into an unconstitutional seizure simply by labeling it a "pat-down." The critical distinction under Fourth Amendment jurisprudence is whether the officer *initiated* the search without consent. Here, the defendant initiated the contact by offering to be searched. This distinguishes the instant case from *Terry*, where the officer initiated the search based on reasonable suspicion.

## III. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S "FRUIT OF THE POISONOUS TREE" ARGUMENT

### A. The Seizure Had Concluded Before Consent Was Given

The prosecution's reliance on the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine is fundamentally flawed because it assumes the defendant was still seized when he consented to the search. This assumption is contrary to the established facts.

The officer explicitly told the defendant he was "free to go" after completing the citation and license forfeiture paperwork. (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12.) The officer began walking back to his patrol car. (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.) The defendant remained behind his own vehicle, waiting for his girlfriend to pick him up. (Suppression Tr. 19:12-18.)

A seizure ends when a reasonable person would feel free to leave. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 436 (1991). Here, the officer *explicitly informed* the defendant that he was free to leave. The defendant then waited for his ride—a clear indication that he understood he was not detained. The prosecution argues that the encounter remained a "seizure" throughout, but this argument ignores the officer's explicit declaration that the defendant was free to go.

The prosecution's reliance on *Bostick* is itself misplaced. In *Bostick*, the Court held that police conduct that would otherwise be consensual may still constitute a seizure if "a reasonable person would not feel free to leave." 501 U.S. at 436. Here, the officer removed any ambiguity by explicitly telling the defendant he was free to go. Unlike *Bostick*, where the defendant was on a bus and felt compelled to submit to questioning, the defendant in this case was told he was free to leave, was not handcuffed, and was waiting for a ride he had arranged.

### B. The Consent Was an Independent Act of Free Will

Even assuming *arguendo* that some prior police conduct was improper, the defendant's consent was an independent act of free will that purged any taint. The Supreme Court has held that voluntary consent "is an act of free will, unconstrained by improper police conduct, that purges the taint of an illegal detention." *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 602 (1975).

The prosecution argues that the vehicle search was "inextricably linked" to the alleged pat-down because the events occurred within minutes of each other. (Prosecution Rebuttal at 8.) However, temporal proximity alone does not establish an inextricable link. The Supreme Court has explicitly rejected such a mechanical application of the attenuation doctrine. *Brown*, 422 U.S. at 603 ("The fact that the arrest and confession were only an hour apart and the petitioner was in the police station does not++ make the confession an act of free will sufficient to purge the primary taint of the unlawful arrest.").

Here, the defendant was told he was free to go, the officer walked away, and the defendant *independently re-engaged the officer* by asking questions about the ID procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18.) The defendant then voluntarily offered to be searched. This sequence of events demonstrates an independent act of free will. The defendant's decision to re-engage the officer and offer consent was not the product of any unlawful seizure—it was a voluntary choice made after the seizure had ended.

### C. The Totality of Circumstances Supports Voluntary Consent

The prosecution's attempt to invalidate the consent fails under the totality of the circumstances test established in *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 227 (1973).

The following circumstances support a finding of voluntary consent:

1. **The defendant was explicitly told he was free to go.** The officer stated, "I told him he was free to go." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.) This is the strongest evidence that the defendant understood he was not detained.

2. **The defendant was not handcuffed or restrained in any manner.** The officer confirmed his weapon was not drawn because he "had no safety concerns." (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4.)

3. **The defendant initiated the renewed contact.** After the officer began walking back to his patrol car, the defendant "engaged me again in conversation" by asking about the ID procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18.) The defendant was not sought out—he approached the officer.

4. **The defendant was fully informed of the nature of the encounter.** The officer asked if he could ask a question, and the defendant agreed. (Suppression Tr. 22:24-25.) The defendant was not confronted with a demand; he was asked a question and answered it.

5. **The defendant's demeanor was cooperative, not coerced.** The officer described the defendant as "agreeable" (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18), and the defendant had been polite and respectful throughout the encounter. (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19.)

The prosecution argues that the lack of warnings about the right to refuse consent renders the consent invalid. However, the Supreme Court has explicitly held that "the Fourth Amendment does not require that a consenting party be informed of his right to refuse." *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. at 232. The prosecution's argument on this point is directly contradicted by binding precedent.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S CHARACTERIZATION OF THE ENCOUNTER IS FACTUALLY FLAWED

The prosecution attempts to portray the encounter as a single, continuous seizure during which the defendant had no choice but to submit to the search. This characterization ignores critical facts:

The officer *did not initiate a traffic stop*. The car "pulled over on its own." (Suppression Tr. 13, 26, 30, 46, 59-60.) The defendant was not seized until the officer activated his lights, and even then, the seizure was lawful based on the traffic observation.

The officer *did not observe any signs of criminal activity*. The officer admitted that "aside from you not believing the story that he is lost, there's no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity." (Suppression Tr. 33:16-19.) There was no evidence of intoxication, no furtive movements, no contraband in plain view, and no articulable facts suggesting criminal activity. The officer's "suspicion" was based entirely on his disbelief of the defendant's story about being lost—a subjective hunch that does not constitute reasonable suspicion. *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 9-10 (1989) (holding that "innocent, unremarkable conduct" cannot support a *Terry* stop).

The prosecution's argument that the officer's subjective suspicions transformed the encounter into an unlawful seizure ignores the fact that the officer *explicitly told the defendant he was free to go*. Subjective beliefs, however strong, cannot override an explicit statement that the defendant was free to leave. If subjective officer belief could convert a consensual encounter into a seizure, the Fourth Amendment framework established in *Florida v. Bostick* would be rendered meaningless.

Fourth, the prosecution's reliance on the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine is misplaced because the consent was an independent act that purged any alleged taint.

The search of the defendant's vehicle was conducted pursuant to valid voluntary consent given after a lawful seizure had concluded. The motion to suppress was properly denied, and this Court should affirm the denial of the motion to suppress.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. Consensual Encounters Do Not Require Reasonable Suspicion

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. A search conducted pursuant to voluntary consent is reasonable and does not violate the Fourth Amendment. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Because consent is an exception to the warrant requirement, police need not have reasonable suspicion or probable cause to request consent to search. Id. at 222.

The key question is whether the consent was voluntary. The Supreme Court has held that voluntariness is a question of fact to be determined by the totality of the circumstances. Id. at 227. The prosecution bears the burden of proving that consent was voluntary by a preponderance of the evidence. Id. at 222.

### B. The Seizure Ended Before Consent Was Obtained

A seizure occurs when "a reasonable person would not feel free to leave." Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429, 436 (1991). Conversely, when a police officer explicitly informs a suspect that they are free to leave and physically departs, the seizure has ended. The suspect's subsequent decision to remain and engage in conversation is a consensual encounter.

Here, the officer testified that he told the defendant he was "free to go" and began walking back to his patrol car. Suppression Tr. 21:5-12. The defendant was not handcuffed, not placed in a police vehicle, and not physically restrained. The officer's weapon was not drawn. The officer had no safety concerns. Under these circumstances, a reasonable person would have felt free to leave. The defendant's subsequent re-engagement with the officer was a consensual encounter, not the continuation of a seizure.

### C. The Totality of the Circumstances Supports Voluntary Consent

In determining whether consent was voluntary, courts consider the characteristics of the defendant (including age, education, intelligence, and experience with the criminal justice system) and the conduct of the police (including the use of violence, the display of weapons, and the duration of the encounter). Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 227 (1973).

Here, the defendant was a cooperative, polite, and respectful individual throughout the encounter. He was not under the influence of alcohol or drugs. He was fully compliant with the officer's requests. He was not threatened or coerced in any manner. The officer did not display his weapon. The encounter occurred in a public location on the shoulder of a road. The defendant had a ride coming and was not stranded against his will.

The prosecution emphasizes the presence of police lights and a backup officer. However, the Supreme Court has held that the display of police authority does not automatically convert a consensual encounter into a seizure. Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429, 435 (1991). The lights were activated for the officer's protection, not to intimidate the defendant. The backup officer arrived as a matter of routine protocol and was not actively involved in the encounter. The defendant was told he was free to go.

The prosecution argues that the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go" was "meaningless" because of the police presence. This argument contradicts the Supreme Court's holding that the test for whether a seizure occurred is objective. The question is not whether the defendant subjectively felt free to leave, but whether a reasonable person in the defendant's position would have felt free to leave. Here, the officer explicitly told the defendant he was free to go and walked away. A reasonable person would have understood that they were at liberty to leave.

## IV. KEY POINTS

1. **The seizure concluded before consent was obtained.** The officer told the defendant he was "free to go" and began walking back to his patrol car. The defendant was not restrained and was free to leave.

2. **The consent was voluntary.** The defendant was not handcuffed, not threatened, and not coerced. The officer's weapon was not drawn. The defendant voluntarily offered to be searched after being told he was free to go.

3. **The defendant initiated the contact that led to the search.** After the officer told him he was free to go and walked away, the defendant re-engaged the officer in conversation. The defendant then voluntarily offered to be searched.

4. **The fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine does not apply.** Even assuming arguendo that some prior illegality occurred, the defendant's consent was an independent act of free will that purged any primary taint.

5. **The prosecution has not met its burden.** The prosecution has not established by a preponderance of the evidence that the consent was involuntary or that the search was otherwise unconstitutional.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

The prosecution's arguments mischaracterize the facts and misapply Fourth Amendment precedent. The search of the defendant's vehicle was conducted pursuant to voluntary consent given after the lawful seizure had concluded. The defendant's offer to be searched negated the need for a Terry analysis, and the fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine does not apply where the defendant's consent was an independent act of free will.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court deny the prosecution's motion to suppress and affirm its ruling denying the motion to suppress.

**FINAL RULING**

Based on the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, and upon a thorough review of the factual findings and applicable precedent, the motion to suppress is **denied**. The search of the defendant's vehicle was constitutional as it was conducted pursuant to the defendant's voluntary consent during a consensual encounter that followed the lawful conclusion of the initial seizure.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Seizure Concluded Prior to the Request to Search**

The prosecution argues that the defendant was not free to leave due to the presence of rotating police lights, a backup officer, and the time of night, rendering the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go" meaningless. However, the factual findings do not support the conclusion that the seizure continued indefinitely.

The record clearly establishes that the officer terminated the traffic stop. After explaining the license forfeiture procedures and citation, the officer testified that he told the defendant he was "free to go" and "started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.) Under *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 436 (1991), a seizure occurs only when a reasonable person would not feel free to leave. Here, the officer's explicit communication that the detention was over, coupled with his physical act of departing the scene, unequivocally signaled the end of the seizure. See *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 40 (1996) (holding that the Fourth Amendment does not require police to inform a motorist they are free to leave before asking for consent, but here the officer did so).

The prosecution's reliance on *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), is misplaced. In *Royer*, the officers retained the defendant's luggage and ticket and did not inform him he was free to leave. In this case, the officer returned the defendant's phone, informed him he was free to go, and physically withdrew. The defendant's decision to remain on the shoulder to wait for his girlfriend was a personal choice, not a legal compulsion. Therefore, the seizure had concluded before the officer requested consent to search.

**II. The Vehicle Search Was Based on Voluntary Consent**

The prosecution contends that the consent was involuntary due to the coercive atmosphere of the stop. The voluntariness of consent is determined by the totality of the circumstances. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 227 (1973). The court must look to the characteristics of the accused and the details of the interrogation.

Here, the facts support a finding of voluntariness. First, the defendant was not handcuffed, was not placed in a police car, and the officer's weapon was never drawn. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5.) Second, and most significantly, the defendant re-initiated the contact. After the officer told him he was free to go and began walking away, the defendant "engaged me again in conversation" to ask further questions about the license forfeiture form. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-18.) The officer responded, and then asked if he could ask a question. The defendant agreed. (Suppression Tr. 22:24-25.) Only then did the officer ask if the defendant had anything that would concern him, leading to the defendant's offer to be searched.

The defendant's offer to be searched—"without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle"—is a clear indication of a willingness to cooperate. (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.) The Supreme Court has held that the mere presence of a police officer or the fact that the encounter occurs on a public highway does not render consent involuntary per se. *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 444, 458 (1980). The prosecution has not shown that the officer's conduct was overtly coercive or that the defendant's will was overborne.

**III. The Lack of Reasonable Suspicion Is Not Dispositive**

The prosecution devotes significant attention to the argument that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to search the vehicle. While it is true that the officer's subjective suspicion regarding the defendant's story of being lost was vague, this fact is irrelevant to the admissibility of the evidence because the search was conducted pursuant to consent.

A search conducted pursuant to voluntary consent is valid under the Fourth Amendment regardless of the absence of probable cause or reasonable suspicion. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 219 (1973). Because the defendant voluntarily consented to the search after the seizure had ended, the officer was not required to articulate reasonable suspicion. The prosecution's reliance on *Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332 (2009), is inapplicable because *Gant* governs searches incident to arrest, not consensual searches.

**IV. The "Fruit of the Poisonous Tree" Doctrine Does Not Apply**

The prosecution argues that the evidence must be suppressed as the fruit of an unlawful seizure under *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). This doctrine is inapplicable because the consent was not the product of an illegal seizure.

Even if the initial detention were viewed with skepticism, the causal chain between the detention and the search was broken by the officer's termination of the seizure and the defendant's voluntary re-engagement. The Supreme Court has held that the "temporal proximity" between an illegality and a consent is not dispositive; rather, the court must look to the presence of intervening circumstances. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603 (1975). Here, the intervening circumstance—the officer telling the defendant he was free to go, walking away, and the defendant choosing to stay and ask further questions—was sufficient to purge any taint. The defendant's consent was an independent act of free will.

**CONCLUSION**

The defense has demonstrated that the seizure ended before the request for consent was made and that the consent was voluntary under the totality of the circumstances. The prosecution's arguments regarding the coercive nature of the environment are outweighed by the officer's clear statement that the defendant was free to leave and the defendant's own initiative in restarting the conversation. Therefore, the motion to suppress is denied.
