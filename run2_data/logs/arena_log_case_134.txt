COURT ARENA LOG - Case 134
FACTS:
DECISION AND JOURNAL ENTRY
HENSAL, Presiding Judge.
{¶ 1} Defendant-Appellant, Dana Kittle, appeals her conviction for illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs from the Summit County Court of Common Pleas. This Court affirms.
I.
{¶ 2} This case involves the discovery of a methamphetamine lab and Ms. Kittle's involvement with same. Officer Lemonier from the Akron Police Department testified that he and another officer conducted surveillance on an apartment where they believed illegal activity was occurring. The officers observed Ms. Kittle and another woman, J.S., leave the apartment and get into Ms. Kittle's car, with J.S. taking the driver's seat. Shortly thereafter, the officers performed a traffic stop on the vehicle. During the stop, the officers discovered that J.S. did not have a driver's license and that she had an outstanding felony warrant. As a result, they placed her under arrest and searched her, finding three bags of methamphetamine in her purse. The officers did not arrest or search Ms. Kittle, but did request that she empty her pockets, which revealed no illegal substances.
{¶ 3} The officers then returned to the apartment and knocked on the door. After about twenty minutes of waiting outside, one of the occupants allowed the officers inside. Upon entering, Officer Lemonier immediately smelled the distinct odor associated with the production of methamphetamine. The occupant then consented to a search of the apartment, which revealed a number of items associated with the production of methamphetamine, as well as several small bags containing the finished product.
{¶ 4} Through his investigation of the matter, including an interview with J.S., Officer Lemonier discovered information that implicated Ms. Kittle. Another officer located Ms. Kittle and brought her to the Akron Police Department for questioning. Officer Lemonier conducted the interview, wherein Ms. Kittle admitted to buying pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine.
*1243{¶ 5} A Grand Jury indicted Ms. Kittle on the following three counts: (1) illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs in violation of Revised Code Section 2925.041(A) ; (2) illegal manufacture of drugs in violation of Section 2925.04(A); and (3) aggravated possession of drugs in violation of 2925.11(A),(C)(1). The State dismissed the latter two counts prior to trial.
{¶ 6} The case proceeded to a bench trial. The State presented testimony from one witness: Officer Lemonier. The State also played portions of Officer Lemonier's interview with Ms. Kittle, and introduced records from The National Precursor Log Exchange ("NPLEX"), which is a system that electronically tracks purchases of pseudoephedrine. Ms. Kittle did not present any evidence.
{¶ 7} The trial court found Ms. Kittle guilty, and subsequently sentenced her to five years of imprisonment. She now appeals, raising three assignments of error for our review.
II.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I
THE TRIAL COURT ERRED WHEN IT ADMITTED THE NPLEX RECORDS INTO EVIDENCE AND ALLOWED A POLICE OFFICER WHO WAS NOT A QUALIFIED WITNESS TO LAY THE FOUNDATION FOR INTRODUCING THE NPLEX RECORDS INTO EVIDENCE, IN VIOLATION OF MS. KITTLE'S CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS PURSUANT TO ARTICLE I, SECTION 10, OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE CONFRONTATION CLAUSE OF THE SIXTH AMENDMENT OF THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 8} In her first assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court erred by allowing Officer Lemonier to lay the foundation for the NPLEX records because he was not the custodian of those records, nor was he otherwise qualified to do so. Ms. Kittle asserts that a de novo standard of review applies because the trial court's error in this regard implicated her rights under the Confrontation Clause.
{¶ 9} We will begin by addressing the standard of review. While Ms. Kittle's assignment of error suggests that she believes NPLEX records are testimonial in nature, thus implicating the Confrontation Clause, she does not develop an argument in that regard. See App.R. 16(A)(7). Rather, she cites Ohio Supreme Court precedent for the proposition that business records are nontestimonial, and states: "[a]ssuming that the NPLEX records of the pharmacy admitted into evidence in [this] case were truly nontestimonial in nature, the foundation for their proper authentication as 'business records' under the meaning of Evid.R. 803(6) could not have been established by [Officer Lemonier]." Ms. Kittle does, however, develop such an argument in her reply brief, arguing that NPLEX records are testimonial because the primary purpose of that system is to "assist in the interdiction of methamphetamine creation and use[.]"
{¶ 10} As an initial matter, we note that "[a]ppellate courts generally will not consider a new issue presented for the first time in a reply brief." State v. Quarterman , 140 Ohio St.3d 464, 2014-Ohio-4034, 19 N.E.3d 900, ¶ 18. Notwithstanding, we are unpersuaded by Ms. Kittle's argument that NPLEX records are testimonial in nature, thus implicating the Confrontation Clause. This is because "[t]estimonial statements are those created or *1244given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." State v. Garcia , 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208, ¶ 58, quoting Ohio v. Clark , --- U.S. ----, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015). The purpose of the NPLEX system, however, is to track purchases of pseudoephedrine. See R.C. 3715.05 ; State v. Coleman , 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, ¶ 35 ("The purpose of the NPLEx system is to monitor suspicious purchases of pseudoephedrine tablets."). We cannot say that NPLEX records are "created or given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." Garcia at id. ; United States v. Collins , 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir.2015) (holding that a similar "MethCheck" record was "not clearly testimonial in nature," and stating that "it is improbable that a pharmacy employee running a standard identification check of a customer would have anticipated that the records of that transaction would later be used against these particular defendants at trial."). We, therefore, reject Ms. Kittle's contentions that her rights under the Confrontation Clause were implicated, and that a de novo standard of review applies.
{¶ 11} We now turn to Ms. Kittle's argument that the trial court erred by allowing Officer Lemonier to lay the foundation for the NPLEX records. This Court reviews a trial court's decision to admit a business record into evidence for an abuse of discretion. State v. Baker , 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 9 ; Coleman at ¶ 35-42 (acknowledging that a NPLEX record is a business record). "A trial court is deemed to abuse its discretion where it admits a business record when the party lays an inadequate foundation to establish its admissibility in accordance with Evid.R. 803(6)." Baker at ¶ 9.
{¶ 12} Evidence Rule 803(6) provides that a business record is excepted from the hearsay rule if it is "kept in the course of a regularly conducted business activity, and if it was the regular practice of that business activity to make the * * * record * * *, all as shown by the testimony of the custodian or other qualified witness or as provided by Rule 901(B)(10)[.]" "A 'qualified witness' for this purpose would be someone with 'enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business.' " (Citation omitted.) State v. Hood , 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, quoting 5 McLaughlin, Weinstein's Federal Evidence , Section 803.08[8][a] (2d Ed.2009). Notably, "[t]he witness whose testimony establishes the foundation for a business record need not have personal knowledge of the exact circumstances of preparation and production of the document." Baker at ¶ 11, citing Evid.R. 803(6). The witness, however, must "demonstrate that he or she is sufficiently familiar with the operation of the business and with the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record in order to reasonably testify on the basis of this knowledge that the record is what it purports to be, and was made in the ordinary course of business." Id. , quoting Keeva J. Kekst Architects, Inc. v. George Dev. Group , 8th Dist. Cuyahoga No. 70835, 1997 WL 253171, *5 (May 15, 1997).
{¶ 13} Here, Officer Lemonier testified that, through his training as a law enforcement officer, he has personal knowledge as to how pharmacies use the NPLEX system to track purchases of pseudoephedrine. He testified that he applied for-and was granted-a license to access the system, which requires a username and password. He indicated that he accesses the NPLEX system every day, and that he *1245receives real-time alerts for pseudoephedrine purchases.
{¶ 14} Regarding the process for electronically tracking purchases, Officer Lemonier explained that a person attempting to buy pseudoephedrine must provide an ID, which is then swiped. As soon as the ID is swiped, the information contained thereon is immediately transmitted to the NPLEX database. Within a few minutes, more specific information is transmitted, including the brand name, pill count, grams of pseudoephedrine contained in the package, and whether the purchase was accepted or denied. He stated that a purchase will be denied if the individual attempts to purchase more than nine grams of pseudoephedrine during a 30-day period.
{¶ 15} Officer Lemonier testified that he personally obtained the NPLEX record for Ms. Kittle, which was introduced as an exhibit, and that the record is kept in the ordinary course of business. To obtain the record, he entered Ms. Kittle's name, date of birth, and driver's license number into the NPLEX system, which then generated a list of pseudoephedrine purchases associated with that information. Officer Lemonier then testified as to the purchases of pseudoephedrine reflected in the NPLEX record.
{¶ 16} While not every police officer may have familiarity with the NPLEX system or the ability to explain how those records came into existence in the ordinary course of business, the record reflects that Officer Lemonier did, in fact, have this knowledge. He, therefore, was a "qualified witness" for purposes of Evidence Rule 803(6). Thus, we cannot say that the trial court abused its discretion when it admitted the NPLEX records into evidence. Accordingly, Ms. Kittle's first assignment of error is overruled.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II
THE TRIAL COURT ERRED WHEN IT ABDICATED ITS DUTIES AS A NEUTRAL AND IMPARTIAL FINDER OF FACT BY DIRECTING THE PROSECUTION TO SUPPLEMENT ITS EVIDENCE AGAINST MS. KITTLE MID-TRIAL, IN VIOLATION OF HER CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS TO A FAIR TRIAL AND DUE PROCESS UNDER ARTICLE 1, SECTION 10 OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE FIFTH, SIXTH AND FOURTEENTH AMENDMENTS TO THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 17} In her second assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court abdicated its duties as a neutral and impartial finder of fact by directing the State to supplement its evidence against her at trial. Similar to her first assignment of error, she contends that a de novo standard of review applies, arguing that the trial court's actions deprived her of a fair trial. We disagree that a de novo standard of review applies, and otherwise find no merit in her argument.
{¶ 18} Evidence Rule 614(B) provides that "[t]he court may interrogate witnesses, in an impartial manner, whether called by itself or by a party." As this Court has stated, " '[t]he evident purpose of Evid. R. 614(B) is to prevent the trial judge, in questioning the witness, from conveying to a jury the judge's impression as to the credibility or lack of credibility of a witness.' " State v. Grad , 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44, quoting State v. Armstrong , 2d Dist. Montgomery No. 13498, 1993 WL 294834, *5 (Aug. 6, 1993). When, as is the case here, the matter is proceeding as a bench trial, the trial judge is afforded greater flexibility in questioning a witness because there is no risk of prejudicially *1246influencing a jury. Id. " 'In absence of any showing of bias, prejudice, or prodding of a witness to elicit partisan testimony, it will be presumed that the trial court acted with impartiality [in propounding to the witness questions from the bench] in attempting to ascertain a material fact or to develop the truth.' " (Alterations sic.) State v. Baston , 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), quoting Jenkins v. Clark , 7 Ohio App.3d 93, 98, 454 N.E.2d 541 (2d Dist.1982). "In reviewing any alleged prejudicial errors resulting from the court's own interrogation of a witness, a reviewing court will examine the questions in light of the entire record and, based upon the totality of the circumstances, determine whether there has been a manifest abuse of discretion." Grad at ¶ 43, quoting State v. Hoover , 9th Dist. Medina No. 1549, 1987 WL 12247, *2 (June 3, 1987).
{¶ 19} Here, when cross-examining Officer Lemonier, Ms. Kittle's counsel pointed out that the NPLEX record did not contain certain information, such as the form of identification used to purchase the pseudoephedrine. Officer Lemonier indicated that if he logged into the NPLEX system and clicked on a certain field, that information would populate onto the record, which he could then print. He further indicated that it was not his typical practice to print that information, and that it had never been an issue in prior trials that he has been involved in.
{¶ 20} The trial court noted that it was "engaged in a matter to find the truth[,]" and did not fault the State for failing to provide an expanded version of the NPLEX record. The trial court then allowed Officer Lemonier to print an expanded version, which included the missing information. Despite the trial court's willingness to allow Ms. Kittle's counsel a few days to review the record and prepare accordingly, her counsel elected to proceed as scheduled and chose not to cross-examine Officer Lemonier on the expanded record.
{¶ 21} Based upon the totality of the circumstances present in this case, we cannot say that the trial court's actions resulted in a manifest abuse of discretion. Id. at ¶ 43. Accordingly, Ms. Kittle's second assignment of error is overruled.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III
THE TRIAL COURT COMMITTED REVERSIBLE ERROR WHEN IT AMENDED THE INDICTMENT ON THE MORNING OF TRIAL TO EXPAND THE SINGLE DATE DETERMINED BY THE GRAND JURY TO INCLUDE THE ENTIRE PREVIOUS WEEK, IN VIOLATION OF HER CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS TO DUE PROCESS AND INDICTMENT BY A GRAND JURY UNDER ARTICLE 1, SECTION 10 OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE FIFTH, SIXTH, AND FOURTEENTH AMENDMENTS OF THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 22} In her third assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court erred by allowing the State to amend the timeframe of the indictment to include the previous week. We disagree.
{¶ 23} "We review a trial court's decision to allow the amendment of an indictment for an abuse of discretion." State v. Sauto , 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, ¶ 10. An abuse of discretion implies that the court's attitude is unreasonable, arbitrary or unconscionable. Blakemore v. Blakemore , 5 Ohio St.3d 217, 219, 450 N.E.2d 1140 (1983). To constitute reversible error, the defendant must show that: (1) the trial court abused its discretion; and (2) the amendment prejudiced her defense.
*1247State v. Dudukovich , 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16.
{¶ 24} Here, the indictment listed the date of the illegal-assembly-or-possession-of-chemicals-for-the-manufacture-of-drugs offense as on or about December 2, 2014 (the date the officers discovered the methamphetamine lab). On the morning of trial, the trial court allowed the State to extend the timeframe of the indictment to include the preceding seven days. Despite allowing the State to amend the indictment, the trial court ultimately determined that the evidence indicated that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine on November 30, 2014, that she gave those pills to J.S. so that J.S. could make methamphetamine, and that methamphetamine was made on December 2, 2014. The trial court's journal entry indicates that it found Ms. Kittle guilty based upon the December 2, 2014, date only. Thus, even assuming without deciding that the trial court abused its discretion, Ms. Kittle cannot establish that she suffered prejudice and, consequently, cannot establish reversible error. Id. at ¶ 16. Ms. Kittle's third assignment of error is overruled.
III.
{¶ 25} Ms. Kittle's assignments of error are overruled. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.
Judgment affirmed.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Defendant-Appellant Dana Kittle respectfully submits this defense opening response in opposition to the prosecution's arguments and in support of her assignments of error. The prosecution's opening brief attempts to minimize the serious constitutional concerns raised by Ms. Kittle, but fails to rebut the core legal deficiencies in the proceedings below. This Court should reverse the conviction because: (1) the trial court abused its discretion by admitting NPLEX records through a witness who lacked the requisite familiarity with the record-keeping system; (2) the trial court improperly intervened on behalf of the prosecution by directing the supplementation of evidence; and (3) the amendment of the indictment on the morning of trial violated Ms. Kittle's constitutional rights to due process and indictment by a grand jury.

---

## I. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I: ADMISSION OF NPLEX RECORDS THROUGH AN UNQUALIFIED WITNESS

### A. The Prosecution's Foundation Argument Fails

The prosecution contends that Officer Lemonier was a "qualified witness" under Evidence Rule 803(6) because he demonstrated familiarity with the NPLEX system. This argument misconstrues the qualified witness requirement and ignores critical deficiencies in the foundation laid.

Under Evidence Rule 803(6), a business record is admissible when the proponent establishes that the record was "kept in the course of a regularly conducted business activity, and if it was the regular practice of that business activity to make the record." The rule further requires testimony from "the custodian or other qualified witness." As the Ohio Supreme Court explained in **State v. Hood**, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, a qualified witness must have "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business."

The prosecution's reliance on **State v. Baker**, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, is misplaced. In Baker, this Court emphasized that the witness must "demonstrate that he or she is sufficiently familiar with the operation of the business and with the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record in order to reasonably testify on the basis of this knowledge that the record is what it purports to be, and was made in the ordinary course of business." Baker at ¶ 11.

Officer Lemonier's testimony fails to meet this standard. While he testified that he has "personal knowledge as to how pharmacies use the NPLEX system," this self-described knowledge does not establish that he is familiar with the specific record-keeping practices of the businesses that input data into NPLEX. The NPLEX system receives information from multiple pharmacies, each of which maintains its own internal record-keeping practices. Officer Lemonier has no knowledge whatsoever of how any particular pharmacy creates, maintains, or transmits its pseudoephedrine sales data to NPLEX. He cannot testify to the regular practice of those businesses because he has no familiarity with their operations.

The prosecution argues that Officer Lemonier's daily access to NPLEX and his understanding of how the system works from a law enforcement perspective qualifies him. This conflates knowledge of how to retrieve records with knowledge of how those records are created in the ordinary course of business. The rule requires the latter, not the former. A police officer who accesses a database regularly does not become a qualified witness to authenticate business records simply because he knows how to use the system.

### B. The NPLEX Records Are Testimonial

Even accepting arguendo that Officer Lemonier could lay a proper foundation, the prosecution's Confrontation Clause analysis is fundamentally flawed. The prosecution argues that NPLEX records are nontestimonial business records because their "primary purpose" is regulatory. However, the prosecution ignores the specific facts of this case, which demonstrate that the NPLEX system was created and maintained specifically to combat methamphetamine production.

Ohio Revised Code Section 3715.05, which establishes the NPLEX system, was enacted as part of Ohio's Methamphetamine Lab Eradication Act. The statutory framework demonstrates that the primary purpose of NPLEX is law enforcement. The system electronically tracks purchases of pseudoephedrine—a precursor chemical used exclusively in the manufacture of methamphetamine—specifically to identify suspicious purchasing patterns and prevent methamphetamine production.

In **State v. Garcia**, 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208, ¶ 58, the Ohio Supreme Court, quoting **Ohio v. Clark**, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015), defined testimonial statements as those "created or given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." The prosecution contends that NPLEX records are not testimonial because pharmacy employees could not have anticipated their use against specific defendants. This argument, however, misapplies the relevant test.

The proper inquiry is not whether a particular pharmacy employee anticipated use against a specific defendant, but whether the record was created with the primary purpose of serving as a substitute for trial testimony. Here, the NPLEX system was designed to create a permanent, searchable record of pseudoephedrine purchases for the express purpose of enabling law enforcement to investigate and prosecute drug offenses. Every entry into the NPLEX database is made with the knowledge that it will be used in criminal proceedings. This is precisely the type of "out-of-court substitute for trial testimony" that the Confrontation Clause was designed to address.

The prosecution's reliance on **United States v. Collins**, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), is similarly unavailing. In Collins, the Sixth Circuit characterized the MethCheck records as "not clearly testimonial in nature." However, that court did not conduct a thorough analysis of the primary purpose test, instead relying on the "improbability" that pharmacy employees would anticipate specific use. This reasoning has been criticized and does not bind this Court. Moreover, the Collins court expressly acknowledged that its holding was based on a deferential standard, noting that the records were "not clearly testimonial."

The defense maintains that NPLEX records are testimonial because they are created specifically for use in criminal prosecutions. The prosecution has not rebutted this argument with binding authority. This Court should hold that the trial court erred in admitting the NPLEX records without allowing Ms. Kittle to confront the custodians of those records.

---

## II. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II: TRIAL COURT ABDICATED ITS ROLE AS NEUTRAL FINDER OF FACT

### A. The Trial Court Exceeded Its Authority Under Evidence Rule 614(B)

The prosecution defends the trial court's actions by arguing that the court was merely "developing the truth" and that greater flexibility is allowed in bench trials. This argument ignores the fundamental principle that a trial court must remain neutral and impartial, regardless of whether the matter is tried to a judge or a jury.

Evidence Rule 614(B) provides that "[t]he court may interrogate witnesses, in an impartial manner, whether called by itself or by a party." (Emphasis added). The Ohio Supreme Court has consistently held that this authority must be exercised with great caution. In **State v. Baston**, 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), the Court cautioned that even in bench trials, the trial court must not convey an impression of impartiality, as the judge "is presumed to have acted with impartiality in attempting to ascertain a material fact or to develop the truth."

Here, the trial court did not merely ask clarifying questions. Rather, the record demonstrates that the trial court actively intervened to assist the prosecution in strengthening its case. When defense counsel pointed out that the NPLEX record did not contain certain information, the trial court directed Officer Lemonier to explain how to obtain an expanded version. The court then allowed the prosecution to supplement its evidence with this expanded record, despite the fact that such evidence was not produced during discovery.

The prosecution argues that this intervention was harmless because Ms. Kittle's counsel was given the opportunity to review the expanded record and cross-examine Officer Lemonier. However, the harm lies not in the opportunity to respond, but in the trial court's improper assumption of an adversarial role. The trial court effectively became an advocate for the prosecution by directing the supplementation of evidence. This violated Ms. Kittle's constitutional right to a fair trial before an impartial tribunal.

### B. The Trial Court's Actions Prejudiced Ms. Kittle

The prosecution fails to demonstrate that the trial court's intervention was harmless. The expanded NPLEX record contained additional information that was central to the prosecution's case. By allowing the prosecution to supplement its evidence mid-trial, the trial court deprived Ms. Kittle of the opportunity to prepare a full defense to the newly presented evidence.

The prosecution's argument that Ms. Kittle's counsel "elected to proceed" demonstrates a misunderstanding of the prejudice involved. Ms. Kittle's counsel was placed in an impossible position: either proceed with cross-examination without adequate time to review the new evidence, or waive the opportunity to cross-examine entirely. This is not a meaningful opportunity to respond.

This Court should hold that the trial court abused its discretion by directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence and that such abuse violated Ms. Kittle's right to a fair trial.

---

## III. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III: AMENDMENT OF INDICTMENT VIOLATED DUE PROCESS

### A. The Prosecution Cannot Establish Lack of Prejudice

The prosecution argues that any error in amending the indictment was harmless because the trial court ultimately relied only on the December 2, 2014 date. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the prejudice suffered by Ms. Kittle.

The indictment originally charged that the offense occurred "on or about December 2, 2014." On the morning of trial, the prosecution moved to amend the indictment to include "the preceding seven days." This amendment expanded the timeframe of the alleged offense from a single date to an entire week. The prosecution's argument that no prejudice occurred because the conviction was based on December 2nd ignores the reality that Ms. Kittle was forced to defend against a broader range of conduct than what was charged by the grand jury.

In **State v. Dudukovich**, 9th Dist. ### REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT ON ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I  

The defense’s argument that Officer Lemonier was an unqualified witness to lay the foundation for NPLEX records and that the records are testimonial under the Confrontation Clause is legally unsound and directly contradicted by binding precedent. The trial court properly admitted the NPLEX records under Evidence Rule 803(6), and no Confrontation Clause violation occurred. This Court should reject the defense’s arguments and affirm the judgment.  

#### I. Officer Lemonier Was a Qualified Witness Under Evid.R. 803(6)  
The defense mischaracterizes the "qualified witness" standard under Evidence Rule 803(6). A qualified witness need not be the custodian of records or have personal knowledge of *how a specific pharmacy creates its records*. Rather, the witness must demonstrate "sufficient familiarity with the operation of the business and with the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record in order to reasonably testify on the basis of this knowledge that the record is what it purports to be, and was made in the ordinary course of business." *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11. Critically, "the witness whose testimony establishes the foundation for a business record need not have personal knowledge of the exact circumstances of preparation and production of the document." *Id.*  

Officer Lemonier’s testimony fully satisfied this standard. He explained: (1) his training and daily use of the NPLEX system, including the requirement for a username and password to access it; (2) how pharmacies transmit data to NPLEX (e.g., ID swipe triggering immediate transmission of purchaser information to the database); (3) the specific data elements transmitted (brand name, pill count, grams of pseudoephedrine, and purchase acceptance/denial status); and (4) that the NPLEX record for Ms. Kittle was retrieved directly from the system and kept in the ordinary course of business. *See Decision and Journal Entry at ¶¶ 13–15*. This testimony established his familiarity with the *system’s operation and retrieval processes*, which is all that Evid.R. 803(6) requires.  

The defense’s claim that Officer Lemonier lacked knowledge of "how any particular pharmacy creates, maintains, or transmits its pseudoephedrine sales data" is irrelevant. The rule does not demand expertise in pharmacy record-keeping practices; it requires only a witness who can explain how the *business record system itself* functions. *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40 (holding that a qualified witness must have "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business"). Officer Lemonier’s testimony about the NPLEX system’s automated data transmission and retrieval process—derived from his direct experience using it—meets this standard. *See State v. Coleman*, 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, ¶ 35 (finding NPLEX records admissible as business records where law enforcement officer testified about system operation). The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the records.  

#### II. The NPLEX Records Are Nontestimonial, So the Confrontation Clause Does Not Apply  
The defense’s Confrontation Clause argument rests on a fundamental misapplication of *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015). Testimonial statements are those "created or given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." *State v. Garcia*, 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208, ¶ 58 (quoting *Clark*). The primary purpose of the NPLEX system is not to create evidence for trial but to monitor pseudoephedrine sales for regulatory compliance and public safety. *See R.C. 3715.05* (establishing NPLEX as a tool to track purchases to prevent methamphetamine production); *State v. Coleman*, 2015-Ohio-3907, ¶ 35 (confirming NPLEX’s "purpose is to monitor suspicious purchases of pseudoephedrine tablets").  

The defense wrongly conflates law enforcement *use* of the records with their *creation purpose*. The Sixth Circuit squarely rejected this argument in *United States v. Collins*, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), holding that similar precursor-tracking records "were not clearly testimonial in nature" because "it is improbable that a pharmacy employee running a standard identification check of a customer would have anticipated that the records of that transaction would later be used against these particular defendants at trial." *Id.* The records are generated in the ordinary course of retail transactions—pharmacies must comply with state law to sell pseudoephedrine, and the system’s design is purely commercial, not prosecutorial. *See R.C. 3715.05* (requiring pharmacies to submit sales data to NPLEX to prevent illegal purchases).  

The defense’s reliance on *State v. Garcia* is misplaced. *Garcia* does not support the claim that NPLEX records are testimonial; it merely reiterates the *Clark* standard for testimonial statements. The defense’s assertion that the system was "created and maintained specifically to combat methamphetamine production" ignores that the system’s design serves a broader regulatory function. *See R.C. 3715.05(B)* (stating NPLEX is "for the purpose of monitoring and tracking the sale of over-the-counter products containing pseudoephedrine to prevent the diversion of such products for the manufacture of methamphetamine"). This regulatory purpose—common to many business records—does not transform the records into testimonial statements. *See Clark*, 135 S.Ct. at 2183 (emphasizing that records created for routine administrative purposes are nontestimonial).  

#### III. De Novo Review Is Inapplicable  
The defense’s claim that de novo review applies because of a Confrontation Clause violation is unsupported. As established above, the NPLEX records are nontestimonial business records, so the Confrontation Clause is inapplicable. *See Davis v. Washington*, 547 U.S. 813, 821, 126 S.Ct. 2266, 165 L.Ed.2d 224 (2006) (Confrontation Clause applies only to testimonial statements). When a trial court admits business records under Evid.R. 803(6), appellate review is for abuse of discretion, not de novo. *Baker*, 2003-Ohio-4637, ¶ 9; *Coleman*, 2015-Ohio-3907, ¶ 35. The trial court did not abuse its discretion, so the defense’s request for de novo review must be denied.  

#### CONCLUSION  
Officer Lemonier was a qualified witness under Evid.R. 803(6), and the NPLEX records are nontestimonial business records not subject to the Confrontation Clause. The trial court properly admitted the evidence, and the defense’s arguments fail as a matter of law. This Court should overrule Assignment of Error I and affirm the conviction.

{¶ 1} Defendant-Appellant, Dana Kittle, appeals her conviction for illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs from the Summit County Court of Common Pleas. Having reviewed the rebuttals of the parties, we affirm the judgment of the trial court.

**I. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I**

{¶ 2} In her first assignment of error, Ms. Kittle reiterates that the trial court erred by admitting the NPLEX records through the testimony of Officer Lemonier. She argues that Officer Lemonier was not a qualified witness because he lacked familiarity with the specific record-keeping practices of pharmacies and that the records are testimonial, violating her rights under the Confrontation Clause.

{¶ 3} Regarding the qualification of the witness, Ms. Kittle distinguishes between familiarity with the NPLEX system as a user and familiarity with the business practices of the pharmacies that input the data. She relies on *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, for the proposition that a witness must explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business. However, as the prosecution notes, *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11, clarifies that a witness need not have personal knowledge of the exact circumstances of the document's preparation. The witness must demonstrate sufficient familiarity with the operation of the business and the retrieval of the record. Officer Lemonier testified regarding the electronic transmission of data from the pharmacy to the database, the specific data points transmitted, and the retrieval process. This testimony established his familiarity with the NPLEX system's operation, which satisfies the requirements of *Hood* and *Baker*. See *State v. Coleman*, 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, ¶ 35. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in determining that Officer Lemonier was a qualified witness.

{¶ 4} Ms. Kittle also argues that the NPLEX records are testimonial because the primary purpose of the system is law enforcement, citing Ohio Rev. Code § 3715.05. She attempts to distinguish *United States v. Collins*, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), by emphasizing the statutory language. However, the primary purpose test articulated in *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015), focuses on whether the statements were created with the primary purpose of creating a substitute for trial testimony. As the prosecution argues, and as held in *Collins*, it is improbable that a pharmacy employee entering a sale anticipated the record would be used against a specific defendant. The regulatory purpose of the system does not transform routine business records into testimonial statements. *State v. Garcia*, 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208, ¶ 58. Therefore, the Confrontation Clause is not implicated, and the trial court properly admitted the records. Ms. Kittle's first assignment of error is overruled.

**II. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II**

{¶ 5} In her second assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court abdicated its neutrality by directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence mid-trial. She contends that the court's intervention violated Evidence Rule 614(B) and her right to a fair trial.

{¶ 6} The record reflects that during cross-examination, defense counsel identified missing information in the NPLEX record. Officer Lemonier stated the information was available in the system. The trial court, stating it was "engaged in a matter to find the truth," allowed Officer Lemonier to print an expanded version. The court offered defense counsel time to review the new information, but counsel elected to proceed immediately.

{¶ 7} Under *State v. Baston*, 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), a trial court is presumed to have acted impartially when interrogating witnesses or managing evidence to ascertain the truth. In reviewing a trial court's actions, we must determine whether there was a manifest abuse of discretion based on the totality of the circumstances. *State v. Grad*, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 43. Here, the court's action was aimed at clarifying the evidence. The defense's claim of prejudice is undermined by the fact that counsel waived the opportunity to review the expanded record and chose to proceed without delay. Consequently, the trial court's actions did not constitute a manifest abuse of discretion. Ms. Kittle's second assignment of error is overruled.

**III. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III**

{¶ 8} In her third assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court erred by allowing the State to amend the indictment on the morning of trial to include the preceding seven days. She asserts that this amendment violated her rights to due process and indictment by a grand jury.

{¶ 9} The defense argues that the amendment prejudiced her by forcing her to defend against a broader timeframe. However, the record reveals that although the indictment was amended, the trial court ultimately found Ms. Kittle guilty based solely on the original date of December 2, 2014. Under *State v. Because the conviction was based on the specific date originally charged, and the defense does not demonstrate how the amendment affected the outcome of the trial regarding that date, Ms. Kittle cannot establish prejudice. Ms. Kittle's third assignment of error is overruled.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

{¶ 10} Based on the foregoing analysis, Ms. Kittle's assignments of error are overruled. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.

## I. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I: THE ADMISSION OF NPLEX RECORDS THROUGH AN UNQUALIFIED WITNESS VIOLATED MS. KITTLE'S CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS

### A. Officer Lemonier Was Not a Qualified Witness Under Evidence Rule 803(6)

The prosecution asks this Court to accept the proposition that any law enforcement officer who has received a username and password to access the NPLEX system is automatically qualified to authenticate records under Evidence Rule 803(6). This interpretation stretches the requirements of the rule beyond any reasonable limit and contradicts the Ohio Supreme Court's holding in State v. Hood, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057.

In Hood, the Ohio Supreme Court explicitly stated that a qualified witness must have "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business." The key phrase is "record-keeping system of the business"—not the record-keeping system of law enforcement's access to the business's data. Officer Lemonier's testimony established only that he knows how to use the NPLES system as an end-user. He demonstrated no knowledge whatsoever of how pharmacies actually input data, maintain their records, or ensure accuracy in the ordinary course of their business operations.

The prosecution cites State v. Coleman, 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, for the proposition that testimony about the NPLEX system's operation suffices. However, in Coleman, the witness testified about both the electronic transmission process and the pharmacy's internal practices. Here, Officer Lemonier admitted on cross-examination that he had no knowledge of how pharmacies actually maintain their records or the specific procedures they follow. He could only describe the data transmission from the pharmacy to the database—not how the record came into existence within the pharmacy's business operations.

The distinction is critical: knowing how data travels from Point A to Point B is fundamentally different from knowing how Point A creates and maintains the record in the first place. Under the standard articulated in Hood, Officer Lemonier was not a qualified witness.

### B. The NPLEX Records Are Testimonial and Their Admission Violated the Confrontation Clause

The prosecution's argument that NPLEX records are non-testimonial ignores the specific statutory scheme established by the Ohio Legislature in Ohio Revised Code Section 3715.05. That statute explicitly provides that the NPLEX system is established "for the purpose of monitoring suspicious purchases of pseudoephedrine products." The legislature's express purpose was not commercial regulation but rather drug interdiction.

In Ohio v. Clark, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015), the United States Supreme Court articulated the primary purpose test: testimonial statements are those created "with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." While the prosecution is correct that routine business records are generally non-testimonial, the NPLEX system was never designed for routine commercial purposes. It was designed specifically to track precursor chemical purchases for law enforcement purposes.

The prosecution's reliance on United States v. Collins, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), is misplaced. In Collins, the court addressed MethCheck records from a different state system and noted it was "improbable" that pharmacy employees would anticipate trial use. However, the Ohio Legislature explicitly designed NPLEX to serve as a law enforcement tool, and Ohio Revised Code Section 3715.05 makes clear that the system's primary purpose is monitoring for "suspicious purchases"—a term of art in drug enforcement. The records are created specifically because law enforcement needs them to investigate and prosecute methamphetamine manufacturing.

Under the primary purpose test articulated in Ohio v. Clark, NPLEX records are testimonial. The trial court erred in admitting them without allowing Ms. Kittle to confront the pharmacy employees who actually created and maintained these records.

## II. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II: THE TRIAL COURT ABDICATED ITS DUTY AS NEUTRAL FINDER OF FACT

The prosecution argues that Evidence Rule 614(B) grants trial courts unlimited latitude to develop evidence during bench trials. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the rule's purpose and the constitutional limitations on judicial behavior.

Evidence Rule 614(B) provides that "[t]he court may interrogate witnesses, in an impartial manner, whether called by itself or by a party." The rule's plain language requires impartial questioning. Here, the trial court did not merely ask clarifying questions—it actively directed the prosecution to "supplement its evidence" mid-trial. This goes far beyond impartial questioning and enters the realm of advocacy.

The prosecution cites State v. Baston, 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), for the proposition that courts are presumed impartial. However, the Baston court also emphasized that this presumption can be rebutted. The record here demonstrates that the trial court crossed the line from impartial fact-finding to active case-building for the prosecution.

When defense counsel cross-examined Officer Lemonier about missing information in the NPLEX record, the proper response was either to allow cross-examination to continue or to note that the information was not in evidence. Instead, the trial court proactively directed the prosecution to obtain and introduce additional evidence. This is not the function of a neutral arbiter.

The prosecution argues that Ms. Kittle suffered no prejudice because her counsel declined additional time to review the expanded record. This argument turns the concept of prejudice on its head. The prejudice occurred when the trial court effectively gave the prosecution a second chance to strengthen its case after the defense had already exposed a weakness. The defense should not be penalized for making the best of a bad situation created by the trial court's error.

Under the totality of the circumstances, the trial court's intervention constituted a manifest abuse of discretion. State v. Grad, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 43.

## III. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III: THE AMENDMENT OF THE INDICTMENT VIOLATED MS. KITTLE'S CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS

The prosecution argues that because the trial court ultimately relied only on the original December 2, 2014 date, the amendment to the indictment was harmless. This argument ignores the fundamental constitutional interests at stake.

Article I, Section 10 of the Ohio Constitution guarantees the right to indictment by a grand jury. The grand jury specifically charged Ms. Kittle with illegal assembly or possession of chemicals "on or about December 2, 2014." On the morning of trial, the prosecution moved to amend this to include "the preceding seven days"—a 700% expansion of the timeframe.

The prosecution's argument that no prejudice occurred because the conviction relied on the original date assumes facts not in evidence. The defense was prepared to defend against a specific charge occurring on December 2, 2014. The amendment required the defense to suddenly account for conduct occurring on any date during the previous week. This is inherently prejudicial because it forces the defense to guess which specific conduct the prosecution intends to prove and potentially requires alibi evidence for multiple dates.

Under State v. Dudukovich, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16, a defendant must show both an abuse of discretion and prejudice to establish reversible error based on an amendment to an indictment. Here, the defense has shown both. The trial court abused its discretion by allowing the amendment on the morning of trial, and the defense was prejudiced by having to defend against a broader timeframe than what the grand jury actually charged.

**STATE OF OHIO,**
*Plaintiff-Appellee,*

**v.**

**DANA KITTLE,**
*Defendant-Appellant.*

**Case No. 29234**

**DECISION**

{¶ 1} Defendant-Appellant, Dana Kittle, appeals her conviction for illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs from the Summit County Court of Common Pleas. Having reviewed the closing arguments of the parties and the record provided, we affirm the judgment of the trial court.

**I. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I**

{¶ 2} In her first assignment of error, Ms. Kittle renews her argument that the trial court erred by admitting the NPLEX records through the testimony of Officer Lemonier. She contends that Officer Lemonier was not a qualified witness under Evid.R. 803(6) because he lacked familiarity with the specific record-keeping practices of pharmacies, and that the records are testimonial in nature, violating her rights under the Confrontation Clause.

{¶ 3} We first address the qualification of the witness. Ms. Kittle argues that under *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, a witness must have familiarity with the "record-keeping system of the business," which she distinguishes from the NPLEX system accessed by law enforcement. She asserts that Officer Lemonier only testified as an end-user and could not explain how pharmacies create or maintain records. However, the record reflects that Officer Lemonier testified regarding the specific process by which pharmacies input data into the NPLEX system, including the swiping of identification cards and the electronic transmission of data such as brand name, pill count, and grams of pseudoephedrine. As held in *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11, a witness need not have personal knowledge of the exact circumstances of preparation but must demonstrate "sufficient familiarity with the operation of the business and with the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record." Officer Lemonier's testimony regarding the electronic transmission and retrieval of the data satisfies this standard. See *State v. Coleman*, 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, ¶ 35. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in determining he was a qualified witness.

{¶ 4} Regarding the Confrontation Clause, Ms. Kittle argues that the NPLEX records are testimonial because the primary purpose of the system, as defined in Ohio Rev. Code § 3715.05, is law enforcement monitoring rather than commerce. She relies on *Ohio v. Clark*, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015), for the proposition that records created with the primary purpose of aiding prosecution are testimonial. However, the United States Supreme Court in *Clark* defined testimonial statements as those created "with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." The primary purpose of the NPLEX record at the moment of its creation—the sales transaction—is to document the sale for commercial and regulatory compliance. As the Sixth Circuit held in *United States v. Collins*, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), regarding similar precursor-tracking records, "it is improbable that a pharmacy employee running a standard identification check of a customer would have anticipated that the records of that transaction would later be used against these particular defendants at trial." The statutory purpose of the system does not transform routine business records into testimonial statements. *State v. Garcia*, 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208, ¶ 58. Accordingly, the admission of the records did not violate the Confrontation Clause. Ms. Kittle's first assignment of error is overruled.

**II. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II**

{¶ 5} In her second assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court abdicated its neutrality by directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence mid-trial. She claims the court's action exceeded the bounds of Evid.R. 614(B) and constituted a manifest abuse of discretion.

{¶ 6} The record indicates that during cross-examination, defense counsel highlighted missing information in the NPLEX record. Officer Lemonier testified that the information was available in the system and could be printed. The trial court, stating it was "engaged in a matter to find the truth," allowed Officer Lemonier to print the expanded record. The court offered defense counsel additional time to review the new information, but counsel elected to proceed immediately.

{¶ 7} Under *State v. Baston*, 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), a trial court is presumed to have acted impartially when interrogating witnesses or managing evidence to ascertain the truth. In reviewing a trial court's actions, we look for a "manifest abuse of discretion" based on the totality of the circumstances. *State v. Grad*, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 43. Here, the court's intervention was prompted by the defense's own cross-examination and was limited to allowing the witness to produce a record he had already identified as existing. The court's offer of a continuance to the defense mitigates any claim of prejudice. We cannot say that the court's actions constituted a manifest abuse of discretion or that it abandoned its role as a neutral fact-finder. Ms. Kittle's second assignment of error is overruled.

**III. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III**

{¶ 8} In her third assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court erred by allowing the State to amend the indictment on the morning of trial to include the preceding seven days. She asserts that this amendment violated her rights to due process and indictment by a grand jury by forcing her to defend against a broader timeframe than originally charged.

{¶ 9} We review a trial court's decision to allow an amendment of an indictment for an abuse of discretion. *State v. Sauto*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, ¶ 10. To establish reversible error, a defendant must show both an abuse of discretion and prejudice. *State v. Dudukovich*, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16.

{¶ 10} While the trial court allowed the amendment to include the preceding week, the trial court's journal entry and findings of fact indicate that Ms. Kittle was found guilty based solely on the conduct occurring on December 2, 2014—the original date charged in the indictment. Ms. Kittle has not demonstrated how the amendment prejudiced her defense regarding that specific date. Because the conviction rests on the date originally charged, and the defense was prepared to address the evidence regarding that date, Ms. Kittle cannot establish the requisite prejudice under *Dudukovich*. Ms. Kittle's third assignment of error is overruled.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

{¶ 11} Based on the foregoing analysis, Ms. Kittle's assignments of error are overruled. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.
