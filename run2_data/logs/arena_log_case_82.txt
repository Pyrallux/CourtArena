This appeal focuses on the financial aspects of a marriage that lasted almost forty years. Background

The parties married in 1967. In 1982, husband started a business, Quality Reinforcement, Inc. (QRI). Its value was one of the major issues at trial. Husband filed his complaint for divorce on March 26, 2004, seeking a divorce and alleging that there were property rights and debt obligations to be adjudicated. Wife filed a counterclaim seeking a divorce and division of the property.

On April 17, 2006, husband made a written offer of judgment pursuant to Ark. R. Civ. P. 68 whereby wife would receive QRI and would assume all liabilities for the business. Wife did not respond to the offer and rejected it at trial.

On August 31, 2006, the parties entered into a written “Property Settlement and Separation Agreement” that resolved some of the outstanding issues between the parties. Wife was to receive two vehicles, one jet ski, a Kawasaki Mule and trailer, and two storage trailers. Husband was to pay the $500 debt remaining on wife’s vehicle. The parties also agreed to equally divide the funds contained in certain accounts and certificates of deposit, as well as the proceeds from the sale of a mobile home and the sale of forty acres under contract. The parties further agreed to list a residence and adjacent lot for sale. If that property was not sold within ninety days, it would be sold by the circuit court clerk. The agreement reserved the issues of alimony and the valuation of QRI. Additionally, husband would be entitled to a credit of $20,000 if the court determined the value of QRI was above $50,000.

On September 7, 2006, the court entered a divorce decree granting wife a divorce, approving and incorporating the parties’ written agreement, and reserving jurisdiction over the issues set out in that agreement.

He would not describe the difference as goodwill; rather, he characterized it as QRI’s earning capacity.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN VALUING QRI AT $425,500 BY IMPROPERLY INCLUDING ALLEGED "BUSINESS GOODWILL" THAT IS LEGALLY INDISTINGUISHABLE FROM PERSONAL GOODWILL

### A. The "Business Goodwill" Versus "Personal Goodwill" Distinction Is Not Applicable to Non-Professional Practices Under Arkansas Law

The plaintiff's reliance on the business goodwill/personal goodwill distinction is fundamentally flawed. Arkansas courts have consistently held that personal goodwill—meaning value attributable to the continued presence and personal reputation of the owner—is not subject to division in divorce proceedings. However, this distinction originated in cases involving *professional practices*, such as medical or dental practices, where the individual's license and personal reputation are the primary drivers of the business value. *See generally* McGough v. McGough, 2011 Ark. 160, 380 S.W.3d 874 (discussing goodwill in professional practices).

The plaintiff argues that QRI is merely a "commercial construction business" that wins contracts through competitive bidding, not through personal reputation. This argument proves too much. If QRI truly derives value solely from competitive bidding processes and operational systems, then the husband's presence should have minimal impact on value. ### B. The Evidence Overwhelmingly Establishes That QRI's Value Derives from the Husband's Personal Involvement

The testimony from all three experts establishes that QRI's value is directly tied to the husband's continued involvement:

1. **Gary Welch**, the court's own expert, acknowledged that he "did not consider the impact upon QRI if husband was unable to operate the business or personal goodwill attributable to husband." He further stated that "in order to sell the business, one would have to demand that husband remain with the business under an employment contract, with most of the purchase price being attributed to husband's employment contract."

This testimony is essentially uncontroverted. Eighty percent of the valuation attributed to wife's own expert stems from the husband's labor and presence. This is the very definition of personal goodwill—value that would not exist without the specific individual's continued involvement.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

In reviewing a circuit court's decision in a divorce case, this Court conducts a *de novo* review. However, we give due deference to the circuit court’s superior position to determine the credibility of witnesses and the weight to be given their testimony. *Cole v. Cole*, 89 Ark. App. 134, 201 S.W.3d 21 (2005). Findings of fact regarding the division of property are affirmed unless they are clearly erroneous. *Id.* The decision to award alimony rests within the sound discretion of the circuit court and will not be reversed absent an abuse of that discretion. *Id.*

**II. Valuation of Quality Reinforcement, Inc. (QRI)**

Appellant Randy Cummings argues that the circuit court erred in valuing QRI at $425,500, asserting that the value should have been based solely on net assets ($60,674) due to the presence of "personal goodwill." The defense relies on *McGough v. McGough*, 2011 Ark. 160, 380 S.W.3d 874, to distinguish between professional and commercial goodwill.

The circuit court found that QRI was a commercial construction business, not a professional practice, and therefore possessed business goodwill subject to division. This finding is supported by the testimony of the court's expert, Gary Welch, and the wife's expert, Rachel Kremer, both of whom utilized an income approach valuing the business as a going concern. While the husband's expert, Bruce Engstrom, testified that the business relied on the husband's personal goodwill, he acknowledged on cross-examination that there were no Arkansas cases allowing a reduction for personal goodwill in non-licensed commercial enterprises.

Given the absence of precedent supporting the husband's position and the substantial evidence that QRI operated as a going concern with earning capacity independent of the husband's specific licensure, the circuit court did not clearly err in rejecting the net asset valuation. The court’s decision to average the two valuations that treated QRI as a going concern ($421,000 and $430,000) was a reasonable exercise of its fact-finding discretion. Therefore, the valuation of QRI is affirmed.

**III. Alimony Award**

Appellant contends that the circuit court abused its discretion in awarding nominal alimony of $1 per year. Citing *Dildine v. Dildine*, 286 Ark. 458, 692 S.W.2d 238 (1985), the defense argues that alimony must be based on the wife's needs and the husband's ability to pay.

The record reflects that the wife will receive approximately $1,100,000 in assets. While she lacks marketable skills and claims health issues, the husband testified to his own significant health problems, including carpal tunnel syndrome and back issues, which impair his ability to work. Furthermore, the husband presented evidence that the business had generated only $3,000 to $4,000 in the three months preceding the hearing. Under these circumstances, and considering the substantial property division awarded to the wife, the circuit court did not abuse its discretion in awarding nominal alimony to preserve jurisdiction for future modification. The alimony award is affirmed.

**IV. Settlement Agreement Credit**

Appellant argues that the circuit court erred in failing to apply a $20,000 credit stipulated in the parties' Property Settlement and Separation Agreement. **FINAL RULING**

**I. Standard of Review**

On appeal, divorce cases are reviewed *de novo*. However, we give due deference to the circuit court’s superior position to determine the credibility of witnesses and the weight to be given their testimony. *Cole v. Cole*, 89 Ark. App. 134, 201 S.W.3d 21 (2005). With respect to the division of property in a divorce case, we review the circuit court’s findings of fact and affirm them unless they are clearly erroneous. *Id.* The decision whether to award alimony is a matter that lies within the circuit court’s sound discretion, and we will not reverse the decision to award alimony absent an abuse of that discretion. *Id.*

**II. Valuation of Quality Reinforcement, Inc. (QRI)**

Appellant Randy Cummings argues that the circuit court erred in valuing QRI at $425,500, asserting that the value should have been based solely on net assets ($60,674) due to the presence of "personal goodwill." The defense relies on *McGough v. McGough*, 2011 Ark. 160, 380 S.W.3d 874, to argue that the distinction between professional and commercial goodwill should not preclude a finding of personal goodwill in a commercial enterprise where the owner's presence is essential. The defense points to testimony that eighty percent of the wife’s expert’s valuation derived from the husband’s labor and that a hypothetical buyer would require the husband to remain under an employment contract.

The prosecution counters that Arkansas law restricts personal goodwill to professional practices and that QRI, a construction company, possesses business goodwill subject to division. The circuit court found that QRI was a commercial construction business, not a professional practice, and therefore possessed business goodwill. This finding is supported by the testimony of the court's expert, Gary Welch, and the wife's expert, Rachel Kremer, both of whom utilized an income approach valuing the business as a going concern. While the defense argues that *McGough* does not foreclose the existence of personal goodwill in commercial businesses, the defense's own expert, Bruce Engstrom, acknowledged on cross-examination that there were no Arkansas cases allowing a reduction for personal goodwill in non-licensed commercial enterprises.

The circuit court was free to accept the valuations of Welch and Kremer, which treated QRI as a going concern with earning capacity independent of the husband's specific licensure, and to reject Engstrom’s net asset valuation. The court’s determination that QRI possessed business goodwill rather than personal goodwill is not clearly erroneous. The court’s decision to average the two valuations ($421,000 and $430,000) was a reasonable exercise of its fact-finding discretion. Therefore, the valuation of QRI is affirmed.

**III. Alimony Award**

Appellant contends that the circuit court abused its discretion in awarding nominal alimony of $1 per year. The defense reiterates arguments regarding the wife's physical capabilities, citing surveillance evidence showing her lifting heavy items and running without difficulty, and contrasts this with the husband's documented health issues and the business's declining income. The prosecution supports the trial court's finding that the wife lacks marketable skills and suffers from spinal stenosis.

The record reflects that the wife will receive approximately $1,100,000 in assets. While she lacks marketable skills and claims health issues, the husband testified to his own significant health problems, including carpal tunnel syndrome and back issues, which impair his ability to work. Furthermore, the husband presented evidence that the business had generated only $3,000 to $4,000 in the three months preceding the hearing. Under these circumstances, and considering the substantial property division awarded to the wife, the circuit court did not abuse its discretion in awarding nominal alimony to preserve jurisdiction for future modification. The alimony award is affirmed.

**IV. Settlement Agreement Credit**

Appellant argues that the circuit court erred in failing to apply a $20,000 credit stipulated in the parties' Property Settlement and Separation Agreement. The agreement explicitly states: "Husband would be entitled to a credit of $20,000 if the court determined the value of QRI was above $50,000." The defense acknowledges and accepts this Court's determination that the circuit court erred in failing to apply this credit.

The circuit court determined the value of QRI to be $425,500. This finding triggers the contractual condition for the credit. Because the condition precedent was met by the court's own valuation, the circuit court erred as a matter of law by omitting the $20,000 credit from the final distribution. This point is granted, and the case is remanded for the entry of an order applying this credit against the amount owed to the wife.

**V. Award Regarding Gifts to Girlfriend**

Appellant challenges the award of $12,000 to the wife for gifts given to the husband's girlfriend, arguing the funds were separate and the expenditure was in the ordinary course of business. The defense further argues that the wife should not receive this award given that she withdrew $42,000 from the QRI account in 2004 for personal use. The circuit court found that the husband admitted to giving $12,000 in cash and $12,000 in personal property to his girlfriend during the pendency of the divorce. The husband's testimony revealed that he used company checks to pay personal debts and expenses, indicating that the funds used for the gifts were likely marital assets.

The transfer of substantial marital funds to a third party during litigation does not constitute a disposition in the ordinary course of business or a necessary expenditure. The defense's argument regarding the wife's prior withdrawals does not negate the husband's dissipation of marital assets during the litigation. The circuit court's finding that these were marital assets subject to division was not clearly erroneous. The award is affirmed.

**VI. Rule 68 Offer**

Appellant argues that the circuit court should have considered his Rule 68 offer of judgment as evidence of value, wherein he offered the wife QRI in exchange for assuming liabilities. The defense contends that the offer demonstrates the husband's assessment of the business's fair market value and highlights the speculative nature of the expert valuations. The prosecution correctly notes that Rule 68 of the Arkansas Rules of Civil Procedure governs cost-shifting for rejected settlement offers and does not dictate the methodology for valuing marital assets.

Rule 68 is a cost-shifting mechanism, not a valuation tool. While a party's offer may reflect their subjective view of the property's worth, the circuit court is required to determine the fair market value of the property based on objective evidence and expert testimony. The wife was entitled to reject the offer due to the risks associated with assuming the business's liabilities. The circuit court did not err in refusing to use the Rule 68 offer as a valuation benchmark or in giving it no weight. The circuit court's ruling on this issue is affirmed.

**Conclusion**

The circuit court's decree is affirmed in part regarding the valuation of QRI, the alimony award, the award for gifts to the girlfriend, and the refusal to utilize the Rule 68 offer as a valuation benchmark. The decree is reversed in part regarding the failure to apply the $20,000 credit from the settlement agreement, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling.
