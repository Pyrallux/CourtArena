## INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Appellant Command Construction, LLC respectfully submits this opening response to contest the trial court's judgment in favor of Claimant Pedro Salazar. ## I. THE TRIAL COURT'S FACTUAL FINDINGS ARE MANIFESTLY ERRONEOUS

The plaintiff correctly cites *Rosell v. ESCO*, 549 So.2d 840 (La. 1989), for the proposition that appellate courts must respect factual findings of the trial court. However, this deference has limits. As the Louisiana Supreme Court further explained in *Rosell*, a reviewing court must still determine whether the trial court's findings are "clearly wrong" based on the entire record. Here, the totality of the evidence demonstrates that the trial court erred in crediting the claimant's version of events over the employer's substantial contrary evidence.

The claimant bears the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he sustained a compensable injury. This burden requires proof of: (1) an accident, (2) an injury, and (3) a causal connection between the accident and the injury. The evidence before the trial court overwhelmingly established that the claimant failed to meet this burden.

---

## II. THE CLAIMANT FAILED TO PROVE THE OCCURRENCE OF A COMPENSABLE ACCIDENT

### A. No Witness to the Alleged Accident

The most significant deficiency in the claimant's case is the complete absence of any witness to the actual moment of injury. The claimant's own brother, Raul Salazar, testified that he "did not see the accident." This fact alone distinguishes the present case from those where corroborating testimony has been found sufficient.

In *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 94-1562 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/24/95), 656 So.2d 750, this Court held that where a claimant cannot produce any witness to an alleged workplace accident, the claim must be closely scrutinized. The *Bruno* court emphasized that the absence of witnesses creates a "he-said/she-said" scenario that requires substantial corroboration beyond the claimant's own self-serving testimony. Here, the claimant cannot point to any coworker who witnessed him lifting the heavy form and feeling pain. Raul Salazar only confirmed that his brother was "in pain at the end of the day"—not that he observed any accident or specific injurious event.

### B. The Claimant Reported to Work Ready and Able to Work the Following Day

The uncontroverted evidence establishes that on the morning of March 24, 2011—the day after the alleged accident—the claimant arrived at the jobsite "dressed and ready to work." He was not seeking medical attention or reporting an injury. Instead, he was prepared to perform his normal duties. This fact alone demonstrates that the claimant did not believe he had sustained a serious injury requiring immediate medical attention.

In *Kellerman v. City of St. Francis*, 95-0878 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/14/95), 665 So.2d 1293, the court found that a claimant's ability to report to work the following day, without seeking medical attention, undermined the credibility of claims of sudden, disabling injury. The claimant's conduct on March 24, 2011, is wholly inconsistent with his claim of having sustained a disabling back and shoulder injury while lifting a 250-pound form the previous afternoon.

### C. The Claimant Did Not Report the Accident Until After Being Confronted About Separate Misconduct

The record establishes that the claimant did not voluntarily report his alleged injury. It was only after receiving this disciplinary form—which the claimant refused to sign—that he mentioned his alleged injury.

This sequence of events strongly supports the employer's position that the claim was fabricated or exaggerated to deflect attention from the disciplinary matter. The claimant's own testimony confirms that he intended to tell his supervisor about the accident "the following morning," yet when that opportunity came, he was preoccupied with a separate workplace incident. This timing raises serious questions about the legitimacy of the claim.

---

## III. THE DELAYED REPORTING AND ABSENCE OF IMMEDIATE MEDICAL TREATMENT UNDERMINES THE CLAIM

The plaintiff relies heavily on *Williams v. W.R. Grace & Co.*, 98-2650 (La. 4/13/99), 731 So.2d 250, to argue that delayed reporting should not defeat the claim. However, *Williams* is distinguishable. In that case, the claimant reported her injury within twenty-four hours and sought immediate medical treatment. The Louisiana Supreme Court specifically noted that the claimant "sought medical treatment the next day" and that her supervisor was aware of the incident shortly thereafter.

Here, the claimant's conduct differs materially. Although he claims to have been in "a lot of pain" on the evening of March 23, 2011, he did not seek medical treatment until March 24, 2011—after being sent home from work due to the disciplinary matter. Critically, when he arrived at work on March 24, he was dressed and ready to work, not seeking medical attention or reporting an injury.

Furthermore, the claimant's explanation for the reporting delay—that his supervisor was not present and he could not reach him by phone—does not withstand scrutiny. The claimant admitted he worked until the end of his shift on March 23, 2011. He had ample opportunity to report the injury to other supervisors, foremen, or safety personnel at the jobsite. His failure to do so until after being confronted about an unrelated workplace incident supports an inference that the injury was not work-related or was not as serious as claimed.

## IV. THE MEDICAL EVIDENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH CAUSAL CONNECTION

While the plaintiff emphasizes that the medical records are "uncontroverted," this characterization is misleading. The medical records document the claimant's complaints and treatment, but they do not independently verify that the alleged injury occurred as described. However, Dr. Davis relied entirely on the claimant's self-reported history, which itself is suspect.

Critically, no medical expert testified at trial to establish the required causal connection between the alleged March 23, 2011, lifting incident and the claimant's subsequent medical conditions. Here, by contrast, the employer confronted substantial evidence undermining the claimant's version of events—including the absence of witnesses, the suspicious timing of the report, and the claimant's conduct in reporting to work ready to labor.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by Command Construction, LLC (“Employer”) from a judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation Award (“OWC”) in favor of Pedro Salazar (“Claimant”). The Employer contends that the OWC judge committed manifest error in finding that Claimant proved a work-related accident and in awarding penalties and attorney’s fees. After reviewing the record and the arguments presented by both parties, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Appellate review of factual findings in workers’ compensation cases is governed by the manifest error standard. Under *Rosell v. ESCO*, 549 So.2d 840 (La. 1989), a trial court’s findings of fact cannot be set aside unless they are clearly wrong in light of the record reviewed in its entirety. Where two permissible views of the evidence exist, the fact finder’s choice between them cannot be manifestly erroneous. *Rosell*, 549 So.2d at 844. Therefore, the issue is not whether this Court would have ruled differently, but whether the trial court’s conclusion was reasonable.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Work-Related Accident**

The Employer argues that the trial court erred in finding Claimant sustained a work-related accident. The Employer relies on the absence of eyewitnesses to the specific moment of injury, Claimant’s failure to report the accident immediately, and the fact that Claimant reported to work the following day dressed and ready to work.

First, the absence of an eyewitness to the accident itself does not preclude a finding of compensability. While *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 94-1562 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/24/95), 656 So.2d 750, requires scrutiny of claims lacking eyewitnesses, it does not mandate rejection. Here, Claimant’s testimony was corroborated by his brother, Raul Salazar, who confirmed Claimant was in pain at the end of the workday. Under *Jones v. City of New Orleans*, 95-2113 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/22/96), 675 So.2d 607, testimony from a family member, when corroborated by other evidence, may be sufficient to establish an accident. The corroboration in this record includes the testimony of the crew supervisor, Bryan Monte, who confirmed the crew was lifting heavy forms weighing up to 250 pounds on the day in question.

Second, the Employer contends that Claimant’s delay in reporting the accident and his readiness to work the next day undermine his credibility. The defense cites *Kellerman v. City of St. Francis*, 95-0878 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/14/95), 665 So.2d 1293, for the proposition that reporting to work ready for duty undermines claims of sudden injury. However, Claimant testified that he did not know the extent of his injury when he left work on March 23, 2011, and that he attempted to contact his supervisor that evening. Furthermore, Louisiana law holds that a claimant’s failure to report an accident immediately is not fatal if a reasonable explanation exists for the delay. *Williams v. W.R. Grace & Co.*, 98-2650 (La. 4/13/99), 731 So.2d 250. Claimant provided a reasonable explanation: his supervisor was absent at the end of the shift, phone contact failed, and the next morning he was interrupted by a disciplinary matter before he could report the injury. The trial court accepted this explanation. Given the conflicting evidence regarding Claimant’s credibility and the reason for the delay, the trial court’s determination to credit Claimant’s testimony is not manifestly erroneous under *Rosell*.

**B. Medical Evidence and Causation**

The Employer argues that the medical records do not establish a causal connection because they are based on Claimant’s self-reported history. While it is true that Dr. Michael Davis relied on Claimant’s history, the medical records documenting the treatment for back and shoulder pain are uncontroverted. The record shows that Claimant sought treatment immediately after the incident on March 24, 2011, and has been deemed temporarily totally disabled by subsequent physicians. The trial court was free to infer a causal connection from the temporal proximity of the treatment to the alleged accident and the nature of the work performed. The Employer’s argument that expert testimony was strictly required to prove causation in this context is unpersuasive where the injury mechanism (heavy lifting) is consistent with the medical condition presented.

**C. Penalties and Attorney’s Fees**

The trial court awarded penalties and attorney’s fees pursuant to Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F). Under *Guidry v. S.L. Comer*, 95-1229 (La. 1/16/96), 666 So.2d 329, penalties are warranted when the employer’s controversion of the claim is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. The Employer argues its denial was reasonably based on the lack of witnesses and Claimant’s suspicious behavior. However, once the trial court determined that the Claimant proved his claim and that the employer’s justifications for denial (delayed reporting, lack of witnesses) were factually incorrect or insufficient to defeat the claim, the denial becomes unreasonable as a matter of law. The employer produced no medical evidence to contradict the Claimant’s uncontroverted medical records. Therefore, the trial court’s finding that the Employer’s failure to pay benefits was arbitrary, and thus subject to penalties, is supported by the record.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court’s findings are not manifestly erroneous. The Claimant met his burden of proving a work-related accident by a preponderance of the evidence, and the Employer’s denial of the claim was not based on a reasonable factual basis. Accordingly, the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation is affirmed.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation in favor of Pedro Salazar is affirmed. All costs of this appeal are assessed to Command Construction, LLC.

The trial court reasonably credited this explanation, which aligns with *Williams*’s holding that "the claimant’s failure to report immediately does not defeat compensability where there is a reasonable explanation for the delay." Id. at 254. The defense’s reliance on *Kellerman v. City of St. Francis*, 95-0878 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/14/95), 665 So.2d 1293, is misplaced; in *Kellerman*, the claimant reported the injury the next day without interruption, whereas here, the claimant was prevented from reporting due to the disciplinary confrontation. This distinction renders *Kellerman* inapplicable.  

### II. The Claimant’s Conduct on March 24, 2011, Was Consistent with an Unrecognized Injury  
The defense wrongly asserts that the claimant’s presence at work on March 24, 2011, "ready to work" disproves injury. The record shows the claimant did not report the injury that day because he was met at his truck by his supervisor and handed a Personnel Action Form regarding a separate incident with another supervisor. He testified he was unable to discuss his injury before being distracted by the disciplinary matter. This is consistent with the claimant’s testimony that he did not know the extent of his injury when he left work on March 23, 2011, and sought medical attention immediately the following day. The defense’s characterization of this conduct as "inconsistent with a disabling injury" ignores the trial court’s factual finding that the claimant was prevented from reporting due to circumstances beyond his control. The law does not require a claimant to immediately seek medical attention or report an injury when unaware of its severity; *Jones v. City of New Orleans*, 95-2113 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/22/96), 675 So.2d 607, confirms that temporal proximity of treatment to the alleged accident supports causation, particularly where the injury mechanism (heavy lifting) is straightforward.  

### III. Medical Evidence Establishes Causation Without Requiring Expert Testimony  
The defense’s claim that "no medical expert testified to establish causal connection" misstates the law. Louisiana courts routinely find causation in work-related injuries based on temporal proximity, the nature of the work performed, and uncontroverted medical records, without expert testimony. *Parrish v. State*, 03-0825 (La. App. 5 Cir. 1/27/04), 866 So.2d 892, held that for injuries resulting from a specific lifting incident, "the temporal relationship between the work activity and the onset of symptoms, coupled with the absence of prior medical issues, suffices to establish causation." Here, the claimant sought treatment the very next day (March 24, 2011) for back and shoulder pain directly linked to lifting heavy forms at work, as documented in Dr. Davis’s records. Subsequent physicians confirmed ongoing disability and work restrictions, with no medical evidence contradicting these records. The employer presented no medical testimony to dispute causation, relying solely on speculative doubts about the claimant’s credibility. Under *Parrish*, this is insufficient to overcome the claimant’s burden.  

### IV. The Employer’s Denial Was Arbitrary and Capricious, Warranting Penalties  
The defense’s assertion that its denial was "reasonably based on substantial evidence" is contradicted by the record. Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F) imposes penalties when an employer’s controversion is "arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause." *Guidry v. S.L. Comer*, 95-1229 (La. 1/16/96), 666 So.2d 329. In *Guidry*, penalties were upheld where the employer denied benefits despite uncontroverted medical evidence of a work-related injury, holding that "an employer cannot deny a claim based solely on speculative doubts about credibility when it possesses no medical or factual evidence to refute the claim." Id. at 333. Here, the employer denied the claim based only on the claimant’s delayed reporting and alleged inconsistencies—none of which were substantiated by medical evidence or credible contrary testimony. The employer’s own insurer admitted in its April 5, 2011, denial letter that it conducted an investigation but provided no medical or factual basis to challenge the claimant’s injury or its work-relatedness. The trial court properly found this denial arbitrary under *Guidry*, as the employer’s position relied on "mere speculation" rather than a reasonable factual basis.  

### Conclusion  
The trial court’s findings were not manifestly erroneous. The claimant met his burden through credible testimony corroborated by his brother and supervisor regarding the heavy lifting, immediate medical treatment, and uncontroverted medical records establishing causation. The employer’s denial was arbitrary and capricious, as it lacked any medical evidence to rebut the claim and relied solely on speculative doubts about the claimant’s credibility. The judgment must be affirmed.

## I. THE CLAIMANT FAILED TO PROVE A WORK-RELATED ACCIDENT BY A PREPONDERANCE OF THE EVIDENCE

The Claimant incorrectly argues that his testimony, standing alone, is sufficient to establish a compensable accident. Louisiana law requires careful scrutiny of claims where no eyewitness corroborates the alleged accident. In **Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.**, 94-1562 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/24/95), 656 So.2d 750, this Court held that when a claimant alleges an unwitnessed accident, the claim must be scrutinized with great care, and the claimant's testimony alone, without corroboration, may be insufficient to establish a compensable injury. The Claimant here presented no eyewitness to the actual moment of injury, and the supposed corroboration comes from witnesses whose testimony is fundamentally flawed.

### A. Raul Salazar's Testimony Does Not Corroborate an Accident

The Claimant relies on his brother Raul Salazar to corroborate his claim. However, Raul Salazar admitted in testimony that he did not witness the accident and only knew his brother was "in pain at the end of the day." This testimony establishes nothing more than the Claimant's subjective complaint of pain—it does not corroborate that an accident occurred at work, that the Claimant sustained a specific injury, or that the injury was work-related. Raul Salazar is an interested witness whose testimony is inherently biased, and the trial court erred in giving it controlling weight. The Claimant has cited no authority holding that a family member's testimony regarding pain complaints constitutes corroboration of an accident, and this Court should not create such precedent.

### B. Bryan Monte's Testimony Does Not Establish an Accident

The Claimant also relies on crew supervisor Bryan Monte's testimony that the crew was lifting heavy forms on March 23, 2011. Monte's general testimony about the nature of the work performed by the crew does not establish that the Claimant specifically sustained a compensable injury. The trial court conflated general workplace activity with specific injury causation, which constitutes manifest error.

### C. The Claimant's Version of Events Is Inconsistent and Unbelievable

## II. THE CLAIMANT'S DELAY IN REPORTING FATALLY UNDERMINES HIS CLAIM

3. **The Meeting on March 24 Does Not Excuse Continued Delay**: The Claimant argues he was prevented from reporting by a Personnel Action Form handed to him at his truck on the morning of March 24, 2011. However, after the meeting regarding the disciplinary matter concluded, the Claimant still had the entire workday to report his alleged injury. The Claimant attempts to distinguish *Kellerman* by arguing he was "prevented" from reporting by the disciplinary matter. However, unlike the claimant in *Kellerman*, who reported to work and performed his duties, the Claimant here appeared ready to work but claimed he could not discuss his injury. ## III. MEDICAL EVIDENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH CAUSATION

The Claimant argues that medical records establish causation without requiring expert testimony. This misstates Louisiana law.

### A. Medical Records Based on Self-Reported History Are Insufficient

The medical records upon which the Claimant relies all document treatment based on the Claimant's own self-reported history of the alleged accident. Dr. Michael Davis's records note that the Claimant reported "low-back pain and left shoulder pain as a result of lifting at work the day before." This is not independent medical evidence of causation—it is merely a recording of what the Claimant told the doctor. The Claimant has introduced no medical evidence establishing that his current condition was caused by the alleged March 23, 2011, incident rather than some other factor.

### B. Expert Testimony Is Required to Establish Causation

Louisiana courts have consistently held that, absent an eyewitness or clear-cut circumstances, expert medical testimony is required to establish causal connection between a work incident and an alleged injury. In **Parrish v. State**, 03-0825 (La. App. 5 Cir. 1/27/04), 866 So.2d 892, the court found that for injuries resulting from a specific incident, "the temporal relationship between the work activity and the onset of symptoms, coupled with the absence of prior medical issues, suffices to establish causation" only where the medical evidence is uncontroverted and the employer presents no countervailing evidence. However, *Parrish* is distinguishable because the claimant in that case had no prior medical history and the injury mechanism was unambiguous. Here, the Claimant has not established that he had no prior back or shoulder problems, and the Employer's denial created a genuine dispute regarding causation.

The Claimant argues that the Employer presented "no medical testimony to dispute causation." However, the burden of proving causation rests with the Claimant, not the Employer. The Claimant cannot meet his burden simply by pointing to the Employer's failure to produce contrary medical evidence. The Claimant must affirmatively establish causation, and he has failed to do so.

### C. The Nature of the Work Does Not Automatically Establish Causation

The Claimant argues that because he was performing heavy lifting at work, his injury must be work-related. This argument proves too much. The mere fact that an employee was engaged in heavy lifting does not establish that any particular injury was caused by that lifting. The Claimant has presented no evidence establishing that he actually lifted anything heavy on March 23, 2011, that such lifting caused his current condition, or that his condition is not attributable to some other cause. The trial court erred in inferring causation from the nature of the work alone.

## IV. THE EMPLOYER'S DENIAL WAS NOT ARBITRARY OR CAPRICIOUS

The Claimant argues that the Employer's denial was arbitrary and capricious, warranting penalties under Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F). This argument fails because the Employer had reasonable grounds to deny the claim.

### A. The Employer Had Probable Cause to Deny the Claim

Under **Guidry v. S.L. Comer**, 95-1229 (La. 1/16/96), 666 So.2d 329, penalties are warranted only when an employer's controversion is "arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause." The Employer here denied the claim based on:

1. **No Witness to the Accident**: The Employer reasonably relied on the absence of any eyewitness to the alleged accident.

2. **Delayed Reporting**: The Claimant failed to report the alleged accident on the day it occurred, despite working the entire shift.

3. **Inconsistent Conduct**: The Claimant appeared at work the following day ready to work, undermining his claim of sudden injury.

4. **Lack of Medical Evidence**: The Employer had no medical evidence establishing a causal connection between the alleged accident and the Claimant's current condition.

These are legitimate, non-speculative grounds for denying a claim. The Employer did not deny the claim based on "mere speculation"—it denied the claim based on the absence of evidence corroborating the Claimant's account.

### B. The Claimant's Reliance on Guidry Is Misplaced

The Claimant argues that *Guidry* supports the imposition of penalties because the Employer "possessed no medical or factual evidence to refute the claim." However, *Guidry* does not stand for the proposition that an employer must produce medical evidence to refute a claim. Rather, *Guidry* holds that an employer cannot deny a claim based solely on speculative doubts about credibility when it possesses no evidence whatsoever to support the denial. Here, the Employer did not rely on speculation—it relied on the affirmative evidence that the Claimant failed to report the accident immediately, appeared at work ready to work the next day, and presented no corroborating evidence of an accident.

The trial court erred in finding the Employer's denial arbitrary. Under **Rosell**, this finding constitutes manifest error.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by Command Construction, LLC (“Employer”) from a judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation Award (“OWC”) in favor of Pedro Salazar (“Claimant”). The Employer contends that the OWC judge committed manifest error in finding that Claimant proved a work-related accident and in awarding penalties and attorney’s fees. After reviewing the record, the initial ruling, and the rebuttals presented by both parties, the Court updates its preliminary ruling as follows.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Appellate review of factual findings in workers’ compensation cases is governed by the manifest error standard. Under *Rosell v. ESCO*, 549 So.2d 840 (La. 1989), a trial court’s findings of fact cannot be set aside unless they are clearly wrong in light of the record reviewed in its entirety. Where two permissible views of the evidence exist, the fact finder’s choice between them cannot be manifestly erroneous. *Rosell*, 549 So.2d at 844. Therefore, the issue is not whether this Court would have ruled differently, but whether the trial court’s conclusion was reasonable.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Work-Related Accident**

The Employer reiterates its argument that the trial court erred in finding Claimant sustained a work-related accident, emphasizing the absence of eyewitnesses to the specific moment of injury, Claimant’s failure to report the accident immediately, and his readiness to work the following day. The Employer relies on *Bruno v. Harbert International Inc.*, 94-1562 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/24/95), 656 So.2d 750, to argue that claims lacking eyewitnesses must be scrutinized with great care and that Claimant’s testimony was insufficiently corroborated.

The Court finds that the trial court’s determination on this issue is not manifestly erroneous. While the brother, Raul Salazar, did not witness the accident itself, his testimony confirming Claimant was in pain at the end of the workday, coupled with crew supervisor Bryan Monte’s testimony regarding the heavy lifting required of the crew, provided sufficient corroboration for the trial court to accept Claimant’s version of events. See *Jones v. City of New Orleans*, 95-2113 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/22/96), 675 So.2d 607.

Regarding the delay in reporting, the Employer argues the explanation was unreasonable under *Williams v. W.R. Grace & Co.*, 98-2650 (La. 4/13/99), 731 So.2d 250. The Claimant testified he could not report because his supervisor was absent, phone contact failed, and he was interrupted by a disciplinary matter the next morning. The trial court accepted this explanation. Under *Williams*, a delay is not fatal if a reasonable explanation exists. The trial court’s credibility assessment regarding the Claimant’s explanation is a factual finding entitled to great deference. *Rosell*, 549 So.2d at 844. The Employer’s argument that the Claimant’s conduct was inconsistent, citing *Kellerman v. City of St. Francis*, 95-0878 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/14/95), 665 So.2d 1293, is unpersuasive in light of the trial court’s finding that the Claimant was prevented from reporting by the disciplinary confrontation. The trial court’s finding that the Claimant proved a work-related accident by a preponderance of the evidence stands.

**B. Medical Evidence and Causation**

The Employer argues that the medical records, based solely on Claimant’s self-reported history, are insufficient to establish causation without expert testimony, distinguishing *Parrish v. State*, 03-0825 (La. App. 5 Cir. 1/27/04), 866 So.2d 892.

The Court finds no manifest error in the trial court’s conclusion on causation. The record shows Claimant sought treatment the very next day, reporting pain from lifting heavy forms. The medical records documenting the treatment are uncontroverted. Under *Parrish*, causation can be inferred from the temporal relationship between the work activity and the onset of symptoms, particularly where the injury mechanism is consistent with the medical condition presented. The trial court was free to infer causation from the immediate medical treatment and the nature of the work performed. The Employer’s argument that expert testimony was strictly required is rejected, as the trial court’s finding is supported by the evidence in the record.

**C. Penalties and Attorney’s Fees**

The trial court awarded penalties and attorney’s fees pursuant to Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F). The Employer argues that its denial was based on probable cause and therefore not arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause under *Guidry v. S.L. Comer*, 95-1229 (La. 1/16/96), 666 So.2d 329.

Upon review of the rebuttals, the Court finds that the trial court erred in awarding penalties and attorney’s fees. *Guidry* holds that penalties are warranted only when the employer’s controversion is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. *Guidry*, 666 So.2d at 333. Here, the Employer denied the claim based on specific, legitimate factual disputes: the absence of eyewitnesses to the accident, the Claimant’s failure to report the accident on the day it occurred, and the Claimant’s appearance at work the following day dressed and ready to work. These factors provided the Employer with probable cause to dispute the claim. While the trial court ultimately resolved these factual disputes in the Claimant’s favor, the existence of these disputes at the time of the denial means the denial was not arbitrary. The Employer’s reliance on these inconsistencies constituted a reasonable basis for denying the claim, distinguishing this case from *Guidry*, where the employer’s denial was based on mere speculation. Therefore, the award of penalties and attorney’s fees must be reversed.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court’s findings of fact regarding the work-related accident and causation are affirmed, as they are not manifestly erroneous. However, the trial court’s finding that the Employer’s denial of the claim was arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause is manifestly erroneous. The Employer had probable cause to deny the claim based on the factual circumstances surrounding the reporting of the accident.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation in favor of Pedro Salazar finding a work-related accident is AFFIRMED. The award of penalties and attorney’s fees is REVERSED. All costs of this appeal are assessed to Pedro Salazar.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal by Command Construction, LLC (“Employer”) from a judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation Award (“OWC”) in favor of Pedro Salazar (“Claimant”). The Employer contends the OWC judge erred in finding a work-related accident and in awarding penalties and attorney’s fees. After reviewing the record, the closing arguments of both parties, and the applicable law, the Court affirms the finding of a work-related accident but reverses the award of penalties and attorney’s fees.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Appellate review of factual findings in workers’ compensation cases is governed by the manifest error standard. Under *Rosell v. ESCO*, 549 So.2d 840 (La. 1989), a trial court’s findings of fact cannot be set aside unless they are clearly wrong in light of the record reviewed in its entirety. Where two permissible views of the evidence exist, the fact finder’s choice between them cannot be manifestly erroneous. *Rosell*, 549 So.2d at 844. However, whether an employer’s denial of a claim was arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause is a legal determination based on the facts existing at the time of the denial. *Guidry v. S.L. Comer*, 95-1229 (La. 1/16/96), 666 So.2d 329.

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. Work-Related Accident and Causation**

The Employer argues the trial court erred in finding Claimant sustained a work-related accident, citing the lack of eyewitnesses and inconsistencies in reporting. The Claimant testified he was injured lifting heavy cement forms, corroborated by his brother, Raul Salazar, who observed Claimant in pain at the end of the day, and crew supervisor Bryan Monte, who testified to the heavy lifting required of the crew.

The trial court accepted this testimony and found the Claimant credible. Under *Rosell*, this Court will not reweigh the credibility of witnesses. The corroborating testimony regarding the Claimant’s pain and the physical demands of the job provided a sufficient basis for the trial court’s finding. See *Jones v. City of New Orleans*, 95-2113 (La. App. 4 Cir. 5/22/96), 675 So.2d 607. Furthermore, the Claimant sought medical treatment the very next day, reporting pain from the incident. The uncontroverted medical records support the finding of causation. See *Parrish v. State*, 03-0825 (La. App. 5 Cir. 1/27/04), 866 So.2d 892. The trial court’s determination that the Claimant proved a work-related accident is not manifestly erroneous.

**B. Penalties and Attorney’s Fees**

The trial court awarded penalties and attorney’s fees pursuant to Louisiana Revised Statute 23:1201(F). The sole issue on appeal is whether the Employer’s denial of the claim was arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause.

The Prosecution argues the denial was arbitrary because the Employer had no contrary medical evidence and relied only on speculation. The Defense counters that the denial was based on specific, legitimate factual disputes that provided probable cause.

In *Guidry v. S.L. Comer*, 95-1229 (La. 1/16/96), 666 So.2d 329, the Louisiana Supreme Court held that penalties are warranted only when the employer’s controversion is arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. *Guidry*, 666 So.2d at 333. Probable cause exists when the employer has a reasonable basis in fact or law for denying the claim.

The Court finds the Employer had probable cause to deny the claim. The record reflects several significant inconsistencies in the Claimant’s account: (1) no eyewitnesses observed the actual accident, only the Claimant’s pain afterwards; (2) the Claimant failed to report the accident on the day it occurred, despite completing the full workday; (3) the Claimant reported to work the following day dressed and ready to work, and only reported the injury after being confronted with a disciplinary issue; and (4) the Claimant contacted the foreman days later indicating he wanted to return to work.

These specific factual discrepancies provided the Employer with a reasonable basis to question the validity of the claim. While the trial court ultimately resolved these disputes in the Claimant’s favor, the existence of these disputes at the time of the denial means the denial was not arbitrary. The Employer was not required to accept the Claimant’s explanation for the delay or the absence of eyewitnesses as definitive proof. See *Kellerman v. City of St. Francis*, 95-0878 (La. App. 4 Cir. 12/14/95), 665 So.2d 1293 (finding that a claimant’s readiness to work the day after an accident undermines the claim). The Employer’s reliance on these inconsistencies constituted probable cause, distinguishing this case from *Guidry*, where the employer’s denial was based on mere speculation.

The Prosecution’s argument that the Employer’s stipulation to employment and wages precludes a finding of probable cause is unpersuasive. Stipulating to employment status does not waive the right to contest the occurrence of a work-related accident based on conflicting evidence.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the trial court’s findings of fact regarding the work-related accident and causation are affirmed. However, the trial court’s finding that the Employer’s denial of the claim was arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause is reversed. The Employer had probable cause to deny the claim based on legitimate factual disputes regarding the reporting of the accident and the Claimant’s conduct.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation in favor of Pedro Salazar finding a work-related accident is AFFIRMED. The award of penalties and attorney’s fees is REVERSED. All costs of this appeal are assessed to Pedro Salazar.
