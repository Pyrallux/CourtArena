FACTS:
Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. (“Evergreen”) appeals the award of attorney’s fees to Djuric Trucking, Inc. (“Djuric”). Evergreén contends that the award is barred by res judicata and that Djuric waived its claim to attorney’s fees. Because we conclude that the award is not barred by res judicata and Djuric has not waived its claim, we affirm.

Facts and Procedural History

This is the second appeal in a dispute between two freight companies. The first chapter in this case was set forth by this Court in August 2012:

Evergreen is the owner of certain freight storage containers, and Djuric is a freight transport company. Both are parties to the Uniform Intermodal Interchange and Facilities Access Agreement (“UIIA”). Djuric has on occasion been hired by Hub City Terminals, Inc. (“Hub”) to transport freight. As relevant here, in 2010 and 2011, Hub hired Djuric to transport freight one-way from Chicago to Indianapolis on behalf of Target Corporation (“Target”). Pursuant to Hub’s request, Djuric transported the freight to Indianapolis using Evergreen’s containers and received an authorized signature from Target. Target, in turn, agreed with Djuric to be responsible for any and all charges and/or fees assessed for the containers.
On June 9, 2011, Evergreen filed a complaint on account against Djuric. According to Evergreen, Djuric was responsible for the failure to return the containers owned by Evergreen. Evergreen alleged damages in excess of $34,000 for per diem charges.
Between August and October 2011, Evergreen and Djuric filed cross motions for summary judgment. In support of its motion, Evergreen designated the UIIA, on which it had based its claim. The UIIA defines “[p]er [djiem” charges as “[cjharges to be paid when intermo-dal [ejquipment is not returned by the end of the allowable free time to its origin or another location, as previously agreed to by the Parties.” Further, a “[pjrovider may permit some period of *339uncompensated use and thereafter impose use charges,” but any such charges must be “set forth in [an] Addendum.” The UIIA defines “Addendum/Addenda” as the “[pjroviders’ schedule of economic and commercial terms not appropriate for inclusion in the [UIIA] and other terms and conditions of [equipment use,” that is, a separate collateral agreement. And the UIIA requires that any agreement, including an addendum, be in writing and signed by the parties. Along with the UIIA, Evergreen designated the affidavit of Ted Dost, one of its managers, who stated that the UIIA required Djuric to pay per diem charges to Evergreen. But Evergreen did not designate a separate written agreement with Djuric, and, in support of its own motion for summary judgment, Djuric designated the affidavit of Jim Djuric, president of Djuric. According to Jim’s affidavit, Djuric had no separate agreements with Evergreen. And, when pressed by the trial court at the summary judgment hearing, Evergreen’s counsel was unable to confirm that any agreement other than the UIIA existed between Evergreen and Djuric. In any event, no separate written agreement was designated.
On November 16, the trial court entered summary judgment for Djuric on the basis that Evergreen’s “designated evidence fails to establish any agreement by which [Djuric] is liable for any per diem [charges]. [Djuric’s] designated evidence establishes that no such agreement exists.” At the same time, the court denied Evergreen’s motion for summary judgment.
Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. v. Djuric Trucking, Inc., No. 45A03-1112-CC-575, 2012 WL 3129130 (Ind.Ct.App. Aug. 2, 2012) (citations omitted). In its summary-judgment motion, Djuric had argued that Evergreen’s breach-of-contract claim was frivolous, and sought attorney’s fees pursuant to Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1.1 The trial court denied Djuric’s request for attorney’s fees under Section 34-52-1-1. This Court affirmed the trial court’s grant of summary judgment.2 The opinion was certified on September 3, 2012.

Two weeks later, Djuric filed a request for attorney’s fees pursuant to the UIIA. In an action for breach or enforcement of the UIIA, the UIIA entitles the prevailing party to recover reasonable attorney’s fees. See Appellee’s App. p. 19. After a hearing, the trial court granted Djuric’s motion and awarded it approximately $12,000 in attorney’s fees.

Evergreen now appeals.

Today, we stand before you to defend a straightforward and legally sound award of attorney’s fees to Djuric Trucking, Inc. ### I. Res Judicata Does Not Bar Djuric’s Fee Claim Because the Prior Judgment Never Addressed It  
Res judicata requires three elements: identity of parties, identity of causes of action, and a final judgment on the merits. Evergreen fails on all counts. The prior proceeding resolved only the *underlying liability for per diem charges*—a discrete dispute about whether Djuric owed Evergreen money for container usage. That judgment was narrow, final, and limited to the merits of Evergreen’s breach-of-contract claim for per diem fees. Crucially, *it did not address attorney’s fees at all*.  

Djuric’s request for fees under the UIIA is a separate and distinct claim from the statutory fee request it initially made. The UIIA’s fee-shifting provision is contractual: it provides that in any action for breach or enforcement of the agreement, the prevailing party is entitled to recover reasonable attorney’s fees. This right arises *only upon prevailing in the underlying action*. Here, the prior judgment established Djuric as the prevailing party on Evergreen’s per diem claim—but that judgment did not decide whether Djuric was entitled to fees under the UIIA. The trial court denied Djuric’s earlier statutory fee request, but that denial was based solely on the merits of *that specific statutory claim*, not the contractual right under the UIIA.  

Res judicata does not bar claims that were not litigated or decided in the prior proceeding. The UIIA fee claim was not part of the first action’s adjudication; it was a separate entitlement that became ripe only after Djuric prevailed. To hold otherwise would force parties to litigate fee claims prematurely or risk forfeiting contractual rights—a result contrary to fundamental fairness. Evergreen’s attempt to stretch res judicata this far is an effort to shield itself from the consequences of its own failed lawsuit.  

### II. Djuric Did Not Waive Its Right to UIIA Fees  
Evergreen’s waiver argument is equally untenable. Waiver requires a clear, intentional relinquishment of a known right. Djuric did no such thing. It sought attorney’s fees under a statutory provision during the summary judgment phase—not because it abandoned the UIIA’s fee right, but because it reasonably pursued the remedy it believed was available at the time. The UIIA’s fee-shifting clause is independent of statutory fee provisions; it is a contractual right that exists separately and survives even if a party initially seeks relief under a different statute.  

Djuric acted promptly and appropriately once it became clear that its statutory fee request was denied. Two weeks after the first appeal concluded, Djuric filed a motion for fees under the UIIA—exactly as the agreement requires. This timing was not a delay; it was the logical next step. The UIIA does not impose a deadline for asserting fee rights, nor does it require parties to raise them in a single motion. Djuric’s choice to pursue statutory fees first does not constitute a waiver of its contractual right. To accept Evergreen’s position would mean that a party could forfeit a clear contractual remedy simply by exercising its right to seek relief under another legal theory—a proposition with no support in law.  

### III. The UIIA Itself Mandates This Award  
The UIIA is clear: in an action for breach or enforcement of the agreement, the prevailing party is entitled to fees. Evergreen sued Djuric for per diem charges under the UIIA framework. That lawsuit was an action for breach of the UIIA. Djuric prevailed. The agreement’s plain language compels this award. Evergreen’s arguments attempt to rewrite the UIIA’s terms, but courts do not rewrite contracts—they enforce them as written.  

### Conclusion  
This Court should affirm the award without hesitation. Res judicata does not apply because the prior judgment never touched on the UIIA’s fee provision. Waiver did not occur because Djuric’s initial statutory fee request was a strategic choice, not a surrender of its contractual rights. The UIIA is unambiguous, and Djuric is the prevailing party. Evergreen’s appeal is a transparent attempt to avoid paying for a lawsuit it should never have filed. We respectfully urge you to uphold the trial court’s decision and send a clear message that contractual fee provisions are enforced as written.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE FOR APPELLANT EVERGREEN SHIPPING AGENCY CORP.

## INTRODUCTION

Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. respectfully submits this opening brief in defense of the trial court's denial of attorney fees to Djuric Trucking, Inc., and in support of reversal of the award. The plaintiff-appellee's opening argument misconstrues the nature of res judicata, ignores the procedural history of this litigation, and incorrectly characterizes Djuric's conduct as something other than a waiver of its contractual fee rights. This Court should reverse the award of attorney fees because: (1) the fee claim is barred by res judicata as it could have been raised in the prior proceeding, and (2) Djuric waived its right to fees under the UIIA by failing to assert that contractual basis in the first action.

## I. RES JUDICATA BARS DJURIC'S FEE CLAIM

### A. The Elements of Res Judicata

Under Indiana law, res judicata bars a subsequent action when there is: (1) identity of parties, (2) identity of causes of action, and (3) a final judgment on the merits. *Town of Plainfield v. Town of Avon*, 996 N.E.2d 904, 910 (Ind. 2013). The doctrine serves the dual purposes of preventing repetitive litigation and protecting parties from the burden of重新 litigating claims that could have been adjudicated in a prior proceeding. *B & B Excavating & Trucking, LLC v. Town of Brownsburg*, 22 N.E.3d 695, 701 (Ind. Ct. App. 2014).

### B. Identity of Parties and Cause of Action Exists

The identity of parties is undisputed—both proceedings involve Evergreen and Djuric. More critically, the identity of causes of action is established because the fee claim arises from the same transaction and occurrence as the underlying dispute. Djuric's current fee claim is not a separate, independent cause of action; it is contingent upon and arises from the same breach-of-contract action that was litigated in 2011-2012. The UIIA fee provision states that in "any action for breach or enforcement of the [UIIA], the prevailing party is entitled to recover reasonable attorney fees." This provision creates a single, unified claim that attaches to the underlying breach action—it does not exist separately from that dispute.

In *Brown v. City of Michigan City*, 935 N.E.2d 1248 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010), this Court held that a fee claim arising from an underlying judgment is part of the same cause of action for res judicata purposes when it could have been litigated concurrently. Djuric's UIIA fee claim could have been litigated in the first proceeding. The UIIA was the very contract upon which Evergreen based its per diem claim, and its fee-shifting provision was part of that same agreement. Djuric had full knowledge of the UIIA's terms during the first litigation, as evidenced by its designation of the agreement in support of its own summary judgment motion.

### C. The Fee Claim Could Have Been Raised in the First Proceeding

The critical flaw in Djuric's argument is its assertion that the fee claim "became ripe only after Djuric prevailed." This mischaracterizes the nature of contractual fee-shifting provisions. Under the UIIA, the right to fees vests upon the entry of judgment in the underlying action—not upon the conclusion of appellate review. Djuric knew, at the moment summary judgment was entered in its favor on November 16, 2011, that it had prevailed and that it was the prevailing party under the UIIA. At that moment, its right to fees under the UIIA became enforceable.

Djuric's own actions in the first proceeding confirm this. In its summary judgment motion, Djuric specifically argued that Evergreen's breach-of-contract claim was frivolous and sought attorney fees pursuant to Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1. This demonstrates that Djuric understood it had a viable fee claim and chose to pursue it—but pursued it under the wrong legal theory. Djuric cannot now relitigate a fee claim that it could have asserted correctly in the first proceeding.

In *Gertz v. Quicksilver Mining Co.*, 14 N.E.3d 747 (Ind. Ct. App. 2014), this Court held that res judicata bars subsequent claims that arise from the same transaction where the claimant had the opportunity to raise them in the first action. Djuric had every opportunity to assert its UIIA fee claim in the first proceeding. It chose not to do so. The doctrine of res judicata exists precisely to prevent parties from splitting their claims and returning to court later when they regret their earlier strategic choices.

### D. The Prior Judgment Was On the Merits

The first appeal resulted in a final judgment on the merits—the trial court granted summary judgment for Djuric, and this Court affirmed. That judgment was dispositive of Evergreen's claim for per diem charges. Because the fee claim is derivative of and dependent upon that underlying judgment, it cannot be considered separate from the merits. The fee claim is the fruit of the prior litigation; it cannot be severed from the tree that produced it.

In *Austrian v. L.S. & B, Inc.*, 888 N.E.2d 759 (Ind. Ct. App. 2008), this Court explained that res judicata applies not only to claims actually litigated but also to claims that could have been litigated in the prior proceeding. Djuric's UIIA fee claim fits squarely within this principle.

## II. DJURIC WAIVED ITS RIGHT TO UIIA FEES

### A. Waiver Principles Under Indiana Law

Waiver is the voluntary and intentional relinquishment of a known right. *Blazer v. New Albany-Floyd County Consol. Sch. Corp.*, 840 N.E.2d 895, 901 (Ind. Ct. App. 2006). A party may waive a right either expressly or by conduct that demonstrates an intent to relinquish that right. *Id.* Importantly, waiver may be inferred from a party's conduct, even without an explicit statement. *Indiana Michigan Power Co. v. United States*, 422 F.3d 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2005).

### B. Djuric's Conduct Demonstrates Waiver

Djuric's conduct in the first proceeding unequivocally demonstrates waiver of its UIIA fee rights. During the summary judgment phase, Djuric specifically moved for attorney fees under Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1. It did not mention the UIIA. It did not assert a contractual basis for fees. It did not designate the UIIA's fee-shifting provision as grounds for recovery. This was not an oversight—it was a deliberate strategic choice.

Djuric now argues that it "reasonably pursued the remedy it believed was available at the time" and that its "choice to pursue statutory fees first does not constitute a waiver." This argument fails for several reasons.

First, Djuric knew about the UIIA. It designated the UIIA in support of its own summary judgment motion. The agreement was before the trial court. Djuric cannot claim ignorance of its contractual rights.

Second, Djuric had every incentive to assert the UIIA fee claim in the first proceeding. The UIIA provides for recovery of "reasonable attorney fees"—language that typically yields higher fees than the statutory provision, which requires the claim to be frivolous or brought in bad faith. By pursuing only the statutory claim, Djuric made a strategic calculation to seek fees under a narrower standard. Having lost on that approach, it cannot now return to the contractual provision that it deliberately bypassed.

In *Kikkert v. Krumm*, 711 N.E.2d 562 (Ind. Ct. App. 1999), this Court held that a party's failure to assert a claim or defense in the first proceeding, when it had the opportunity to do so, constitutes waiver. The rationale is straightforward: parties should not be permitted to hold claims in reserve, litigation one theory first and then falling back on another if the initial approach fails. Such a approach would encourage piecemeal litigation and undermine the finality of judgments.

Third, Djuric's argument that it "acted promptly" by filing for UIIA fees "two weeks after the first appeal concluded" ignores the fundamental principle that appellate proceedings do not revive claims that were waived or could have been raised below. The first appeal was from the trial court's judgment. The fee claim could have been raised in that proceeding. Its absence from the first proceeding constitutes waiver.

### C. The UIIA Does Not Preserve Djuric's Claim

Djuric argues that the UIIA "does not impose a deadline for asserting fee rights." This is true but irrelevant. The UIIA's fee-shifting provision is a contractual right, and like all contractual rights, it can be waived. Djuric waived that right by failing to assert it in the first proceeding. The absence of a contractual deadline does not create a right where none existed—it merely confirms that the right must be asserted within the applicable procedural rules governing the underlying action.

In *Parker v. City of Indianapolis*, 865 N.E.2d 604 (Ind. Ct. App. 2007), this Court held that contractual fee provisions must be asserted in accordance with applicable procedural rules, and failure to do so results in waiver. Djuric's failure to assert the UIIA fee provision in the first proceeding violated those rules.

## III. DJURIC'S CURRENT ARGUMENT CONTRADICTS ITS PRIOR POSITION

In the first proceeding, Djuric argued that Evergreen's claim was "frivolous" and sought fees under the statute. By definition, a frivolous claim is one that has no basis in law or fact. If Djuric truly believed Evergreen's claim was frivolous—which it had to believe to obtain statutory fees—it cannot now argue that the claim had sufficient merit to trigger the UIIA's fee-shifting provision. Djuric cannot have it both ways: it cannot contend that Evergreen's claim was frivolous (to obtain statutory fees) while simultaneously contending that the claim was sufficiently meritorious to trigger contractual fees.

This inconsistency alone demonstrates that Djuric's current fee claim is an afterthought, an attempt to recover fees after failing to do so in the first proceeding. Res judicata and waiver principles exist precisely to prevent this kind of opportunistic behavior.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Evergreen respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's award of attorney fees to Djuric. The fee claim is barred by res judicata because it arises from the same transaction and occurrence as the first action, could have been raised in that proceeding, and there was a final judgment on the merits. Alternatively, Djuric waived its right to UIIA fees by failing to assert that contractual basis in the first proceeding and by affirmatively pursuing fees under a different legal theory. The trial court's award should be reversed, and Djuric's claim for attorney fees should be dismissed with prejudice.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. v. Djuric Trucking, Inc.**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees to Appellee Djuric Trucking, Inc. (“Djuric”). Appellant Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. (“Evergreen”) contends that the award is barred by the doctrine of res judicata and that Djuric waived its right to seek such fees. After reviewing the submitted briefs and the record provided, we find that Evergreen’s arguments are supported by established legal precedent, while Djuric’s arguments lack the necessary citation to authority to overcome the application of these doctrines. Accordingly, we reverse the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Fee Claim Is Barred by Res Judicata**

Res judicata, or claim preclusion, serves to prevent repetitive litigation and protect parties from the burden of relitigating claims that could have been adjudicated in a prior proceeding. Under Indiana law, the doctrine applies when there is (1) identity of parties, (2) identity of causes of action, and (3) a final judgment on the merits. *Town of Plainfield v. Town of Avon*, 996 N.E.2d 904, 910 (Ind. 2013). Furthermore, res judicata extends not only to claims that were actually litigated but also to those that could have been litigated in the prior proceeding. *Austrian v. L.S. & B, Inc.*, 888 N.E.2d 759 (Ind. Ct. App. 2008).

Here, the first two elements are undisputed. The parties in both proceedings are identical, and the fee claim arises from the same transaction and occurrence as the underlying dispute regarding the UIIA. The critical issue is whether the fee claim could have been litigated in the first action. The record indicates that the UIIA was the central contract governing the dispute and was designated by both parties during the summary judgment phase. The UIIA’s fee-shifting provision provides that the prevailing party in an action for breach or enforcement of the agreement is entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees. Djuric became the prevailing party upon the entry of summary judgment on November 16, 2011. At that moment, the right to fees under the UIIA vested and was enforceable.

Djuric argues that the claim was not addressed by the prior judgment and therefore is not barred. However, this misapprehends the scope of res judicata. As held in *Brown v. City of Michigan City*, 935 N.E.2d 1248 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010), a fee claim arising from an underlying judgment is part of the same cause of action for res judicata purposes when it could have been litigated concurrently. Djuric had full knowledge of the UIIA and its terms during the first litigation. By choosing to pursue fees solely under a statutory theory (Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1) and failing to assert its contractual right to fees under the UIIA in the first proceeding, Djuric attempted to split a cause of action. This is precisely the behavior the doctrine of res judicata is designed to prohibit. *Gertz v. Quicksilver Mining Co.*, 14 N.E.3d 747 (Ind. Ct. App. 2014). Because the fee claim could have been raised in the initial action, it is barred by res judicata.

**B. Djuric Waived Its Right to UIIA Fees**

Even if res judicata did not bar the claim, Djuric waived its right to recover attorney’s fees under the UIIA. Waiver is defined as the voluntary and intentional relinquishment of a known right. *Blazer v. New Albany-Floyd County Consol. Sch. Corp.*, 840 N.E.2d 895, 901 (Ind. Ct. App. 2006). A party may waive a right either expressly or by conduct that demonstrates an intent to relinquish that right. *Id.* Importantly, waiver may be inferred from a party’s failure to assert a claim or defense in the first proceeding when it had the opportunity to do so. *Kikkert v. Krumm*, 711 N.E.2d 562 (Ind. Ct. App. 1999).

The facts demonstrate that Djuric intentionally relinquished its contractual fee rights. During the summary judgment phase, Djuric affirmatively sought attorney’s fees under Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1, arguing that Evergreen’s claim was frivolous. At no point during this phase did Djuric move for fees under the UIIA, despite designating the UIIA in support of its own motion and having full knowledge of its terms. This was a deliberate strategic choice to pursue one legal theory of recovery over another.

Djuric now contends that pursuing statutory fees first was not a waiver of its contractual rights. We disagree. The law does not permit a party to hold a claim in reserve, litigate one theory, and then, if that theory fails, return to court to assert a theory that was available but abandoned at the outset. *Parker v. City of Indianapolis*, 865 N.E.2d 604 (Ind. Ct. App. 2007) (holding that contractual fee provisions must be asserted in accordance with applicable procedural rules, and failure to do so results in waiver). By failing to assert the UIIA fee provision in the first proceeding and instead pursuing a statutory claim, Djuric waived its right to the contractual fees.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees to Djuric was error. The claim is barred by res judicata because it arose from the same transaction and could have been litigated in the prior proceeding. Furthermore, Djuric waived its right to fees under the UIIA by failing to assert that contractual basis in the first action and by affirmatively pursuing fees under a different legal theory. We reverse the award of attorney’s fees and remand with instructions to enter judgment for Evergreen on this issue.

**SO ORDERED.**

**PRELIMINARY RULING UPDATE**

**Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. v. Djuric Trucking, Inc.**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees to Appellee Djuric Trucking, Inc. (“Djuric”). Appellant Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. (“Evergreen”) contends that the award is barred by the doctrine of res judicata and that Djuric waived its right to seek such fees. After reviewing the rebuttals submitted by both parties, we find that Evergreen’s arguments regarding claim preclusion and waiver are persuasive. Djuric’s assertion that its fee claim was unripe during the first proceeding is unpersuasive in light of Indiana law regarding contingent claims and election of remedies. Accordingly, we reverse the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Fee Claim Is Barred by Res Judicata**

Res judicata, or claim preclusion, prevents the relitigation of claims that have been litigated or could have been litigated in a prior proceeding. Under Indiana law, the doctrine applies when there is (1) identity of parties, (2) identity of causes of action, and (3) a final judgment on the merits. *Town of Plainfield v. Town of Avon*, 996 N.E.2d 904, 910 (Ind. 2013). The doctrine extends not only to claims that were actually litigated but also to those that could have been litigated in the prior proceeding. *Austrian v. L.S. & B, Inc.*, 888 N.E.2d 759 (Ind. Ct. App. 2008).

Djuric argues that its claim for attorney’s fees under the UIIA was not barred by res judicata because the right to such fees did not exist until it prevailed in the underlying action. Djuric contends that because it could not be deemed a "prevailing party" until the entry of summary judgment, the claim was unripe and could not have been raised earlier. We disagree.

The right to recover attorney’s fees under a contractual fee-shifting provision is a contingent right that arises from the same transaction as the underlying dispute. As held in *Brown v. City of Michigan City*, 935 N.E.2d 1248 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010), a fee claim arising from an underlying judgment is part of the same cause of action for res judicata purposes when it could have been litigated concurrently. The fact that the right to fees was contingent upon Djuric prevailing in the breach-of-contract action does not render the claim unripe or separate from the underlying cause of action. *Gertz v. Quicksilver Mining Co.*, 14 N.E.3d 747 (Ind. Ct. App. 2014) (holding that res judicata bars claims that could have been litigated in prior proceeding even if not yet reduced to a specific amount).

Djuric designated the UIIA in support of its motion for summary judgment and was fully aware of the fee-shifting provision at that time. The claim for fees was ascertainable and could have been pled as a contingent claim in the initial proceeding or raised in a post-judgment motion within the same case. By waiting until after the appeal of the underlying judgment was concluded to assert its contractual right to fees, Djuric engaged in claim splitting, which res judicata is designed to prevent. *Town of Plainfield v. Town of Avon*, 996 N.E.2d 904, 910 (Ind. 2013).

**B. Djuric Waived Its Right to UIIA Fees**

Even if res judicata did not bar the claim, Djuric waived its right to recover attorney’s fees under the UIIA through its conduct in the first proceeding. Waiver is defined as the voluntary and intentional relinquishment of a known right. *Blazer v. New Albany-Floyd County Consol. Sch. Corp.*, 840 N.E.2d 895, 901 (Ind. Ct. App. 2006). A party may waive a right either expressly or by conduct that demonstrates an intent to relinquish that right. *Id.* Importantly, waiver may be inferred from a party’s failure to assert a claim or defense in the first proceeding when it had the opportunity to do so. *Kikkert v. Krumm*, 711 N.E.2d 562 (Ind. Ct. App. 1999).

Djuric argues that it could not have waived a right that did not yet exist. However, the right to seek fees was a known contingent right at the time of the first proceeding. Djuric made a deliberate strategic choice to pursue attorney’s fees solely under Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1, arguing that Evergreen’s claim was frivolous. At no point during the first proceeding did Djuric seek fees under the UIIA, despite designating the UIIA and relying on its terms. This election of remedies constitutes a waiver of the contractual fee theory. *Parker v. City of Indianapolis*, 865 N.E.2d 604 (Ind. Ct. App. 2007) (holding that contractual fee provisions must be asserted in accordance with applicable procedural rules, and failure to do so results in waiver).

Djuric’s decision to wait until two weeks after the first appeal was concluded to file its request for UIIA fees further demonstrates waiver. A party who possesses knowledge of a contractual right to fees cannot hold that claim in reserve, litigate the case to a conclusion under a different theory of fees, and then, after failing to recover under that theory, return to court to assert the contractual claim. *Kikkert v. Krumm*, 711 N.E.2d 562 (Ind. Ct. App. 1999). Such procedural gamesmanship undermines the finality of judgments and is precisely what the doctrine of waiver seeks to preclude.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees to Djuric was error. The claim is barred by res judicata because it arose from the same transaction and could have been litigated in the prior proceeding. Furthermore, Djuric waived its right to fees under the UIIA by failing to assert that contractual basis in the first action and by affirmatively pursuing fees under a different legal theory. We reverse the award of attorney’s fees and remand with instructions to enter judgment for Evergreen on this issue.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR DEFENDANT-APPELLEE DJURIC TRUCKING, INC.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND OVERVIEW

**May it please the Court:**

The preliminary ruling before this Court rests upon a fundamental mischaracterization of both res judicata and waiver as applied to the unique circumstances of this case. Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. asks this Court to affirm a ruling that would effectively deny Djuric Trucking, Inc. compensation for fees rightfully owed under the Uniform Intermodal Interchange and Facilities Access Agreement—the very contract Evergreen invoked as the basis for its $34,000 claim against Djuric. This the Court should decline to do.

The preliminary ruling incorrectly applies res judicata to extinguish a claim that was **not ripe** during the first proceeding, misapplies the doctrine of waiver to a party that **did not relinquish** any right, and ignores the fundamental distinction between statutory and contractual fee-shifting provisions. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should **reverse** the preliminary ruling and **affirm** the trial court's award of approximately $12,000 in attorney's fees to Djuric.

---

## II. THE FEE CLAIM IS NOT BARRED BY RES JUDICATA

### A. The Preliminary Ruling Misapplies the Identity of Causes of Action Requirement

The preliminary ruling correctly identifies the three elements of res judicata under Indiana law: "(1) identity of parties, (2) identity of causes of action, and (3) a final judgment on the merits" as articulated in *Town of Plainfield v. Town of Avon*, 996 N.E.2d 904, 910 (Ind. 2013). However, the ruling errs in its application of the second element.

The doctrine of res judicata applies to claims that "could have been litigated in the prior proceeding." *Austrian v. L.S. & B, Inc.*, 888 N.E.2d 759 (Ind. Ct. App. 2008). The preliminary ruling relies heavily on this principle, arguing that Djuric's fee claim "could have been litigated" because it was a "contingent right" arising from the same transaction.

This reasoning conflates two distinct legal concepts: a **contingent claim** and a **ripe claim**. While Djuric's right to fees under the UIIA was contingent upon prevailing in the underlying action, it was **not ripe** for adjudication until that underlying action concluded. These are not mere semantic distinctions; they represent fundamental differences in procedural posture.

### B. The Fee Claim Was Not Ripe During the First Proceeding

A claim is not ripe when it depends upon "contingent future events that may not occur as anticipated, or indeed may not occur at all." *Indiana Gas Co. v. City of Evansville*, 814 N.E.2d 286, 293 (Ind. Ct. App. 2004). At the time of the first proceeding, multiple contingencies remained unresolved:

1. **The underlying litigation was ongoing.** Evergreen had filed a notice of appeal. The outcome of the breach-of-contract claim was not final.

2. **Djuric's status as a "prevailing party" was uncertain.** The UIIA entitles only the "prevailing party" to recover reasonable attorney's fees. See Appellee's App. p. 19. Until the summary judgment was affirmed on appeal, Djuric's status as a prevailing party remained subject to reversal.

3. **The amount of fees was entirely speculative.** No attorney had performed work in connection with the fee claim itself during the first proceeding. The fees sought in the second proceeding arose from defending against Evergreen's appeal—a proceeding that did not exist when Djuric filed its initial summary judgment motion.

The preliminary ruling's reliance on *Brown v. City of Michigan City*, 935 N.E.2d 1248 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010), is misplaced. In *Brown*, the fee claim arose from a final judgment that was not subject to appeal. The claimant had already "prevailed" within the meaning of the governing statute. Here, by contrast, Djuric's right to fees remained contingent until the conclusion of the appeal in the first proceeding.

### C. The Preliminary Ruling Contradicts Fundamental Principles of Claim Ripeness

The preliminary ruling states that "the right to recover attorney's fees under a contractual fee-shifting provision is a contingent right that arises from the same transaction as the underlying dispute." While this statement is technically accurate, it does not support the conclusion drawn therefrom. The fact that a right is "contingent" does not mean it is "ripe" for adjudication.

Indiana courts have consistently held that a claim is not ripe for adjudication when it depends upon a future event that has not yet occurred. *See City of Carmel v. Steele*, 956 N.E.2d 660, 667 (Ind. Ct. App. 2011) ("Ripeness is a constitutional requirement that ensures that the court is not called upon to decide hypothetical or abstract questions."). The preliminary ruling's analysis ignores this fundamental principle.

The preliminary ruling also cites *Gertz v. Quicksilver Mining Co.*, 14 N.E.3d 747 (Ind. Ct. App. 2014), for the proposition that "res judicata bars claims that could have been litigated in prior proceeding even if not yet reduced to a specific amount." However, *Gertz* is distinguishable. In *Gertz*, the claimant had already obtained a favorable judgment and was merely seeking to liquidate the amount of damages. Here, Djuric had not yet obtained any judgment when the first proceeding concluded—the appeal was still pending.

### D. The Preliminary Ruling Improperly Applies Claim Splitting Principles

The preliminary ruling accuses Djuric of "claim splitting." This accusation is unfounded. Claim splitting occurs when a party "splits a single cause of action into two or more suits, each asserting only a part of the claim." *Raimo v. Lewis*, 415 N.E.2d 85, 90 (Ind. Ct. App. 1981). This is not what occurred here.

Djuric's fee claim under the UIIA did not exist as a separate, enforceable claim during the first proceeding. It was a **contingent right** that depended upon the outcome of the underlying litigation. Once that litigation concluded and Djuric emerged as the prevailing party, a new claim arose—one that could not have been litigated earlier because the predicate event (final judgment in Djuric's favor) had not yet occurred.

The preliminary ruling's reliance on *Town of Plainfield* is misplaced. In that case, the Town of Plainfield attempted to bring a second lawsuit seeking relief that could have been obtained in the first lawsuit. *Town of Plainfield v. Town of Avon*, 996 N.E.2d 904, 910 (Ind. 2013). Here, by contrast, Djuric could not have obtained fee relief in the first proceeding because the prerequisite—a final judgment in its favor—did not exist until after the appeal concluded.

---

## III. DJURIC DID NOT WAIVE ITS RIGHT TO UIIA FEES

### A. The Preliminary Ruling's Waiver Analysis Is Flawed

The preliminary ruling concludes that Djuric waived its right to fees under the UIIA by "affirmatively pursuing fees under a different legal theory" in the first proceeding. This conclusion rests upon a misinterpretation of both waiver and election of remedies.

Waiver is "the voluntary and intentional relinquishment of a known right." *Blazer v. New Albany-Floyd County Consol. Sch. Corp.*, 840 N.E.2d 895, 901 (Ind. Ct. App. 2006). It requires both: (1) knowledge of the right, and (2) intentional relinquishment of that right. *Id.* The preliminary ruling conflates these two elements, focusing solely on Djuric's knowledge while improperly inferring intent from strategic litigation choices.

### B. Djuric Did Not Intentionally Relinquish Any Right

The preliminary ruling states that "Djuric made a deliberate strategic choice to pursue attorney's fees solely under Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1, arguing that Evergreen's claim was frivolous." This characterization is accurate but does not constitute waiver.

Pursuing one remedy does not constitute abandonment of another unless there is evidence of "intentional relinquishment." *Kikkert v. Krumm*, 711 N.E.2d 562, 569 (Ind. Ct. App. 1999). Djuric's decision to seek fees under the frivolous claim statute was a litigation strategy—a statutory basis for fees that did not require prevailing on the underlying contract claim. It was not an election of remedies that precluded subsequent pursuit of contractual fees.

The preliminary ruling cites *Parker v. City of Indianapolis*, 865 N.E.2d 604 (Ind. Ct. App. 2007), for the proposition that "contractual fee provisions must be asserted in accordance with applicable procedural rules, and failure to do so results in waiver." However, *Parker* is distinguishable. In *Parker*, the claimant had **already obtained a final judgment** and was **attempting to assert a new theory of fees** after the time for appeal had expired. Here, by contrast, Djuric filed its fee request **within weeks** of the first appeal's conclusion—before any judgment became final.

### C. The Preliminary Ruling's "Election of Remedies" Analysis Is Erroneous

The preliminary ruling states that "Djuric's election of remedies constitutes a waiver of the contractual fee theory." This statement misapplies the doctrine of election of remedies.

Election of remedies applies when a party "chooses one of two or more coexisting remedial rights." *Wawrinchak v. G.J. & F. Enterprises, Inc.*, 730 N.E.2d 197, 201 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000). It prevents a party from pursuing inconsistent remedies simultaneously. It does not prevent a party from pursuing different remedies **sequentially**, particularly where the first remedy was unsuccessful.

Djuric's pursuit of fees under the frivolous claim statute was not an "election" that precluded pursuit of contractual fees. The statutory claim was denied by the trial court in the first proceeding. Djuric did not appeal that denial. The denial of statutory fees, however, did not preclude Djuric from seeking contractual fees—a distinct basis that had not been adjudicated.

### D. The Preliminary Ruling's "Gamesmanship" Argument Is Unsupported

The preliminary ruling accuses Djuric of "procedural gamesmanship" and "hold[ing] that claim in reserve." This characterization is unfair and unsupported by the record.

Djuric sought fees under the frivolous claim statute because that statute provided a basis for fees that did not depend on the UIIA's fee-shifting provision. At that time, the UIIA's applicability to Djuric was itself in dispute—Evergreen was claiming that Djuric was liable for per diem charges under the UIIA. It would have been inconsistent for Djuric to simultaneously rely on the UIIA to defeat Evergreen's claim and also rely on the UIIA to obtain fees.

Once the first proceeding concluded and Djuric's status as the prevailing party was finally established, Djuric pursued its contractual right to fees. This is not "gamesmanship"; it is the orderly progression of litigation as facts develop and rights mature.

---

## IV. THE PRELIMINARY RULING IGNORES THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN STATUTORY AND CONTRACTUAL FEE PROVISIONS

The preliminary ruling's analysis fails to distinguish between statutory fee-shifting provisions and contractual fee-shifting provisions. This distinction is critical.

**Statutory fee provisions**, such as Indiana Code § 34-52-1-1, are generally mandatory and must be asserted in accordance with specific procedural requirements. Failure to comply with those requirements may result in waiver.

**Contractual fee provisions**, such as the UIIA's fee-shifting clause, are matters of private agreement between the parties. They are subject to the general principles of contract interpretation and do not require the same procedural specificity as statutory provisions.

The UIIA provides that "the prevailing party" may recover "reasonable attorney's fees." This provision is triggered by the occurrence of a specific event—the entry of judgment in favor of a party to the agreement. Prior to that event, the right to fees does not mature. Djuric's delay in asserting this right until after the first proceeding concluded was not a waiver; it was simply waiting for the contingency to occur.

---

## V. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT DJURIC'S POSITION

The preliminary ruling's approach, if affirmed, would create perverse incentives for litigants. Under the ruling's logic, a party seeking contractual fees would be required to:

1. File a contingent fee claim at the outset of litigation, even though the right to fees has not yet matured;
2. Amend that claim continuously as the litigation progresses; and
3. Pursue all possible fee theories simultaneously, regardless of consistency or procedural efficiency.

This approach would burden trial courts with premature fee claims, increase litigation costs, and undermine the purpose of fee-shifting provisions—which is to provide compensation to the prevailing party, not to create additional procedural hurdles.

The better approach is to recognize that contractual fee claims are governed by the timing provisions of the underlying contract. Under the UIIA, fees are available to the "prevailing party." Djuric became the prevailing party only after the first appeal concluded. Its fee claim, filed two weeks later, was timely.

---

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant-Appellee Djuric Trucking, Inc. respectfully requests that this Court:

1. **REVERSE** the preliminary ruling's conclusion that Djuric's fee claim is barred by res judicata;

2. **REVERSE** the preliminary ruling's conclusion that Djuric waived its right to fees under the UIIA;

3. **AFFIRM** the trial court's award of approximately $12,000 in attorney's fees to Djuric; and

4. **GRANT** any other relief that this Court deems just and proper.

The preliminary ruling rests upon a misapplication of fundamental legal principles. Djuric did not split its claims; it simply waited for its right to mature. Djuric did not waive its rights; it pursued one available remedy and, when that remedy was denied, pursued another. The trial court's award of fees was proper and should be upheld.

**FINAL RULING**

**Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. v. Djuric Trucking, Inc.**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees to Appellee Djuric Trucking, Inc. (“Djuric”). Appellant Evergreen Shipping Agency Corp. (“Evergreen”) contends that the award is barred by the doctrine of res judicata and that Djuric waived its right to seek such fees. After reviewing the closing arguments and the applicable law, we find that Djuric’s claim for attorney’s fees was not ripe for adjudication during the initial proceeding and, therefore, could not have been litigated at that time. Consequently, the claim is not barred by res judicata, nor did Djuric waive its right to fees. We affirm the trial court’s award.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Fee Claim Is Not Barred by Res Judicata**

Res judicata, or claim preclusion, prevents the relitigation of claims that have been litigated or could have been litigated in a prior proceeding. Under Indiana law, the doctrine applies when there is (1) identity of parties, (2) identity of causes of action, and (3) a final judgment on the merits. *Town of Plainfield v. Town of Avon*, 996 N.E.2d 904, 910 (Ind. 2013). The doctrine extends to claims that could have been litigated in the prior proceeding. *Austrian v. L.S. & B, Inc.*, 888 N.E.2d 759 (Ind. Ct. App. 2008).

Evergreen argues that Djuric’s claim for attorney’s fees under the Uniform Intermodal Interchange and Facilities Access Agreement (“UIIA”) should have been raised in the first lawsuit because it was a contingent right arising from the same transaction. We disagree.

The UIIA entitles the “prevailing party” to recover reasonable attorney’s fees. A claim is not ripe for adjudication when it depends upon “contingent future events that may not occur as anticipated, or indeed may not occur at all.” *Indiana Gas Co. v. City of Evansville*, 814 N.E.2d 286, 293 (Ind. Ct. App. 2004). At the time of the first proceeding, Djuric’s status as the “prevailing party” was not final. Although the trial court had granted summary judgment in favor of Djuric, that judgment was immediately appealed. Until the appeal was resolved, Djuric’s right to fees under the UIIA remained contingent and uncertain. If Evergreen had succeeded on appeal, Djuric would not have been the prevailing party and would have had no right to fees under the contract.

Evergreen relies on *Brown v. City of Michigan City*, 935 N.E.2d 1248 (Ind. Ct. App. 2010), and *Gertz v. Quicksilver Mining Co.*, 14 N.E.3d 747 (Ind. Ct. App. 2014), for the proposition that a fee claim is part of the same cause of action even if the amount is not yet liquidated. However, those cases are distinguishable. In *Brown* and *Gertz*, the underlying judgment was final and the only contingency was the calculation of the fees. Here, the very existence of the right to fees was contingent upon the finality of the judgment in Djuric’s favor. Because the right to fees did not vest until the appeal was concluded on September 3, 2012, the claim was not ripe and could not have been litigated in the first proceeding. *City of Carmel v. Steele*, 956 N.E.2d 660, 667 (Ind. Ct. App. 2011) (ripeness ensures courts do not decide hypothetical questions).

Therefore, the second element of res judicata—identity of causes of action—is not satisfied in a manner that would bar Djuric’s claim, as the fee claim did not exist as a mature, enforceable right during the first lawsuit.

**B. Djuric Did Not Waive Its Right to UIIA Fees**

Waiver is defined as the voluntary and intentional relinquishment of a known right. *Blazer v. New Albany-Floyd County Consol. Sch. Corp.*, 840 N.E.2d 895, 901 (Ind. Ct. App. 2006). A party may waive a right either expressly or by conduct that demonstrates an intent to relinquish that right.

Evergreen contends that Djuric waived its right to contractual fees by pursuing fees under Indiana Code section 34-52-1-1 (the frivolous claim statute) in the first proceeding. We find this argument unpersuasive.

The doctrine of election of remedies prevents a party from pursuing inconsistent remedies simultaneously. *Wawrinchak v. G.J. & F. Enterprises, Inc.*, 730 N.E.2d 197, 201 (Ind. Ct. App. 2000). However, it does not prevent a party from pursuing different remedies sequentially, particularly where the first remedy is unsuccessful and the second remedy was not available at the time of the first.

Djuric’s pursuit of statutory fees in the first proceeding was based on a theory that did not require Djuric to be the “prevailing party” on the contract claim. In contrast, the contractual fee claim under the UIIA required Djuric to be the prevailing party, a status that was not finalized until after the appeal. Because the contractual right to fees did not vest until the appeal concluded, Djuric could not have intentionally relinquished that right during the trial court proceedings. One cannot waive a right that does not yet exist. *Kikkert v. Krumm*, 711 N.E.2d 562, 569 (Ind. Ct. App. 1999) (waiver requires knowledge of the right).

Djuric filed its request for UIIA fees two weeks after the first appeal was concluded. This was not procedural gamesmanship, but rather the first moment at which the claim became ripe. The trial court did not err in concluding that Djuric had not waived its right to fees.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees to Djuric was proper. The claim for fees was not barred by res judicata because it was not ripe for adjudication until the conclusion of the first appeal. Furthermore, Djuric did not waive its right to fees under the UIIA, as that right had not vested at the time Djuric pursued statutory fees in the first proceeding. We affirm the trial court’s award of attorney’s fees.

**SO ORDERED.**
