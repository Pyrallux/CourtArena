# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE OFFICE OF WORKERS' COMPENSATION

---

**DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE TO PLAINTIFF'S APPEAL**

**Your Honor,** the defense, Command Construction, LLC ("Employer"), respectfully submits this opening response to the plaintiff's appeal and opposes the affirmation of the trial court's judgment. The plaintiff has failed to meet his burden of proving a compensable work-related accident by a preponderance of the evidence. The trial court committed manifest error in finding otherwise, and this Court should reverse the judgment, dismiss the claim with prejudice, and deny any penalties or attorney's fees.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This case presents a close question of fact regarding whether the claimant, Pedro Salazar, actually sustained a work-related injury on March 23, 2011. The Employer's defense rests not on a denial that the claimant was employed or that he reported some pain, but rather on the fundamental issue of whether the alleged accident actually occurred as described. The evidence presented at trial was conflicting, and the trial court failed to properly weigh the significant credibility issues that undermine the claimant's version of events.

The plaintiff has characterized the Employer's arguments as a "fundamental misunderstanding of Louisiana workers' compensation law." To the contrary, the Employer acknowledges that Louisiana workers' compensation law is liberally construed in favor of coverage. However, even under the most liberal construction, the law does not abandon the requirement that the claimant prove each element of his claim by a preponderance of the evidence. The trial court erred in failing to properly analyze the evidence and in ignoring the substantial evidence that contradicted the claimant's self-serving testimony.

---

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Claimant Failed to Prove That a Work-Related Accident Occurred

The plaintiff correctly notes that Louisiana law does not require direct eyewitness testimony to establish a compensable accident. However, the plaintiff misapplies this principle. The case law establishes that a claimant's testimony alone *may* be sufficient—not that it *is* always sufficient. The determination depends on the totality of the circumstances and the credibility of the claimant's account.

In **Boudreaux v. American Ins. Co.**, 635 So. 2d 205 (La. 1994), the Louisiana Supreme Court held that a claimant's testimony may be sufficient to establish an accident, but this holding was expressly conditioned on the testimony being "credible" and consistent with the surrounding circumstances. The Court emphasized that "the testimony must be believable" and that the circumstances must "give rise to a reasonable inference that the accident did occur." The plaintiff cannot simply invoke *Boudreaux* as a blanket shield against any challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence.

Here, the claimant's testimony is contradicted by multiple material facts:

**First**, none of the workers who were allegedly working near the claimant at the time of the accident—including the claimant's own brother—actually witnessed the incident. While the plaintiff attempts to minimize this fact, the absence of any eyewitness is significant when the claimant's own witnesses were positioned to observe the accident but did not do so.

**Second**, the claimant's own witnesses provided testimony that undermines his version of events. The claimant testified that he intended to report the accident to his supervisor, Bryan Monte, on the morning of March 24, 2011. However, when he arrived at the jobsite, he was met by Monte with a Personnel Action Form regarding an incident with another supervisor. The claimant did not mention his alleged injury at that time—he simply refused to sign the form. This delay in reporting, combined with his failure to mention the injury when directly confronted by his supervisor, casts serious doubt on the spontaneity and severity of the alleged injury.

**Third**, the claimant's actions in the days following the alleged accident are inconsistent with a man who had sustained a serious back and shoulder injury while lifting a 250-pound form. On March 29, 2011—just six days after the alleged accident—the claimant contacted the construction foreman, Byron Dupre, indicating that he wanted to return to work. This proactive communication suggests that the claimant did not believe himself to be seriously injured, or at minimum, that his condition was not as disabling as now claimed.

In **Morrison v. McCracken**, 935 So. 2d 192 (La. App. 2d Cir. 2006), the court held that a claimant's delay in reporting an injury and failure to seek immediate medical attention could be considered in evaluating the credibility of his claim. The court noted that such conduct "tends to negate the occurrence of a serious industrial accident."

### B. The Trial Court Erred in Relying on Raul Salazar's Testimony

The plaintiff attempts to bolster the claimant's case by relying on the testimony of Raul Salazar, the claimant's brother. This reliance is misplaced.

Raul Salazar explicitly testified that he **did not witness the alleged accident**. He merely heard about it from the claimant later that evening. His testimony that the claimant was "in pain at the end of the day" is insufficient to corroborate the specific mechanism of injury—lifting a 250-pound cement form—because Raul had no personal knowledge of how the claimant allegedly sustained his injury.

More critically, the defense elicited testimony that Raul Salazar had been paying the claimant's living expenses because the claimant had not worked since March 23, 2011. The plaintiff characterizes this as "irrelevant and prejudicial." To the contrary, this evidence goes directly to Raul Salazar's credibility and potential bias. As the Louisiana Supreme Court held in **Crawford v. Louisiana State University**, 28 So. 3d 1054 (La. 2010), "the credibility of a witness may be impeached by evidence that the witness has a financial interest in the outcome of the litigation." Raul Salazar's financial support of the claimant creates a direct financial interest in the claimant's success in this litigation.

The trial court erred in failing to properly weigh this credibility evidence. Raul Salazar's testimony, while perhaps admissible, carries minimal probative value and cannot serve as substantial evidence of a work-related accident.

### C. The Employer Had a Reasonable Basis to Controvert the Claim

The plaintiff seeks penalties and attorney's fees under La. R.S. 23:1201(F), arguing that the Employer's denial of benefits was arbitrary and capricious. This argument fails.

The plaintiff incorrectly asserts that the employer "denied the claim on April 5, 2011, without even reviewing the claimant's medical records from Dr. Davis." This assertion misrepresents the record. The April 5, 2011 letter from Gray Insurance Company states only that "we have completed our investigation"—not that no investigation occurred. The insurer was entitled to rely on the information gathered during its investigation, which included the employer's contesting of the accident and the absence of any eyewitnesses to corroborate the claimant's version.

The standard for determining whether a denial constitutes "reasonable controversion" was established by the Louisiana Supreme Court in **Banks v. Industrial Roofing & Sheet Metal Works**, 696 So. 2d 1148 (La. 1997). In *Banks*, the Court held that "the purpose of the penalty provision is to deter obstinate refusal to pay compensation" and that "a denial is not arbitrary and capricious if there is a reasonable basis for it at the time it is made."

Here, the employer had multiple reasonable bases for denying the claim:

1. **No eyewitnesses to the accident**: The claimant's own witnesses—including his brother—did not see the accident occur.

2. **Inconsistent reporting**: The claimant failed to report the injury when he first saw his supervisor on March 24, 2011.

3. **Contradictory conduct**: The claimant contacted his employer six days after the alleged accident seeking to return to work.

4. **Medical uncertainty**: The initial medical treatment from Dr. Davis was for only five days (March 24-29, 2011), and Dr. Davis referred the claimant to another clinic on April 1, 2011.

These circumstances, taken together, provided a reasonable basis for the employer to contest the claim. The mere fact that medical evidence ultimately supported the claimant's position does not render the initial denial arbitrary, particularly where the medical evidence was incomplete at the time of the denial.

In **Williams v. Louisiana Painting & Drywall**, 941 So. 2d 634 (La. App. 1st Cir. 2006), the court held that "the question of whether a denial is reasonable must be determined based on the information available to the insurer at the time of the denial, not on hindsight." The insurer is not required to credit the claimant's version of events when other evidence casts doubt on the claim.

The plaintiff cites **Johnson v. Louisiana Riverboat Gaming Commission**, 752 So. 2d 1251 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1999), for the proposition that ignoring uncontroverted medical evidence constitutes arbitrary denial. However, *Johnson* is distinguishable. In that case, the insurer had no evidence whatsoever to contradict the medical evidence. Here, by contrast, the employer had substantial evidence casting doubt on the claimant's account—including the absence of eyewitnesses, the claimant's own conduct, and the contradictory testimony of other witnesses.

### D. The Stipulation Does Not Support the Plaintiff's Position

The plaintiff argues that the employer's stipulation to the claimant's average weekly wage "underscores the employer's inconsistent position" and "further demonstrates the lack of reasonable basis for their denial." This argument is without merit.

The parties stipulated to the claimant's average weekly wage and workers' compensation rate for the sole purpose of calculating potential benefits **if** the claimant proved entitlement to benefits. Stipulating to these figures is not an admission that the claimant is entitled to benefits, nor does it estop the employer from contesting the fundamental issue of whether a compensable accident occurred. The stipulation was limited in scope and was expressly entered for a limited purpose.

In **Hebert v. General Motors Corp.**, 959 So. 2d 824 (La. App. 3d Cir. 2007), the court held that stipulations to wage data do not constitute admissions regarding the occurrence of an industrial accident. The plaintiff conflates separate issues and attempts to transform a limited stipulation into an admission of liability.

---

## III. CONCLUSION

The plaintiff has failed to carry his burden of proving a compensable work-related accident. The trial court's judgment is manifestly erroneous and should be reversed. The evidence at trial established that:

1. No eyewitnesses corroborated the claimant's account of the accident;
2. The claimant's own conduct in the days following the alleged accident was inconsistent with a serious injury;
3. The claimant's brother, the sole corroborating witness, had a financial interest in the outcome;
4. The employer had a reasonable basis to contest the claim based on the evidence available at the time of denial.

The Employer respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment, dismiss the claimant's claim with prejudice, and deny the plaintiff's request for penalties and attorney's fees.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before the Court:** Appeal of the Judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation.

**Pursuant to the review of the opening arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, the Court renders the following preliminary ruling.**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

In workers' compensation cases, the appellate court's review of the trial court's factual findings is governed by the manifest error standard of review. *Banks v. Industrial Roofing & Sheet Metal Works*, 696 So. 2d 1148, 1152 (La. 1997). A trial court's finding of fact cannot be reversed unless the appellate court finds the trial court was clearly wrong or manifestly erroneous. *Stobart v. State, Through Dept. of Transp. and Dev.*, 617 So. 2d 880, 882 (La. 1993). However, questions of law are subject to a de novo review. *Banks*, 696 So. 2d at 1152.

**II. ANALYSIS OF THE CLAIM**

**A. The Claimant’s Burden of Proof**

The defense correctly asserts that the claimant bears the burden of proving a work-related accident by a preponderance of the evidence. The prosecution argues that the claimant met this burden through his own testimony, corroborated by the physical demands of the job and immediate medical treatment.

The defense contends that the claimant’s testimony is uncorroborated and incredible due to the lack of eyewitnesses and inconsistencies in his reporting. Specifically, the defense relies on *Boudreaux v. American Ins. Co.*, 635 So. 2d 205 (La. 1994), to argue that a claimant’s testimony must be credible and consistent with the surrounding circumstances.

The Court finds the prosecution’s reliance on *Boudreaux* and *Smith v. Town of Marksville*, 777 So. 2d 1154 (La. App. 3d Cir. 2001), to be persuasive. As established in *Boudreaux*, a claimant’s testimony alone may be sufficient to establish an accident, and direct eyewitness testimony is not a prerequisite for recovery. *Boudreaux*, 635 So. 2d at 208. Here, the claimant’s testimony regarding the mechanism of injury—lifting heavy cement forms—is consistent with the supervisor’s testimony regarding the physical requirements of the job, which involved lifting forms weighing up to 250 pounds. This constitutes sufficient circumstantial evidence to support the claimant’s burden under *Smith*.

**B. Credibility of the Claimant and Witnesses**

The defense challenges the credibility of the claimant, citing *Morrison v. McCracken*, 935 So. 2d 192 (La. App. 2d Cir. 2006), for the proposition that a delay in reporting and failure to seek immediate medical attention negate the occurrence of a serious accident. The defense points to the claimant’s failure to immediately report the injury to Supervisor Monte on the morning of March 24, 2011, and his contact with the foreman on March 29, 2011, regarding a return to work.

The Court finds these arguments unpersuasive in light of the record. The facts indicate that the claimant intended to report the accident but was interrupted by a disciplinary discussion with his supervisor. He subsequently reported the accident and sought medical attention from Dr. Davis on the very same day, March 24, 2011. Regarding the March 29 contact, the facts show this was consistent with Dr. Davis’s initial instructions to stay out of work only through March 29. Therefore, the claimant’s conduct does not rise to the level of incredibility suggested by the defense.

The defense further attacks the credibility of Raul Salazar, the claimant’s brother, citing *Crawford v. Louisiana State University*, 28 So. 3d 1054 (La. 2010), regarding the financial interest of a witness. While Raul Salazar’s financial support of the claimant establishes a potential bias, his testimony was limited to observing the claimant’s pain at the end of the workday. This observation is corroborated by the uncontroverted medical records of Dr. Davis from the following day. Consequently, the bias of the witness does not undermine the foundational medical evidence supporting the claim.

**C. Stipulation of Facts**

The defense correctly argues, citing *Hebert v. General Motors Corp.*, 959 So. 2d 824 (La. App. 3d Cir. 2007), that the stipulation to the claimant’s average weekly wage is not an admission of liability. The Court agrees with this legal principle; however, the stipulation is irrelevant to the determination of whether the accident occurred. It merely establishes the economic parameters of the claim should liability be found.

**III. ANALYSIS OF PENALTIES AND ATTORNEY’S FEES**

The prosecution requests penalties and attorney’s fees under La. R.S. 23:1201(F), arguing the employer’s denial was arbitrary and capricious. The defense contends the denial was based on a reasonable basis, citing *Banks v. Industrial Roofing & Sheet Metal Works*, 696 So. 2d 1148 (La. 1997), and *Williams v. Louisiana Painting & Drywall*, 941 So. 2d 634 (La. App. 1st Cir. 2006).

The Court finds the defense’s argument regarding reasonable controversion to be meritorious. Under *Banks*, a denial is not arbitrary if there is a reasonable basis for it at the time it is made. *Banks*, 696 So. 2d at 1152. The employer’s denial on April 5, 2011, was based on a lack of eyewitnesses to the accident and conflicting accounts of the incident. While the prosecution cites *Johnson v. Louisiana Riverboat Gaming Commission*, 752 So. 2d 1251 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1999), to argue that ignoring uncontroverted medical evidence is arbitrary, the record here indicates a genuine dispute of fact regarding the occurrence of the accident. The absence of any eyewitnesses, including the claimant's brother who was working nearby, provided the employer with a legitimate factual dispute to support its denial. As held in *Williams*, the reasonableness of a denial is judged by the information available at the time, not hindsight. *Williams*, 941 So. 2d at 638. The employer’s reliance on the lack of corroboration constitutes a reasonable basis to contest the claim, precluding an award of penalties and attorney’s fees.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court preliminarily rules that the trial court’s judgment finding the claimant sustained a work-related accident is affirmed. The claimant met his burden of proof through credible testimony and corroborating medical evidence. However, the defense’s argument regarding the reasonableness of the claim denial is sustained. The employer had a reasonable basis to controvert the claim based on the lack of eyewitnesses and conflicting evidence. Therefore, the award of penalties and attorney’s fees is reversed.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

In workers' compensation cases, the appellate court's review of the trial court's factual findings is governed by the manifest error standard of review. *Banks v. **A. The Claimant’s Burden of Proof and Credibility**

The defense reiterates its argument that the claimant failed to meet his burden of proof because his testimony was uncorroborated and inconsistent with the surrounding circumstances. Specifically, the defense points to the testimony of the claimant's brother, Raul Salazar, who was working nearby but did not see the accident, and the supervisor's testimony that lifting the forms required three to four men. The Court finds that the trial court's determination regarding the credibility of the claimant is not manifestly erroneous. Therefore, the finding that the claimant sustained a work-related accident is affirmed.

**III. ANALYSIS OF PENALTIES AND ATTORNEY’S FEES**

**A. Reasonableness of the Denial**

The prosecution argues that the employer's denial of the claim was arbitrary and capricious because it ignored uncontroverted medical evidence establishing a causal link between the injury and work. The prosecution relies on *Johnson v. Louisiana Riverboat Gaming Commission*, 752 So. 2d 1251 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1999), and contends that under *Banks v. Industrial Roofing & Sheet Metal Works*, 696 So. 2d 1148 (La. 1997), the employer lacked "actual evidence contradicting the claim."

The defense responds that the employer's denial was based on a reasonable factual dispute, specifically the complete absence of eyewitnesses to an accident that allegedly required multiple workers to perform. The defense distinguishes *Johnson*, arguing that the medical records in this case do not independently prove the accident occurred but rather document the claimant's subjective complaints. The defense relies on *Williams v. Louisiana Painting & Drywall*, 941 So. 2d 634 (La. App. 1st Cir. 2006), for the proposition that a denial is reasonable if based on the information available at the time, which here included a lack of corroboration.

The Court finds the defense's argument regarding the reasonableness of the denial to be persuasive. Under *Banks*, a denial is not arbitrary if there is a reasonable basis for it at the time it is made. *Banks*, 696 So. 2d at 1152. The employer's investigation revealed that no witnesses, including the claimant's brother who was working nearby, observed the accident. Given the supervisor's testimony that lifting the forms required three to four men, the absence of any corroborating witnesses provided the employer with a legitimate factual dispute regarding the occurrence of the accident.

The prosecution's reliance on *Johnson* is misplaced in this context. In *Johnson*, the employer denied benefits despite uncontroverted medical evidence establishing the injury. *Johnson*, 752 So. 2d at 1255. Here, the medical records cited by the prosecution (Dr. Davis's notes) rely on the claimant's self-reported history of the accident. They do not constitute independent medical verification of the accident's occurrence itself. The employer did not ignore uncontroverted medical evidence of the accident; it disputed the factual premise of the claimant's report due to the lack of corroboration.

Furthermore, as held in *Williams*, the reasonableness of a denial is judged by the information available at the time, not by hindsight. *Williams*, 941 So. 2d at 638. The employer's denial on April 5, 2011, was based on the absence of evidence supporting the claimant's account. This constitutes a reasonable basis to controvert the claim. The prosecution's assertion that the employer was required to produce affirmative evidence contradicting the claim misstates the law; a genuine dispute as to the facts surrounding the alleged accident is sufficient to preclude a finding of arbitrary and capricious behavior.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court's judgment finding the claimant sustained a work-related accident. The trial court's credibility determinations are not manifestly erroneous. Additionally, the Court affirms the trial court's ruling denying penalties and attorney's fees. The employer established a reasonable basis for its denial based on the lack of eyewitness corroboration and the resulting factual dispute, consistent with *Banks* and *Williams*. The prosecution's arguments regarding the arbitrary nature of the denial are rejected.

**Before the Court:** Appeal of the Judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation.

**Pursuant to the review of the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, the Court issues its final ruling as follows.**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The appellate standard of review for workers' compensation cases is well established. However, the question of whether an employer's denial of benefits was arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause, thus warranting penalties and attorney's fees, is a legal issue subject to *de novo* review. *Banks v. Industrial Roofing & Sheet Metal Works*, 696 So. 2d 1148, 1152 (La. 1997).

**II. ANALYSIS OF THE CLAIM**

**A. Finding of Work-Related Accident**

The trial court found that the claimant sustained a work-related accident. The prosecution urges this Court to affirm this finding, arguing that the claimant's testimony was credible and consistent with the surrounding circumstances, including the physical demands of the job and immediate medical treatment. The defense does not contest the affirmance of the accident finding in its closing argument, focusing instead on the reasonableness of the employer's denial.

Upon review, the record supports the trial court's determination. The claimant testified to the specific events of March 23, 2011, and sought medical attention the following day. While the defense points to the lack of eyewitnesses, Louisiana law explicitly holds that a claimant's testimony alone may be sufficient to establish an accident. *Boudreaux v. American Ins. Co.*, 635 So. 2d 205, 208 (La. 1994). The trial court's acceptance of the claimant's version, supported by the medical records documenting his complaint immediately following the incident, is not manifestly erroneous. Therefore, the finding that the claimant sustained a work-related accident is affirmed.

**B. Penalties and Attorney's Fees**

The central issue on appeal is whether the employer's denial of the claim was arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. La. R.S. 23:1201(F) imposes penalties and attorney's fees if the employer's failure to pay benefits is not based on a reasonable dispute.

**1. Reasonableness of the Denial**

The prosecution argues that the denial was arbitrary because it ignored uncontroverted medical evidence establishing a causal link between the injury and work. Specifically, the prosecution relies on *Johnson v. Louisiana Riverboat Gaming Commission*, 752 So. 2d 1251 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1999), asserting that the medical records from Dr. Davis, which documented the claimant's report of pain "as a result of lifting at work," constituted such evidence.

The defense counters that the medical records relied upon by the prosecution were based solely on the claimant's subjective complaints and self-reported history, rather than independent medical verification of the accident itself. The defense further argues that the employer had a reasonable basis to deny the claim based on factual disputes, specifically the supervisor's testimony that lifting the forms required three to four men, which contradicted the claimant's account of the incident.

The Court finds the defense's argument persuasive. Under *Banks*, a denial is not arbitrary if there is a reasonable basis for it at the time it is made. *Banks*, 696 So. 2d at 1152. The employer's investigation revealed significant discrepancies in the claimant's account. Supervisor Bryan Monte testified that the cement forms weighed up to 250 pounds and required three to four men to lift. This testimony directly contradicted the claimant's assertion that he was injured while lifting the form himself. This contradiction provided the employer with a legitimate factual dispute regarding the occurrence and mechanism of the accident.

Furthermore, the medical records cited by the prosecution do not constitute the type of "uncontroverted medical evidence" that renders a denial arbitrary under *Johnson*. In *Johnson*, the employer denied benefits despite objective medical findings supporting the claim. Here, Dr. Davis's notes reflect only the claimant's subjective report of how the injury occurred. As the defense correctly notes, these records document the claimant's statement, not an independent medical observation of the accident. An employer is entitled to rely on the absence of corroborating evidence and the presence of contradictory testimony regarding the feasibility of the claimant's description of the accident.

The prosecution's reliance on *Boudreaux* to argue that the employer cannot deny a claim based on a lack of eyewitnesses is misplaced. While *Boudreaux* holds that a claimant need not produce eyewitnesses to prove a claim, it does not hold that an employer must accept a claimant's version of events in the face of contradictory evidence from a supervisor regarding the physical requirements of the job. The employer's denial was based on a reasonable assessment of the evidence available at the time, including the lack of witnesses and the supervisor's testimony regarding the weight of the forms. This constitutes a "reasonable basis" for controverting the claim under *Banks* and *Williams v. Louisiana Painting & Drywall*, 941 So. 2d 634, 638 (La. App. 1st Cir. 2006).

**2. Conclusion on Penalties and Fees**

Because the employer had a reasonable basis for its denial based on the factual disputes surrounding the accident, the denial was not arbitrary, capricious, or without probable cause. The trial court correctly denied the claim for penalties and attorney's fees.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Office of Workers’ Compensation judge is affirmed in its entirety. The trial court's finding that the claimant sustained a work-related accident is not manifestly erroneous. Furthermore, the trial court correctly denied the claim for penalties and attorney's fees, as the employer established a reasonable basis for denying the claim based on the information available at the time of the denial.
