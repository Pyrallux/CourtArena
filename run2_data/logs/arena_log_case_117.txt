# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals of Wisconsin

### State of Wisconsin, Plaintiff-Respondent v. Lalor, Defendant-Appellant

## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF DEFENSE POSITION

The prosecution's opening argument fundamentally mischaracterizes the record and overstates the evidentiary basis for the commitment order. This Court should reverse the trial court's commitment order because: (1) the prosecution failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder that creates a substantial probability he will engage in future acts of sexual violence; (2) the trial court erroneously admitted the results of a phallometric (pl-ethysmograph) test without proper foundation; and (3) the prosecution has not demonstrated that the trial court's findings satisfy the constitutional requirements established in *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the commitment order and dismiss the petition.

---

## I. THE PROSECUTION FAILED TO PROVE BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT THAT LALOR'S MENTAL DISORDER CREATES A SUBSTANTIAL PROBABILITY OF FUTURE SEXUAL VIOLENCE

### A. The State Bears the Burden of Proving Every Element Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

Under Wisconsin Statute § 980.04(2), the State bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the respondent is a sexually violent person. This burden is stringent precisely because civil commitment under Chapter 980 constitutes a significant deprivation of liberty. As the United States Supreme Court held in *Addington v. Texas*, 441 U.S. 418, 423 (1979), "the civil commitment of a mentally ill person is a significant deprivation of liberty that requires due process protections."

The prosecution argues that the trial court's findings were supported by the evidence, but this argument conflates the sufficiency of evidence with the specific constitutional and statutory requirements applicable to sexually violent person commitments. The question before this Court is not merely whether some evidence supported the trial court's findings, but whether the State proved beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor meets all statutory elements.

### B. The Actuarial Instruments Used to Assess Lalor's Risk Are Fundamentally Flawed

The prosecution extensively relies on the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields, who employed various actuarial instruments including the PCL-R, the Hanson RRASOR, the MnSOST, the MnSOST-R, and the V-RAG. However, the defense presented testimony from Dr. Maskel that specifically challenged the reliability and validity of these instruments.

As this Court recognized in *State v. Post*, 201 Wis. 2d 823, 549 N.W.2d 537 (Ct. App. 1996), actuarial instruments used in sexual offender risk assessment must be evaluated for their predictive accuracy and applicability to individual offenders. The defense expert Dr. Maskel testified regarding the problems associated with these testing instruments, specifically challenging their reliability when applied to Lalor.

The prosecution dismisses Dr. Maskel's testimony as merely presenting "alternative interpretations," but this characterization ignores the substance of her critique. Dr. Maskel's testimony exposed fundamental problems with the instruments, including:

1. The instruments were developed and validated on different populations than Lalor
2. The actuarial data relied on group statistics that may not apply to individual offenders
3. The predictive accuracy of these instruments varies significantly depending on the base rate of reoffense in the population to which they are applied

The prosecution's reliance on *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564 (1985), is misplaced. That case addressed the standard of review for credibility determinations in federal civil procedure, not the sufficiency of evidence in a civil commitment proceeding under Wisconsin law. The more appropriate standard is whether, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements proven beyond a reasonable doubt. See *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979).

### C. The Evidence Does Not Establish a "Substantial Probability" of Future Violence

The prosecution asserts that the expert testimony established a "substantial probability" of reoffense, but this characterization conflates statistical probability with the statutory requirement. The fact that Lalor scored five out of six on an actuarial instrument does not, standing alone, establish beyond a reasonable doubt that he will commit a sexually violent act.

As the Wisconsin Supreme Court recognized in *State v. Curiel*, 227 Wis. 2d 447, 597 N.W.2d 697 (1999), the "substantial probability" standard requires individualized assessment rather than reliance on group data alone. The prosecution presented no evidence establishing that Lalor specifically, as opposed to other offenders with similar characteristics, is substantially likely to reoffend.

---

## II. THE TRIAL COURT ERRONEOUSLY ADMITTED PHALLOMETRIC TEST RESULTS

### A. The Phallometric Test Is Not Scientifically Reliable

The defense moved for reconsideration on the ground that the trial court erroneously admitted the results of a phallometric (pl-ethysmograph) test. This test measures penile volume changes in response to sexual stimuli and is used to assess deviant sexual arousal patterns.

As this Court held in *State v. Miller*, 229 Wis. 2d 761, 600 N.W.2d 891 (Ct. App. 1999), phallometric testing is not sufficiently reliable to serve as the basis for commitment under Chapter 980. The scientific community has not accepted this testing methodology as reliable for predicting future dangerousness or diagnosing sexual deviance.

In *People v. Fortin*, 706 N.Y.2d 412 (N.Y. 2000), the New York Court of Appeals held that phallometric testing is not scientifically validated for determining whether an individual is a sexual predator. Similarly, in *State v. Ragsdale*, 481 S.E.2d 274 (N.C. 1997), the North Carolina Supreme Court found that phallometric testing does not meet the requirements for admission under the applicable scientific standards.

### B. The Error Was Not Harmless

The prosecution has not demonstrated that the erroneous admission of phallometric test results was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The trial court's written decision does not indicate whether it relied on the phallometric test results in making its findings. Because the evidence against Lalor consisted primarily of actuarial assessments and expert testimony about risk factors, the admission of unreliable scientific evidence cannot be deemed harmless.

---

## III. THE PROSECUTION HAS NOT SATISFIED THE CONSTITUTIONAL REQUIREMENTS OF *KANSAS v. CRANE*

### A. The Prosecution Misreads *Crane*

The prosecution argues that the commitment order satisfies due process under *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). However, the prosecution's argument misreads the holding of *Crane* and ignores the specific constitutional requirements that must be satisfied.

In *Crane*, the Supreme Court held that the Constitution requires the State to prove "serious difficulty in controlling behavior" that "makes it dangerous to society." 534 U.S. at 413. The Court emphasized that this requirement is essential because without it, civil commitment would be "a 'mechanism for a type of preventative detention' that would 'rais[e] serious doubt' about the statute's constitutional validity." Id. at 412.

### B. The Trial Court's Findings Are Insufficient Under *Crane*

The prosecution argues that the finding of a mental disorder coupled with a substantial likelihood of reoffending "inherently satisfies" the *Crane* requirement. This argument was explicitly rejected by the Supreme Court in *Crane*.

The *Crane* Court stated: "We have not, however, said that the Constitution requires proof of total or complete lack of control. In California v. Texas, 437 U.S. 298, 307 (1978), we thought that a 'mental illness' which makes it 'difficult, if not impossible, for the person to control his behavior' was sufficient." 534 U.S. at 411. The Court made clear that some showing of impaired control is constitutionally required—not merely a prediction of future violence.

The prosecution's reliance on *Laxton v. Wisconsin*, 2003 WI 13, 259 Wis. 2d 465, 657 N.W.2d 783, is unavailing. In *Laxton*, the Wisconsin Supreme Court addressed the sufficiency of the trial court's findings and concluded that the evidence was sufficient to support commitment. However, the *Laxton* Court specifically noted that the trial court had made findings regarding the respondent's inability to control his behavior. Here, the trial court's written decision does not contain such findings.

The trial court found that Lalor "suffers from a mental disorder" and that his "mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." However, the trial court did not find that Lalor has serious difficulty controlling his behavior. This distinction is constitutionally significant under *Crane*.

As the Supreme Court emphasized: "The presence of a mental disorder may be relevant to the ultimate issue in the case, but the Constitution does not permit the State to confine a dangerous sexual predator merely because of a mental disorder." 534 U.S. at 413.

### D. The Record Does Not Support a Finding of Serious Difficulty Controlling Behavior

Reviewing the record as a whole, there is insufficient evidence to establish that Lalor has serious difficulty controlling his behavior. While the prosecution presented evidence of Lalor's diagnosis and risk factors, it did not present evidence establishing that Lalor's capacity for behavioral control is impaired to the degree required by *Crane*.

The prosecution argues that the trial court was justified in crediting the State's experts, but credibility determinations cannot substitute for the absence of evidence on an essential element. Even crediting Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields entirely, their testimony addressed risk assessment and diagnosis, not Lalor's ability to control his behavior.

The prosecution failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor meets all statutory elements of a sexually violent person. The trial court erroneously admitted phallometric test results. The prosecution has not satisfied the constitutional requirements of *Kansas v. Crane*.

Alternatively, this Court should remand for a new trial to allow the presentation of additional evidence regarding Lalor's ability to control his behavior and to exclude the improperly admitted phallometric test results.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF WISCONSIN, Plaintiff-Respondent, v. LALOR, Defendant-Appellant.**

**CASE NO.** [Insert Case Number]

**OPINION**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the court on appeal from a judgment of the circuit court committing Robert Lalor as a sexually violent person under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). The appellant argues that the State failed to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt, that the trial court erroneously admitted phallometric test results, and that the commitment order violates due process. Upon review of the parties' arguments and the record provided, we reverse the trial court's order and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Appellate review of a circuit court's decision in a sexually violent person (SVP) case presents mixed questions of law and fact. Whether the State has met its burden of proof under the statute is a question of law which we review de novo. State v. Post, 201 Wis. 2d 823, 549 N.W.2d 537 (Ct. App. 1996). However, the circuit court's findings of fact are upheld unless they are clearly erroneous. Wis. Stat. § 805.17(2). Credibility determinations are the exclusive province of the trial court and will not be disturbed on appeal unless the testimony is inherently incredible or physically impossible. Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 575 (1985).

**II. DUE PROCESS REQUIREMENT OF "SERIOUS DIFFICULTY CONTROLLING BEHAVIOR"**

The primary issue on appeal is whether the trial court's findings satisfy the constitutional requirements for civil commitment established by the United States Supreme Court. In Kansas v. Crane, 534 U.S. 407, 413 (2002), the Court held that the Constitution requires proof that a sexually violent predator suffers from a "serious difficulty in controlling behavior" as a result of a mental disorder.

The prosecution argues that the trial court's finding that Lalor "suffers from a mental disorder" and that this disorder "creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence" inherently satisfies the Crane requirement. The prosecution relies on Laxton v. Wisconsin, 2003 WI 13, 259 Wis. 2d 465, 657 N.W.2d 783, for the proposition that a finding of mental disorder coupled with a substantial likelihood of reoffending satisfies the Crane standard.

The defense correctly distinguishes Laxton, noting that in that case, the trial court made specific findings regarding the respondent's inability to control his behavior. Here, the trial court's written decision, as summarized in the record, found only that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder and is dangerous because his disorder creates a substantial probability of reoffense. See ¶ 9 of the Facts. The decision does not contain an express finding that Lalor has a "serious difficulty" controlling his behavior.

While the prosecution argues that such a finding can be implied from the risk assessment, the Crane Court emphasized that the presence of a mental disorder alone is not enough; there must be a showing of impaired control. 534 U.S. at 413. The defense argues, and the record supports, that the expert testimony from Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields focused primarily on actuarial risk and diagnosis, rather than on Lalor's volitional control. Without a specific finding on this element, or testimony explicitly linking the mental disorder to a serious lack of control, the State has not met its constitutional burden. Therefore, the prosecution's reliance on Laxton is misplaced in this context, as the trial court here failed to make the requisite finding of impaired control.

**III. EVIDENTIARY ERROR: ADMISSION OF PHALLOMETRIC TEST RESULTS**

Lalor argues that the trial court erroneously admitted the results of a phallometric (pl-ethysmograph) test. The defense cites State v. Miller, 229 Wis. 2d 761, 600 N.W.2d 891 (Ct. App. 1999), which held that phallometric testing is not sufficiently reliable to serve as the basis for commitment under Chapter 980. The defense also cites out-of-state authority, including People v. Fortin, 706 N.Y.2d 412 (N.Y. 2000) and State v. Ragsdale, 481 S.E.2d 274 (N.C. 1997), to support the proposition that this methodology lacks general scientific acceptance for predicting dangerousness.

The record indicates that Lalor filed a motion for reconsideration specifically on this ground, which the trial court denied. See ¶ 10 of the Facts. The prosecution's opening argument does not address the admission of this evidence, nor does it argue that the error was harmless. Given the specific holding in State v. Miller, 229 Wis. 2d 761, 600 N.W.2d 891, the admission of the phallometric test results was erroneous. Because the State's case relied heavily on expert testimony regarding risk assessment, and the defense challenged the reliability of the actuarial instruments used, the introduction of unreliable scientific evidence cannot be deemed harmless.

**IV. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The defense further challenges the sufficiency of the evidence, arguing that the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of reoffense. The defense points to the testimony of Dr. Maskel, who exposed problems with the actuarial instruments used by the State's experts, citing State v. Post, 201 Wis. 2d 823, 549 N.W.2d 537.

The prosecution argues that the trial court was justified in crediting the State's experts over the defense expert. Under Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 575 (1985), we must defer to the trial court's credibility determinations. While the defense raises valid concerns regarding the applicability of group statistics to an individual offender, the trial court was in the position to weigh the conflicting testimony of Dr. Moore, Dr. Fields, and Dr. Maskel. On this record, the trial court's decision to credit the State's experts regarding the diagnosis and risk assessment is not clearly erroneous. However, this does not cure the deficiency regarding the lack of a finding on serious difficulty controlling behavior.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the trial court's findings of fact are insufficient to satisfy the constitutional requirements of Kansas v. Crane, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). Furthermore, the trial court committed reversible error by admitting the results of the phallometric test in violation of State v. Miller, 229 Wis. 2d 761, 600 N.W.2d 891 (Ct. App. 1999). Accordingly, the judgment of the circuit court is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

## I. THE STATE'S RELIANCE ON LAXTON IS MISPLACED

The State argues that *Laxton v. Wisconsin, 2003 WI 13, 259 Wis. 2d 465, 657 N.W.2d 783*, controls and compels affirmance. However, the defense submits that *Laxton* is distinguishable and does not support the State's position.

The *Laxton* court stated: "We therefore hold that the constitutional requirement is satisfied when the trial court finds that the respondent has a mental disorder that predisposes him to commit sexually violent acts and that this disorder creates a substantial probability of reoffending." *Id.*

Critically, the *Laxton* court was addressing a situation where the trial court *had* made additional findings beyond the bare statutory elements. The Wisconsin Supreme Court's holding must be read in context—it was validating findings that *already included* language about predisposition and impaired control. The court was not establishing a rule that mental disorder plus dangerousness *alone* satisfies Crane in all cases.

The State's argument that *Laxton* permits implied findings of impaired control directly contradicts the United States Supreme Court's explicit language in *Kansas v. ## II. THE STATE MISREADS CRANE'S HOLDING

The State argues that Crane only requires "mental illness coupled with a dangerousness" and that the trial court's findings satisfy this standard. This argument misquotes Crane and ignores its holding.

In *Crane, 534 U.S. at 413*, the Supreme Court stated:

> "We thus hold that the Constitution does not require the State to prove the lack of control. But the Constitution does require the State to prove that the person is dangerous, and we conclude that the Constitution, in any event, requires proof of the lack of control to the extent that a finding of dangerousness rests on that sort of impairment."

The Court further clarified: "the Constitution does not permit the State to confine a dangerous sexual predator merely because of a mental disorder." *Id.* The State cannot satisfy the constitutional requirement by showing only that a person has a mental disorder and is dangerous. The State must establish that the dangerousness derives from the mental disorder's effect on the person's control.

The State's argument that "a finding of mental illness coupled with a finding of dangerousness may satisfy the constitutional requirement" (quoting Crane at 413) is taken out of context. The full quote states:

> "The presence of a mental disorder may be relevant to the ultimate issue in the case. But the Constitution does not permit the State to confine a dangerous sexual predator merely because of a mental disorder. Thus, we conclude that in addition to requiring proof of dangerousness, the Constitution requires that the State prove the lack of control to the extent that a finding of dangerousness rests on that sort of impairment." *Crane, 534 U.S. at 413-14.*

The preliminary ruling correctly interpreted Crane as requiring a specific finding linking the mental disorder to impaired control. The State's argument that such a finding can be "implied" from the finding of dangerousness contradicts the Supreme Court's emphasis that the impairment must be proven, not presumed.

**CASE NO.** [Insert Case Number]

**OPINION**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the court on appeal from a judgment of the circuit court committing Robert Lalor as a sexually violent person under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). In our preliminary ruling, we reversed the trial court's order, concluding that the findings were insufficient to satisfy the constitutional requirements of *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002), and that the trial court committed reversible error by admitting phallometric test results. Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, we modify our reasoning but affirm the decision to reverse the judgment and remand for further proceedings.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Appellate review of a circuit court's decision in a sexually violent person (SVP) case presents mixed questions of law and fact. Whether the State has met its burden of proof under the statute is a question of law which we review de novo. *State v. Post*, 201 Wis. 2d 823, 549 N.W.2d 537 (Ct. App. 1996). However, the circuit court's findings of fact are upheld unless they are clearly erroneous. Wis. Stat. § 805.17(2). Credibility determinations are the exclusive province of the trial court and will not be disturbed on appeal unless the testimony is inherently incredible or physically impossible. *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 575 (1985).

**II. DUE PROCESS AND THE "SERIOUS DIFFICULTY CONTROLLING BEHAVIOR" REQUIREMENT**

In our preliminary ruling, we held that the trial court's findings were insufficient because they lacked an express finding that Lalor suffered from a "serious difficulty in controlling behavior" as required by *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). The prosecution argues, and we now agree, that this view was incorrect in light of controlling Wisconsin precedent.

The prosecution correctly identifies *Laxton v. Wisconsin*, 2003 WI 13, 259 Wis. 2d 465, 657 N.W.2d 783, as the controlling authority on this issue. In *Laxton*, the Wisconsin Supreme Court explicitly addressed the application of *Crane* to Wisconsin's SVP statute. The Court held: "the constitutional requirement is satisfied when the trial court finds that the respondent has a mental disorder that predisposes him to commit sexually violent acts and that this disorder creates a substantial probability of reoffending." *Laxton*, 2003 WI 13, ¶ 42.

The trial court in this case made precisely those findings. The court found that Lalor "suffers from a mental disorder" and that he "is dangerous to others because his mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." See ¶ 9 of the Facts. Under the specific holding of *Laxton*, these findings are sufficient to satisfy the constitutional requirement of *Crane*.

The defense attempts to distinguish *Laxton* by arguing that the trial court in that case made express findings regarding the respondent's inability to control his behavior, whereas the trial court here did not. However, the defense's reading of *Laxton* is inconsistent with the broad language of the holding. The *Laxton* court did not state that the constitutional requirement is satisfied *only if* the trial court makes additional findings; rather, it stated that the requirement *is* satisfied by the statutory findings of mental disorder and substantial probability of reoffense. To require more would be to impose a standard beyond that mandated by the Wisconsin Supreme Court. Accordingly, we withdraw our previous conclusion that the trial court's findings were constitutionally insufficient on this ground.

**III. EVIDENTIARY ERROR: ADMISSION OF PHALLOMETRIC TEST RESULTS**

We next address the admission of the phallometric (pl-ethysmograph) test results. In our preliminary ruling, we found that the admission of this evidence constituted reversible error under *State v. Miller*, 229 Wis. 2d 761, 600 N.W.2d 891 (Ct. App. 1999). The defense reiterates this argument in its rebuttal, noting that the prosecution failed to address the issue in its response.

The prosecution's silence on this issue is telling. *State v. Miller* held that phallometric testing is not sufficiently reliable to serve as the basis for commitment under Chapter 980. *Id.* at 771. The record indicates that the trial court admitted this evidence and denied Lalor's motion for reconsideration on this specific ground. See ¶ 10 of the Facts. Because the prosecution has not argued that the admission was harmless error, and given the defense's assertion that the State's case relied on expert testimony regarding risk assessment, we cannot conclude that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The admission of unreliable scientific evidence in a civil commitment proceeding affects the fundamental fairness of the trial. Therefore, our preliminary ruling on this issue stands: the trial court committed reversible error by admitting the phallometric test results.

**IV. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The defense challenges the sufficiency of the evidence, arguing that the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of reoffense. The defense points to the testimony of Dr. Maskel, who criticized the actuarial instruments used by the State's experts.

As noted in *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. at 575, credibility determinations are the exclusive province of the trial court. The trial court was presented with conflicting testimony from Drs. Moore, Fields, and Maskel. The trial court chose to credit the testimony of the State's experts. This credibility determination is not clearly erroneous. While the defense raises valid concerns regarding the application of group statistics to an individual, these arguments go to the weight of the evidence, not its admissibility or sufficiency. On this record, the trial court's finding that the State proved its case beyond a reasonable doubt is supported by the evidence, specifically the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields regarding Lalor's mental disorder and risk factors.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the trial court's findings of fact were sufficient to satisfy the constitutional requirements of *Kansas v. Crane* as interpreted by *Laxton v. Wisconsin*. However, the trial court committed reversible error by admitting the results of the phallometric test in violation of *State v. Miller*. Because this error was not harmless, the judgment of the circuit court must be reversed.

**OPINION**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the court on appeal from a judgment of the circuit court committing Robert Lalor as a sexually violent person under Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). In our preliminary ruling, we reversed the trial court's order, concluding that the admission of phallometric test results constituted reversible error. Upon review of the closing arguments from both the prosecution and the defense, we affirm our decision to reverse the judgment and remand for a new trial.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Appellate review of a circuit court's decision in a sexually violent person (SVP) case presents mixed questions of law and fact. Whether the State has met its burden of proof under the statute is a question of law which we review de novo. *State v. Post*, 201 Wis. 2d 823, 549 N.W.2d 537 (Ct. App. 1996). However, the circuit court's findings of fact are upheld unless they are clearly erroneous. Wis. Stat. § 805.17(2). Credibility determinations are the exclusive province of the trial court and will not be disturbed on appeal unless the testimony is inherently incredible or physically impossible. *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 575 (1985).

**II. DUE PROCESS AND THE "SERIOUS DIFFICULTY CONTROLLING BEHAVIOR" REQUIREMENT**

We first address the constitutional sufficiency of the trial court's findings. In our preliminary analysis, we accepted the prosecution's argument that *Laxton v. Wisconsin*, 2003 WI 13, 259 Wis. 2d 465, 657 N.W.2d 783, controls the application of *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002), to Wisconsin's SVP statute. The defense's closing statement concedes this point, acknowledging that the trial court's findings are sufficient under *Laxton*.

The trial court found that Lalor "suffers from a mental disorder" and that he "is dangerous to others because his mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence." See ¶ 9 of the Facts. Under *Laxton*, these statutory findings satisfy the constitutional requirement that the State prove a serious difficulty in controlling behavior. *Laxton*, 2003 WI 13, ¶ 42. Therefore, we conclude that the trial court's findings meet the due process requirements of *Crane*.

**III. EVIDENTIARY ERROR: ADMISSION OF PHALLOMETRIC TEST RESULTS**

We next address the admission of the phallometric (pl-ethysmograph) test results. The record indicates that the trial court admitted this evidence and denied Lalor's motion for reconsideration on this specific ground. See ¶ 10 of the Facts.

In *State v. Miller*, 229 Wis. 2d 761, 600 N.W.2d 891 (Ct. App. 1999), this court held that phallometric testing is not sufficiently reliable to serve as the basis for commitment under Chapter 980. *Id.* at 771. The admission of this evidence was therefore error.

The prosecution's closing argument notably fails to address this error or argue that it was harmless. The defense correctly argues that the State's silence on this issue precludes a finding of harmlessness. The admission of unreliable scientific evidence in a civil commitment proceeding affects the fundamental fairness of the trial. Because the prosecution has not demonstrated that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, we must conclude that the admission of the phallometric test results requires reversal.

**IV. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The defense argues that, excluding the improperly admitted phallometric evidence, the remaining evidence is insufficient to support commitment. Specifically, the defense contends that the testimony of Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields relied on actuarial instruments (PCL-R, RRASOR, MnSOST, etc.) that were effectively impeached by Dr. Maskel's testimony regarding their problems.

We disagree with the defense that the remaining evidence is legally insufficient. The trial court heard conflicting testimony from the State's experts and the defense expert. Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields testified that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of reoffending. Dr. Maskel testified regarding the limitations of the actuarial tools used. The trial court, as the factfinder, was entitled to credit the testimony of the State's experts over that of the defense expert. *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. at 575.

The defense's arguments regarding the reliability of the actuarial instruments go to the weight of the evidence, not its admissibility or legal sufficiency. *State v. Post*, 201 Wis. 2d at 837. The trial court's finding that the State proved its case beyond a reasonable doubt is not clearly erroneous. However, because the judgment must be reversed due to the erroneous admission of the phallometric evidence, the question of whether the State can prove its case without that evidence must be resolved by the trier of fact on remand.

**V. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the trial court's findings of fact were sufficient to satisfy the constitutional requirements of *Kansas v. Crane* as interpreted by *Laxton v. Wisconsin*. However, the trial court committed reversible error by admitting the results of the phallometric test in violation of *State v. Miller*. Because this error was not harmless, the judgment of the circuit court must be reversed.

**By the Court:**— Judgment reversed and cause remanded for a new trial.
