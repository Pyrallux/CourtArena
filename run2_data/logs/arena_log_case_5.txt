# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SUPREME COURT OF THE STATE OF HAWAII

### STATE OF HAWAII, Plaintiff-Appellant

### v. DAVID A. HAILI, Defendant-Appellee

---

## INTRODUCTION

The State of Hawaii seeks to reverse the circuit court's well-reasoned order granting the motion to suppress evidence. The defense respectfully submits that the circuit court correctly determined that the officers' actions violated the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, Section 7 of the Hawaii Constitution. The prosecution's arguments, while creative, fail to establish the constitutional validity of the search and seizure at issue. This Court should affirm the suppression order for the reasons set forth below.

---

## I. THE INITIAL STOP OF THE VEHICLE WAS UNCONSTITUTIONAL DUE TO THE ABSENCE OF REASONABLE SUSPICION

The prosecution contends that Officer Davis had sufficient justification to stop the vehicle based on a police bulletin regarding a "reported incident" at the Kapaa Quarry. This argument fails because the record establishes that the security guard "furnished no other information" when reporting the incident. A police bulletin based on such a barebones report cannot provide the reasonable suspicion required for an investigative stop.

In *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), the Supreme Court held that a police officer may stop an individual for investigative purposes only if the officer has "reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot." The Court emphasized that the officer must be able to "point to specific facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." Id. at 21-22.

Here, the prosecution asks this Court to infer criminal activity from the mere fact that a vehicle was traveling on an uncompleted freeway at night. However, the prosecution has cited no authority establishing that the mere presence of a vehicle on an uncompleted roadway at night constitutes sufficient grounds for an investigative stop. The fact that the roadway was "uncompleted" does not automatically render its use unlawful, particularly where, as here, there is no evidence that any signage or barriers prohibited access. The officers had no specific information that the vehicle was involved in the reported incident at the quarry. The bulletin provided no description of vehicles, suspects, or criminal activity—it merely communicated that an unspecified "incident" had been reported.

The prosecution further claims that Defendant's five-second hesitation in exiting the vehicle "heightened" the officers' suspicion. This argument is unpersuasive. The time period was minimal—five seconds—and the Defendant did ultimately comply with the officer's command. A momentary hesitation, without more, does not transform a lawful order into a reasonable suspicion. As the Supreme Court noted in *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 7 (1989), the totality of circumstances must be considered, but those circumstances must actually suggest criminal activity. Here, the totality established nothing more than a vehicle on an unfinished roadway—hardly the stuff of reasonable suspicion.

---

## II. THE PAT-DOWN SEARCH OF THE OCCUPANTS WAS UNLAWFUL

The prosecution does not meaningfully address the "pat down" of the four occupants, presumably because it cannot justify this action under any recognized exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement. The officers conducted a pat-down without any articulable basis to believe that the occupants were armed and dangerous.

In *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 24 (1968), the Court held that a frisk or pat-down is permissible only "where a police officer observes unusual conduct which leads him reasonably to conclude in light of his experience that criminal activity may be afoot and that the persons with whom he is dealing may be armed and presently dangerous." The officer must have "specific reasonable inferences" that the individual is armed, not merely a generalized concern for officer safety. *Id.* at 27.

Here, there was no evidence that any occupant made threatening movements, possessed weapons, or engaged in conduct suggesting danger. The vehicle was stopped based on nothing more than a vague bulletin. The occupants complied with commands to exit the vehicle. The Defendant's brief hesitation, without more, cannot support a reasonable belief that he was armed and dangerous. The pat-down was therefore unconstitutional, and any evidence derived from the unlawful pat-down must be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. *See Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 487-88 (1963).

---

## III. THE FLASHLIGHT OBSERVATION CONSTITUTED A SEARCH REQUIRING PROBABLE CAUSE

The prosecution relies heavily on *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), to argue that the flashlight use was permissible. However, the prosecution misreads that decision and ignores critical distinctions.

In *Texas v. Brown*, the officer was lawfully positioned outside the vehicle and used a flashlight to observe what was already in plain view. The Court held that this did not constitute a search because the officer "was not physically inside the vehicle with the defendant and did not disturb the defendant's effects." 460 U.S. at 740. The key to the decision was that the officer was lawfully positioned and was merely enhancing his ability to observe what was already exposed to plain view.

However, the prosecution's argument proves too much. In *Texas v. Brown*, the Court also emphasized that the "plain view" doctrine requires that the officer have probable cause to believe the item is contraband or evidence of a crime before seizing it. 460 U.S. at 741-42. The doctrine of plain view "cannot be used to extend a general exploratory search from one object to another." *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443, 466 (1971).

Here, the critical question is not whether the flashlight use itself constituted a search, but rather whether the officers had probable cause to believe the vehicle contained contraband *before* they began their search. The prosecution argues that the officers observed the rifle casing "upon scanning the interior with a flashlight." But this observation was the result of an exploratory scan of the vehicle's interior—an action that requires probable cause under the Fourth Amendment.

The prosecution conflates two separate Fourth Amendment concepts: (1) the plain view observation of contraband that is already exposed to view, and (2) the exploratory examination of a vehicle's interior to locate contraband. The former requires only that the officer be lawfully positioned; the latter requires probable cause. *See California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386, 392 (1985) (explaining that the automobile exception requires probable cause before a search).

Even if the flashlight use is viewed as permissible under *Texas v. Brown*, the officers still lacked probable cause at the time they began their search. The prosecution argues that probable cause arose from the "suspicious location" and the Defendant's five-second hesitation. These facts, taken together, do not establish probable cause to believe that the vehicle contained a firearm. The mere presence of a vehicle on an uncompleted roadway at night is not indicative of any specific crime, and a five-second hesitation in exiting a vehicle is not evidence of criminal activity. The officers had no information connecting this particular vehicle to any criminal conduct.

---

## IV. THE AUTOMOBILE EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY BECAUSE PROBABLE CAUSE DID NOT EXIST

The prosecution invokes the automobile exception to justify the warrantless search. This argument fails because probable cause did not exist at the time the search commenced.

The automobile exception, as articulated in *California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386, 392 (1985), permits a warrantless search of a vehicle "when the officer has probable cause to believe that the vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime." Probable cause is a "practical, nontechnical" concept that requires "facts sufficient for a prudent person to conclude that the vehicle contained contraband." *See United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 809 (1982).

The prosecution argues that probable cause arose from the combination of: (1) the suspicious location; (2) the Defendant's hesitation; and (3) the discovery of the rifle casing. However, this analysis is flawed because it relies on evidence obtained during an unlawful search to justify that very search. The rifle casing was discovered only after the officers unlawfully scanned the vehicle's interior without probable cause. The officers cannot rely on the fruits of an unlawful search to establish the probable cause necessary to justify that search. *See Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533, 542 (1988) (explaining that evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is generally inadmissible).

Moreover, even accepting the prosecution's facts, they do not establish probable cause. The fact that a vehicle was on an uncompleted freeway at night does not suggest the presence of contraband. The Defendant's five-second hesitation does not indicate the presence of a firearm. Together, these facts might justify further investigation—a request for information, a request to see a driver's license—but they do not rise to the level of probable cause required for a search.

The prosecution's reliance on *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), is misplaced. In *Gates*, the Court found probable cause based on a detailed tip from a reliable informant, observations of drug paraphernalia, and other corroborating evidence. Here, by contrast, the officers had no reliable information connecting the vehicle to criminal activity, no observations of contraband until after the unlawful search began, and no corroboration of any tip.

---

## V. EVEN IF PROBABLE CAUSE EXISTED, THE SCOPE OF THE SEARCH EXCEEDED CONSTITUTIONAL LIMITS

Assuming arguendo that probable cause existed to believe the vehicle contained a firearm, the officers' actions still exceeded the scope of a permissible search under the automobile exception.

In *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982), the Supreme Court held that when probable cause exists, officers may search "any part of the vehicle and its contents that might conceal the object of the search." However, this authority is not unlimited. The search must still be conducted in a reasonable manner and must be limited to areas where the contraband might be found.

Here, the officers observed a .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor. This observation, even if it provided probable cause, would justify a search of the front area of the vehicle. It would not justify pushing the front seat forward to examine a black case on the rear floor, much less opening a ukulele case found within that black case.

The prosecution argues that the officers could search "any part of the vehicle in which the object of the search might be found." However, this argument ignores the requirement that the search be tied to the particular contraband sought. A rifle casing suggests the presence of a rifle, but it does not provide probable cause to believe that a modified rifle would be found in a ukulele case in the rear of the vehicle. The officers' actions amounted to a general exploratory search of the entire vehicle, which exceeds the scope permitted under the automobile exception. *See Ross*, 456 U.S. at 809 (emphasizing that the scope of a warrantless search must be "defined by the object of the search").

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence. The officers lacked reasonable suspicion to stop the vehicle, lacked probable cause to search the vehicle, and exceeded the scope of any permissible search. The evidence obtained from the vehicle was the fruit of an unconstitutional search and seizure, and its suppression was proper.

The prosecution's attempt to retroactively justify each step of the investigation by reference to evidence obtained during earlier constitutional violations must be rejected. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures, and the protections apply with full force whether the investigation concerns a firearm or any other contraband. The circuit court correctly applied these principles, and its order should be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF HAWAII, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Defendant-Appellee.**

**NO. **

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

**B. K.**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence. The State of Hawaii appeals, arguing that the evidence seized from Defendant’s vehicle was obtained lawfully under the Fourth Amendment. After reviewing the record, the arguments of counsel, and the applicable legal precedents, this Court finds that the circuit court properly granted the motion to suppress. The officers lacked the reasonable suspicion necessary to justify the initial stop, and consequently, the evidence obtained as a result of that stop is inadmissible.

**I. THE INITIAL STOP LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION**

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures. A brief investigative stop of a vehicle constitutes a "seizure" within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment and must be justified by reasonable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot. *See Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 653 (1979). In *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), the Supreme Court held that an officer may stop a person only if the officer has "specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion."

Here, the prosecution argues that Officer Davis had reasonable suspicion based on two factors: (1) a police bulletin regarding a "reported incident" at the Kapaa Quarry, and (2) the observation of a vehicle traveling on an uncompleted freeway at night. The Court finds these arguments insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion.

First, the record indicates that the security guard’s report contained no specific information regarding the nature of the "incident," the suspects, or a vehicle description. A police bulletin based solely on a vague report of an "incident" at a specific location cannot provide the particularized suspicion required to stop any vehicle found in the general vicinity. As the Supreme Court noted in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), probable cause—and by extension reasonable suspicion—requires a "substantial chance" that criminal activity is occurring, not mere conjecture. The bulletin here provided no nexus between the reported incident and the specific vehicle stopped by Officer Davis.

Second, while the presence of a vehicle on an uncompleted freeway at night is undoubtedly unusual, the facts provided do not establish that such presence is, in itself, criminal. The prosecution asserts that "no lawful traffic should exist" on an uncompleted freeway. However, the defense correctly points out that the record is devoid of any evidence indicating that the roadway was posted with signs prohibiting entry or that the area was restricted by law. Without such evidence, the mere fact that a vehicle is driving on an uncompleted roadway does not equate to specific facts of criminal activity. The vehicle was traveling at a reasonable speed with its headlights on, behavior consistent with a driver who may have inadvertently turned onto the roadway rather than a person engaged in criminal conduct.

The prosecution further relies on Defendant’s five-second hesitation to exit the vehicle as a factor heightening suspicion. However, under *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 7 (1989), the totality of the circumstances must be viewed through the lens of a reasonable person. A momentary delay in complying with an officer's command, standing alone, is insufficient to transform an otherwise innocuous situation into one justifying a seizure. *See United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 444, 554 (1980) (Stewart, J., concurring). Because the initial stop was not supported by specific and articulable facts suggesting criminal activity, it violated the Fourth Amendment.

**II. THE PAT-DOWN AND SUBSEQUENT SEARCH WERE UNLAWFUL**

Because the initial stop was unconstitutional, the evidence obtained thereafter must be analyzed as the fruit of that unlawful intrusion. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963). The prosecution attempts to justify the search of the vehicle under the "automobile exception" and the "plain view" doctrine. However, these doctrines presuppose that the officer’s presence and initial observations are lawful.

The prosecution cites *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983), for the proposition that the use of a flashlight to illuminate a vehicle’s interior does not constitute a search. While this statement of law is correct, it applies only when the officer is lawfully present. *See id.* at 739-40. Since Officer Davis lacked reasonable suspicion to stop the vehicle, his subsequent detention of the occupants and his examination of the vehicle’s interior were unlawful. Therefore, the observation of the .22-caliber rifle casing cannot be used to retroactively justify the stop or establish probable cause. *See Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533, 542 (1988) (evidence observed during an unlawful entry cannot be used to establish probable cause).

Furthermore, the officers’ decision to conduct a pat-down search of the occupants was unsupported by the facts. Under *Terry*, a frisk is permitted only when an officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27. The record contains no evidence of threatening behavior, furtive movements, or any indication that the occupants were armed. The State’s reliance on the Defendant’s brief hesitation is insufficient to meet this standard.

**III. THE SCOPE OF THE SEARCH EXCEEDED CONSTITUTIONAL LIMITS**

Even assuming, arguendo, that the discovery of the rifle casing provided probable cause to search for a weapon, the scope of the search conducted by the officers exceeded the limits of the automobile exception. In *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982), the Supreme Court held that probable cause to search a vehicle justifies a search of "every part of the vehicle and its contents that may conceal the object of the search." However, this authority is not limitless.

The officers observed a rifle casing on the front floor. While this might justify a search of the front compartment for a weapon, it does not automatically justify a search of a closed container located in the rear of the vehicle that bears no obvious connection to the observed casing. The prosecution argues that a rifle could be hidden in a ukulele case. While this is physically possible, the officers’ decision to push the front seat forward to access a black case on the rear floor, and then to open a latched ukulele case within it, constituted a general exploratory search rather than a search for a specific object linked to the probable cause. *See Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332, 351 (2009) (emphasizing that the scope of a search must be tied to the rationale for its allowance). The defense correctly notes that the officers’ actions amounted to a fishing expedition unsupported by the specific facts known to them at the inception of the search.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the circuit court did not err in granting the motion to suppress. The officers lacked reasonable suspicion to justify the initial stop of the vehicle. Consequently, the pat-down search and the subsequent search of the vehicle were unconstitutional. The evidence obtained as a result of these violations is inadmissible.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the judgment of the circuit court is affirmed.

#### I. THE INITIAL STOP WAS SUPPORTED BY REASONABLE SUSPICION

The circuit court incorrectly concluded that the security guard's report and the vehicle's presence on the uncompleted freeway failed to establish reasonable suspicion. Under *United States v. The rifle casing indicated the presence of a firearm, making it reasonable to search for the rifle itself—including in closed containers like the ukulele case found in the rear. *See Ross*, 456 U.S. at 825 ("[T]he scope of the search is defined by the object of the search"). The officers were justified in ensuring their safety before conducting the flashlight scan, as the circumstances suggested potential violence. *See Michigan v. Carney*, 471 U.S. at 392 (automobile exception applies regardless of arrest status).

The circuit court erred in suppressing the evidence. The flashlight scan was lawful under *Texas v. Brown* and revealed the rifle casing, which provided probable cause for a full search under the automobile exception. ## I. THE INITIAL STOP LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION

The prosecution's argument that reasonable suspicion existed rests on a mischaracterization of the facts and an improper conflation of vague information with specific, articulable facts required by *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968).

### A. The Security Guard's Report Was Insufficient

The prosecution argues that the security guard's report of an "incident" at the Kapaa Quarry, combined with the vehicle's presence on the uncompleted freeway, established reasonable suspicion. However, the prosecution conflates the standard for reasonable suspicion with the higher standard of probable cause. As this Court correctly noted, the guard furnished "no other information"—no description of suspects, no description of vehicles, no indication of the nature of the criminal activity, and no indication that a firearm was involved. *See Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983) (requiring a "substantial chance" that criminal activity is occurring, not mere conjecture).

The prosecution cites *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 7-8 (1989), for the proposition that "unusual activity in a location where criminal activity is known to occur" can support reasonable suspicion. However, *Sokolow* involved specific, articulable facts: the defendant paid $2,100 in cash for two airline tickets, was traveling under an alias, had no checked luggage, and was extremely nervous. *Id.* at 3-4. Here, by contrast, the prosecution offers only that an unnamed guard reported an unspecified "incident" at a quarry. This is precisely the type of vague, unsubstantiated tip that *Gates* held insufficient to establish probable cause—and it is even more insufficient for the lower standard of reasonable suspicion.

### B. The Vehicle's Presence on the Uncompleted Freeway Did Not Create Reasonable Suspicion

The prosecution argues that the barricaded nature of the uncompleted freeway inherently created reasonable suspicion. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the record contains no evidence that "no lawful traffic should exist" on this roadway. The prosecution asserts that the chain-link fence "barricading the uncompleted end" proves the area was "restricted," but the facts indicate only that the freeway was uncompleted—not that it was legally closed to the public. The defense correctly points out that the record is devoid of any evidence indicating that the roadway was posted with signs prohibiting entry or that the area was restricted by law. An uncompleted freeway is not ipso facto a restricted area.

Second, even assuming the location was unusual, unusualness alone does not create reasonable suspicion. In *Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 663 (1979), the Supreme Court emphasized that reasonable suspicion requires "specific articulable facts" indicating criminal activity, not merely the absence of a legitimate reason for being in a particular place. Here, the vehicle was traveling at a reasonable speed with headlights on—behavior consistent with a driver who may have inadvertently turned onto the roadway. The prosecution asks the Court to infer criminal intent from the mere fact of presence in an unusual location, but *Terry* requires something more.

### C. The Five-Second Delay Did Not Create Reasonable Suspicion

The prosecution characterizes the defendant's five-second hesitation as "deliberate refusal to obey a lawful order" and "evasive behavior" that "heightened" suspicion. This argument is unpersuasive for three reasons.

First, the command to exit the vehicle was given during an already-unlawful stop. An officer cannot manufacture reasonable suspicion by issuing unlawful commands and then citing non-compliance as suspicious. *See Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 274 (2000) (holding that a Terry stop requires independent reasonable suspicion, not merely compliance with an officer's command).

Second, the prosecution's characterization of "deliberate refusal" is unsupported by the facts. The defendant was "leaning over and watching the officer"—hardly the behavior of someone attempting to evade or conceal. The five-second delay, while perhaps longer than ideal, is consistent with a momentary confusion or surprise, not evasive conduct.

Third, even if the delay were suspicious, it occurred *after* the stop. The question under *Terry* is whether reasonable suspicion existed *at the time of the stop*, not whether suspicious behavior occurred afterward. The prosecution conflates post-hoc rationalization with pre-existing suspicion, a distinction fatal to its argument.

## II. THE FLASHLIGHT OBSERVATION WAS NOT LAWFUL UNDER *TEXAS V. BROWN*

The prosecution argues that Officer Davis's flashlight scan of the vehicle's interior was permissible under *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983), and that the observation of the rifle casing provided probable cause for a full search. This argument fails because *Texas v. Brown* presupposes lawful police presence.

In *Texas v. Brown*, the Court held that "the use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle... does not violate the Fourth Amendment where the officer is lawfully positioned outside the vehicle." *Id.* at 740 (emphasis added). The prosecution omits this critical limitation. The doctrine of *Texas v. Brown* applies only when the officer is lawfully present at the vantage point from which the observation is made. Here, Officer Davis was not lawfully present because his initial stop of the vehicle was unsupported by reasonable suspicion.

The prosecution attempts to sidestep this issue by arguing that the "flashlight scan was lawful under *Texas v. Brown*, so the rifle casing was lawfully observed." This reasoning is circular. If the stop was unlawful—as this Court correctly found—then Officer Davis was not "lawfully positioned" when he scanned the interior. The prosecution cannot use evidence obtained from an unlawful seizure to establish the lawfulness of that same seizure. *See Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533, 542 (1988) (holding that evidence observed during an unlawful entry cannot be used to establish probable cause for a search).

The prosecution's reliance on *Murray* is misplaced. In *Murray*, the Court held that evidence discovered during an unlawful entry could not be used to establish probable cause *unless* the government could show that the evidence would have been discovered through an independent source. *Id.* at 542-43. Here, the prosecution has made no such showing. The rifle casing was observed only because Officer Davis unlawfully stopped the vehicle and then unlawfully scanned its interior. There was no independent source.

## III. THE AUTOMOBILE EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

The prosecution argues that once the rifle casing was observed, the automobile exception permitted a warrantless search of the entire vehicle, including the ukulele case. This argument fails for two reasons.

### A. The Probable Cause Was Derived From an Unlawful Stop

The automobile exception permits a warrantless search of a vehicle when an officer has probable cause to believe that the vehicle contains contraband. *See California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386, 392 (1985). However, this exception presupposes that the officer's presence at the vehicle is lawful. As the Supreme Court held in *Murray*, evidence obtained during an unlawful stop cannot form the basis for probable cause. *Murray*, 487 U.S. at 542.

Here, the prosecution asks the Court to use the rifle casing—observed during an unlawful flashlight scan—to establish probable cause for a full vehicle search. This is precisely the "fruit of the poisonous tree" analysis that *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963), prohibits. The rifle casing was the product of an unlawful seizure, and it cannot be used to justify a subsequent search.

### B. The Search Exceeded the Scope Permitted by the Automobile Exception

Even assuming *arguendo* that the rifle casing provided probable cause, the scope of the search exceeded constitutional limits.

In *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982), the Supreme Court held that probable cause to search a vehicle justifies a search of "every part of the vehicle and its contents that may conceal the object of the search." However, this scope is limited to places where the object of the search could reasonably be found. The officers observed a rifle casing on the front floor. While this might justify a search of the front compartment, it does not automatically justify searching a closed container in the rear of the vehicle.

The prosecution argues that "rifles are commonly concealed in seemingly innocuous containers like musical instrument cases." This argument proves too much. If any container could be searched merely because it *might* conceal a rifle, the automobile exception would become a blanket exception to the Fourth Amendment, permitting searches of every container in a vehicle whenever any contraband is observed. *Ross* does not authorize such unlimited exploration. Rather, the scope of the search must be "defined by the object of the search." *Id.* at 824.

Here, the officers had no specific reason to believe the ukulele case contained a rifle. They pushed the front seat forward to access a black case on the rear floor, then opened a latched ukulele case within it. This was a general exploratory search, not a search for a specific object linked to the probable cause. *See Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332, 351 (2009) (emphasizing that the scope of a search must be tied to the rationale for its allowance).

## IV. THE PAT-DOWN WAS UNLAWFUL

The prosecution argues that the pat-down of the occupants was justified under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968), and *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049 (1983). This argument fails.

Under *Terry*, a frisk is permitted only when an officer has "reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and presently dangerous." *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27. The prosecution argues that the totality of circumstances—including the security guard's report, the vehicle's presence on the freeway, the defendant's hesitation, and the discovery of the rifle casing—created reasonable suspicion that the occupants were armed.

This argument fails for two reasons.

First, the *Terry* analysis must be conducted at the time of the frisk. The officers conducted the pat-down *before* observing the rifle casing. At the time of the pat-down, the officers knew only that (1) a security guard had reported an unspecified "incident" at the quarry, and (2) a vehicle was on the uncompleted freeway. These facts do not support a reasonable inference that the occupants were armed.

Second, the defendant's five-second hesitation does not transform an otherwise non-threatening situation into one justifying a frisk. The prosecution cites *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. at 8, for the proposition that "evasive behavior" can support reasonable suspicion. However, *Sokolow* involved a defendant who had paid $2,100 in cash for airline tickets, was traveling under an alias, had no checked luggage, and was extremely nervous—behavior far more indicative of criminal activity than a momentary delay in exiting a vehicle. A five-second hesitation, without more, does not create a reasonable belief that the occupants are armed and dangerous.

The prosecution's reliance on *Michigan v. Long* is misplaced. In *Long*, the Court held that a pat-down is permissible during a lawful traffic stop when officers reasonably believe that the suspect is dangerous and might access a weapon in the vehicle. *Long*, 463 U.S. at 1049-50. However, *Long* explicitly前提 that the initial stop was lawful. *Id.* at 1048 ("We have repeatedly acknowledged that the 'temporary detention of occupants of a vehicle' during a traffic stop is a seizure."). Here, the initial stop was unlawful. Therefore, *Long* does not apply.

## V. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON *MURRAY* AND *GANT* IS MISPLACED

The prosecution argues that the defense's reliance on *Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533 (1988), and *Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332 (2009), is unfounded. This argument is without merit.

### A. *Murray* Supports the Defense

The prosecution argues that *Murray* "prohibits using evidence from an unlawful search to establish probable cause—but here, the flashlight scan was lawful." This argument is precisely backwards. The defense's argument—which the prosecution does not even attempt to rebut—is that the flashlight scan was *not* lawful because the initial stop was unlawful. *Murray* holds that evidence observed during an unlawful entry cannot be used to establish probable cause. *Murray*, 487 U.S. at 542. Here, the rifle casing was observed during an unlawful stop and flashlight scan. Therefore, under *Murray*, it cannot be used to establish probable cause for the subsequent search.

### B. *Gant* Supports the Defense

The prosecution argues that *Gant* "limits searches incident to arrest, but this search was justified under the automobile exception." This argument is a non sequitur. The defense's argument regarding *Gant* is not that the automobile exception is inapplicable, but that the scope of the search exceeded constitutional limits. *Gant* held that the scope of a search must be tied to the rationale for its allowance. *Gant*, 556 U.S. at 351. Here, the officers had no specific reason to believe the ukulele case contained a rifle. Their search was a general exploratory search, not a search for a specific object linked to the probable cause. This is precisely the type of search *Gant* prohibits.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the circuit court's grant of the motion to suppress evidence. **STATE OF HAWAII, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Defendant-Appellee.**

**NO. **

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

**B. K.**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence. The State of Hawaii appeals, arguing that the evidence seized from Defendant’s vehicle was obtained lawfully under the Fourth Amendment. After reviewing the record, the rebuttals of both parties, and the applicable legal precedents, this Court affirms the circuit court’s order. The officers lacked the reasonable suspicion necessary to justify the initial stop, and consequently, the evidence obtained as a result of that stop is inadmissible.

**I. THE INITIAL STOP LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION**

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures. A brief investigative stop of a vehicle constitutes a "seizure" within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment and must be justified by reasonable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot. *See Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 653 (1979). In *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), the Supreme Court held that an officer may stop a person only if the officer has "specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion."

The prosecution argues that the totality of the circumstances—the security guard's report of an "incident," the vehicle's presence on a barricaded freeway, and the Defendant's hesitation to exit the vehicle—provided reasonable suspicion. The Court finds these arguments insufficient.

First, the security guard's report was devoid of the specificity required to justify a seizure. The record indicates that the guard reported an "incident" at the Kapaa Quarry but "furnished no other information." There was no description of suspects, no description of a vehicle, and no indication of the nature of the criminal activity. As the Supreme Court noted in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), probable cause—and by extension reasonable suspicion—requires a "substantial chance" that criminal activity is occurring, not mere conjecture. A bulletin based solely on a vague report of an "incident" cannot provide the particularized suspicion required to stop any vehicle found in the general vicinity.

Second, the prosecution contends that the vehicle's presence on the uncompleted freeway was inherently suspicious because the area was "barricaded" and "restricted." However, the facts provided do not establish that the roadway was posted with signs prohibiting entry or that the area was restricted by law. The mere presence of a chain-link fence at the uncompleted end of the freeway does not, without more, transform the area into a location where all presence is presumptively criminal. The vehicle was traveling at a reasonable speed with its headlights on, behavior consistent with a driver who may have inadvertently turned onto the roadway rather than a person engaged in criminal conduct. *See Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. at 663 (holding that reasonable suspicion requires specific articulable facts, not merely the absence of a legitimate reason for being in a particular place).

Third, the prosecution relies heavily on the Defendant's five-second hesitation to exit the vehicle as a factor heightening suspicion. However, this hesitation occurred *after* the stop had already been effected. Reasonable suspicion must exist at the time the seizure is made. *See United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 7 (1989). The Defendant's reaction to the officer's command cannot be used to retroactively justify the initial stop. *See Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 274 (2000) (emphasizing that a Terry stop requires independent reasonable suspicion). Because the initial stop was not supported by specific and articulable facts suggesting criminal activity, it violated the Fourth Amendment.

**II. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN AND SUBSEQUENT SEARCH WERE UNLAWFUL**

Because the initial stop was unconstitutional, the evidence obtained thereafter must be analyzed as the fruit of that unlawful intrusion. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963). The prosecution attempts to justify the search of the vehicle under the "plain view" doctrine and the "automobile exception." However, these doctrines presuppose that the officer’s presence and initial observations are lawful.

The prosecution cites *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983), for the proposition that the use of a flashlight to illuminate a vehicle’s interior does not constitute a search. While this statement of law is correct, it applies only when the officer is lawfully present. *See id.* at 739-40. Since Officer Davis lacked reasonable suspicion to stop the vehicle, his subsequent detention of the occupants and his examination of the vehicle’s interior were unlawful. Therefore, the observation of the .22-caliber rifle casing cannot be used to retroactively justify the stop or establish probable cause. *See Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533, 542 (1988) (evidence observed during an unlawful entry cannot be used to establish probable cause).

The prosecution argues that the rifle casing provided probable cause to search the vehicle under the automobile exception. *California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386, 392 (1985). Even if the casing were properly observed, the scope of the search must be tied to the object of the search. *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982). However, because the casing was discovered during an unlawful flashlight scan, it is tainted fruit. The prosecution cannot bootstrap probable cause from an unconstitutional observation. *Murray*, 487 U.S. at 542.

**III. THE PAT-DOWN WAS UNLAWFUL**

The officers’ decision to conduct a pat-down search of the occupants was also unsupported by the facts. Under *Terry*, a frisk is permitted only when an officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27. The record contains no evidence of threatening behavior, furtive movements, or any indication that the occupants were armed at the time the frisk was conducted. The frisk occurred before the discovery of the rifle casing; at that moment, the officers relied only on the vague "incident" report and the vehicle's location. These facts are insufficient to establish a reasonable belief that the occupants were armed and dangerous. *See Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049 (1983) (noting that a frisk is justified only during a lawful stop based on specific safety concerns).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the circuit court did not err in granting the motion to suppress. The officers lacked reasonable suspicion to justify the initial stop of the vehicle. Consequently, the pat-down search and the subsequent search of the vehicle were unconstitutional. The evidence obtained as a result of these violations is inadmissible.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the judgment of the circuit court is affirmed.

Second, the vehicle was traveling at a reasonable speed with its headlights on—behavior consistent with a driver who may have inadvertently turned onto the roadway rather than a person engaged in criminal conduct. The prosecution's argument in United States v. Sokolow, 490 U.S. 1, 8 (1989), is distinguishable. In Sokolow, the Court found reasonable suspicion based on a combination of factors including cash payment, no checked luggage, and nervous behavior. Here, the prosecution points to only one factor: the vehicle's location. This is insufficient.

### C. The Defendant's Alleged Hesitation Cannot Retroactively Justify the Stop

The prosecution argues that the defendant's five-second hesitation to exit the vehicle corroborated suspicion. This argument fails because the hesitation occurred after the stop had already been effected.

Reasonable suspicion must exist at the time the seizure is made. See Sokolow, 490 U.S. at 7. The defendant's reaction to the officer's command cannot be used to retroactively justify the initial stop. See Florida v. J.L., 529 U.S. at 274 (emphasizing that a Terry stop requires independent reasonable suspicion). The prosecution's argument that "the officer's initial approach and command were part of the investigative stop" conflates the timing of the seizure. The vehicle was stopped when Officer Davis ordered it to halt; the defendant's subsequent hesitation is irrelevant to whether reasonable suspicion existed at the moment of the seizure.

---

## II. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN WAS UNLAWFUL BECAUSE THE STOP WAS UNLAWFUL

The prosecution argues that the flashlight scan was lawful under Texas v. Brown, 460 U.S. 730 (1983). This argument misapprehends the holding of that case.

In Texas v. Brown, the Court held that "the use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle... does not violate the Fourth Amendment where the officer is lawfully positioned outside the vehicle." See Brown, 460 U.S. at 740 (emphasis added). The prosecution omits the critical qualifier: the officer must be lawfully present.

Since Officer Davis lacked reasonable suspicion to stop the vehicle, his subsequent detention of the occupants and his examination of the vehicle's interior were unlawful. Therefore, the observation of the .22-caliber rifle casing cannot be used to retroactively justify the stop or establish probable cause. See Murray v. United States, 487 U.S. 533, 542 (1988) (evidence observed during an unlawful entry cannot be used to establish probable cause).

The prosecution attempts to distinguish Murray by arguing the search was lawful because the stop was justified. This is circular reasoning. The very question before this Court is whether the stop was justified. Assuming arguendo that the stop was unlawful, the observation of the rifle casing is tainted fruit that cannot establish probable cause.

---

## III. THE RIFLE CASING CANNOT ESTABLISH PROBABLE CAUSE

The prosecution argues that the rifle casing provided probable cause to search the vehicle under the automobile exception. This argument fails because the casing was discovered during an unlawful search.

Even if the casing were properly observed, the scope of the search must be tied to the object of the search. See United States v. Ross, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982). However, because the casing was discovered during an unlawful flashlight scan, it is tainted fruit. The prosecution cannot bootstrap probable cause from an unconstitutional observation. See Murray, 487 U.S. at 542.

---

## IV. THE PAT-DOWN SEARCH WAS UNLAWFUL

The prosecution does not meaningfully address the pat-down search. Nevertheless, this Court should affirm the circuit court's finding that the frisk was unlawful.

Under Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968), a frisk is permitted only when an officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. The record contains no evidence of threatening behavior, furtive movements, or any indication that the occupants were armed at the time the frisk was conducted. The frisk occurred before the discovery of the rifle casing; at that moment, the officers relied only on the vague "incident" report and the vehicle's location. These facts are insufficient to establish a reasonable belief that the occupants were armed and dangerous. See Michigan v. Long, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049 (1983) (noting that a frisk is justified only during a lawful stop based on specific safety concerns).

---

## V. THE FRUIT OF THE POISONOUS TREE DOCTRINE APPLIES

Because the initial stop was unconstitutional, all evidence obtained thereafter must be suppressed as fruit of the poisonous tree. See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963). The prosecution cannot rehabilitate an unlawful stop by pointing to evidence discovered as a result of that stop.

The prosecution argues that Murray v. United States only applies when evidence is discovered during an unlawful search. This is correct—and here, the search was unlawful because the stop was unlawful. The prosecution's argument proves too much.

---

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution asks this Court to adopt a broad interpretation of reasonable suspicion that would permit stops based on vague reports and mere presence in unusual locations. This interpretation would eviscerate the Fourth Amendment's protections.

The circuit court correctly applied the law. The officers lacked reasonable suspicion to justify the initial stop. Consequently, the pat-down search and the subsequent search of the vehicle were unconstitutional. The evidence obtained as a result of these violations is inadmissible.

This Court should affirm the circuit court's order granting the motion to suppress evidence.

**STATE OF HAWAII, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Defendant-Appellee.**

**NO. **

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

**B. K.**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence. The State of Hawaii appeals, arguing that the evidence seized from Defendant’s vehicle was obtained lawfully under the Fourth Amendment. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, as well as the factual record and applicable legal precedents, this Court affirms the circuit court’s order. The officers lacked the reasonable suspicion necessary to justify the initial stop, and consequently, the evidence obtained as a result of that stop is inadmissible.

**I. THE INITIAL STOP LACKED REASONABLE SUSPICION**

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures. A brief investigative stop of a vehicle constitutes a "seizure" within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment and must be justified by reasonable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot. *See Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 653 (1979). In *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), the Supreme Court held that an officer may stop a person only if the officer has "specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion."

The prosecution argues that the totality of the circumstances—the security guard's report of an "incident," the vehicle's presence on a barricaded freeway, and the Defendant's hesitation to exit the vehicle—provided reasonable suspicion. The Court finds these arguments insufficient to overcome the deficiencies in the record.

First, the security guard's report was devoid of the specificity required to justify a seizure. The record indicates that the guard reported an "incident" at the Kapaa Quarry but "furnished no other information." There was no description of suspects, no description of a vehicle, and no indication of the nature of the criminal activity. As the Supreme Court noted in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), probable cause—and by extension reasonable suspicion—requires a "substantial chance" that criminal activity is occurring, not mere conjecture. A bulletin based solely on a vague report of an "incident" cannot provide the particularized suspicion required to stop any vehicle found in the general vicinity. The prosecution’s attempt to characterize this as a "contemporaneous, on-scene report" does not cure the lack of descriptive detail linking the report to the specific vehicle stopped.

Second, while the prosecution contends that the vehicle's presence on the uncompleted freeway was inherently suspicious because the area was "barricaded" and "restricted," the facts provided do not establish that the roadway was posted with signs prohibiting entry or that the area was restricted by law. The mere presence of a chain-link fence at the uncompleted end of the freeway does not, without more, transform the area into a location where all presence is presumptively criminal. The vehicle was traveling at a reasonable speed with its headlights on, behavior consistent with a driver who may have inadvertently turned onto the roadway rather than a person engaged in criminal conduct. *See Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. at 663 (holding that reasonable suspicion requires specific articulable facts, not merely the absence of a legitimate reason for being in a particular place).

Third, the prosecution relies heavily on the Defendant's five-second hesitation to exit the vehicle as a factor heightening suspicion. However, this hesitation occurred *after* the stop had already been effected. Reasonable suspicion must exist at the time the seizure is made. *See United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 7 (1989). The Defendant's reaction to the officer's command cannot be used to retroactively justify the initial stop. *See Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 274 (2000) (emphasizing that a Terry stop requires independent reasonable suspicion). Because the initial stop was not supported by specific and articulable facts suggesting criminal activity, it violated the Fourth Amendment.

**II. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN AND SUBSEQUENT SEARCH WERE UNLAWFUL**

Because the initial stop was unconstitutional, the evidence obtained thereafter must be analyzed as the fruit of that unlawful intrusion. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963). The prosecution attempts to justify the search of the vehicle under the "plain view" doctrine and the "automobile exception." However, these doctrines presuppose that the officer’s presence and initial observations are lawful.

The prosecution cites *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983), for the proposition that the use of a flashlight to illuminate a vehicle’s interior does not constitute a search. While this statement of law is correct, it applies only when the officer is lawfully present. *See id.* at 739-40. Since Officer Davis lacked reasonable suspicion to stop the vehicle, his subsequent detention of the occupants and his examination of the vehicle’s interior were unlawful. Therefore, the observation of the .22-caliber rifle casing cannot be used to retroactively justify the stop or establish probable cause. *See Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533, 542 (1988) (evidence observed during an unlawful entry cannot be used to establish probable cause).

The prosecution argues that the rifle casing provided probable cause to search the vehicle under the automobile exception. *California v. Carney*, 471 U.S. 386, 392 (1985). Even if the casing were properly observed, the scope of the search must be tied to the object of the search. *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982). However, because the casing was discovered during an unlawful flashlight scan, it is tainted fruit. The prosecution cannot bootstrap probable cause from an unconstitutional observation. *Murray*, 487 U.S. at 542.

**III. THE PAT-DOWN WAS UNLAWFUL**

The officers’ decision to conduct a pat-down search of the occupants was also unsupported by the facts. Under *Terry*, a frisk is permitted only when an officer has reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27. The record contains no evidence of threatening behavior, furtive movements, or any indication that the occupants were armed at the time the frisk was conducted. The frisk occurred before the discovery of the rifle casing; at that moment, the officers relied only on the vague "incident" report and the vehicle's location. These facts are insufficient to establish a reasonable belief that the occupants were armed and dangerous. *See Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049 (1983) (noting that a frisk is justified only during a lawful stop based on specific safety concerns).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the circuit court did not err in granting the motion to suppress. The officers lacked reasonable suspicion to justify the initial stop of the vehicle. Consequently, the pat-down search and the subsequent search of the vehicle were unconstitutional. The evidence obtained as a result of these violations is inadmissible.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the judgment of the circuit court is affirmed.
