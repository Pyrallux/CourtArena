FACTS:
Defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic (""Argentina"") and YPF S.A. (""YPF"") (together, ""defendants"") appeal an order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New *199York (Preska, J .), denying defendants' motions to dismiss under (1) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on grounds of foreign sovereign immunity and (2) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) pursuant to the act of state doctrine. We affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

BACKGROUND
Unless otherwise noted, the facts herein are undisputed. They are drawn from the complaint and the documents submitted by the parties in reference to defendants' motions to dismiss.

I. YPF Becomes a Publicly Traded Company

YPF is a petroleum company that was wholly owned and operated by the Argentine government until 1993. That year, in accordance with broader efforts to reform its economy, Argentina decided to privatize the petrol firm through an initial public offering (""IPO"") of nearly 100% of YPF's voting stock (the ""shares"").1 Argentina and YPF took a number of steps to entice investors to participate in the IPO and thereby ensure its success, two of which are particularly relevant to this case. First, they arranged for YPF to offer shares in the United States as American Depository Receipts (""ADRs"") listed on the New York Stock Exchange (""NYSE""). Second, they amended YPF's bylaws-that is, the contract governing the relationship among YPF, Argentina (in its capacity as a shareholder), and other YPF shareholders. In particular, the bylaws were amended to incorporate protections for investors from (1) hostile takeovers and (2) attempts by Argentina to renationalize the company. These takeover protections form the basis of this breach of contract dispute, and so we describe them in some detail.

Section 7(d) of the amended bylaws prohibits (with certain exceptions inapplicable here) the direct or indirect acquisition of YPF shares if the acquisition results in the acquirer controlling 15% or more of the shares, unless the acquirer makes a tender offer for all of the outstanding shares in accordance with certain procedures and at a price determined by a formula in the bylaws. Among the prescribed procedures, section 7(f) requires that any such tender offer comply with the rules and regulations imposed by the governments and stock exchanges where YPF's shares are listed. Because YPF's securities were to be listed on the NYSE, those conducting tender offers in accordance with these shareholder protection measures would be compelled by section 7(f) to comply with NYSE and Securities and Exchange Commission (""SEC"") rules and regulations. Section 7(f)(iv) further obligates the acquirer to publish notice of its tender offer ""in the business section of the major newspapers ... in the City of New York, U.S.A. and any other city where the shares [of YPF]
*200shall be listed."" App. 340. Perhaps most importantly for purposes of this appeal, section 28(A) of the bylaws extends the tender offer requirement of sections 7(e) and 7(f) to:
all acquisitions made by the [Government of Argentina], whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument, of shares or securities of [YPF], 1) if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the [Government] becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of [YPF], which, in addition to the prior holdings thereof of any class of shares, represent, in the aggregate, at least 49% of the capital stock [of YPF]; or 2) if the [Government] acquires at least 8% of class D outstanding shares of stock, while withholding class A shares of stock amounting at least to 5% of the capital stock.
App. 432.

The penalties for breaching these provisions are drastic. Section 7(h) provides that ""[s]hares of stock and securities acquired in breach of [the tender offer requirements] shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends."" App. 342. And section 28(C) extends such treatment to shares acquired by Argentina, unless its breach is accidental. In that case, ""[t]he penalties provided for in subsection (h) of Section 7 shall be limited ... to the loss of the right to vote."" App. 355. At bottom, these shareholder protection measures appear to promise investors a compensated exit from their ownership position in the firm if Argentina were to decide to renationalize YPF.

Argentina and YPF touted these protections in the prospectus filed with the SEC in connection with the IPO. That document stated that ""[u]nder [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws."" App. 23. The prospectus further stated that ""any Control Acquisition carried out by the Argentine Government other than in accordance with th[at] procedure ... will result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired."" Id. (alteration in original).

By all accounts, Argentina's marketing efforts worked. YPF launched a successful IPO on June 29, 1993. Through the sale of YPF securities, Argentina raised billions of dollars in investment capital with the largest share (more than $1.1 billion in total) coming from the sale of ADRs in the United States on the NYSE. A firm called Repsol S.A. (""Repsol"") emerged from the IPO as YPF's majority shareholder. Even after the IPO, however, Argentina continued to participate in YPF's corporate governance as a commercial actor. It remained a holder of YPF's Class A shares, entitling it to elect at least one member of the firm's board of directors. Argentina also retained a veto right over certain third-party acquisitions of YPF's capital stock. After the IPO, YPF's shares, via the ADRs, were traded publicly on the NYSE and other exchanges.

Plaintiffs-appellees Petersen EnergÃ­a Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen EnergÃ­a, S.A.U. (together, ""Petersen"") entered the picture in 2008. Between 2008 and 2011, Petersen conducted a series of acquisitions and came to own approximately 25% of YPF's shares, held in the form of ADRs issued by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. All of Petersen's acquisitions were made in accordance with YPF's bylaws, including the tender offer provisions in section 7. The bulk of Petersen's shares were purchased from Repsol and their purchase was financed by Repsol and various financial institutions, which maintained *201a security interest in the stock as collateral. As part of a shareholder agreement with Petersen, Repsol agreed to cause YPF to make biannual distributions of 90% of its profits to shareholders via dividends in accordance with section 25 of the bylaws. Petersen often used these dividends to make payments on the loans it used to finance the purchase of YPF stock.

All of that changed in 2012. Early that year, members of the Argentine government began publicly criticizing Repsol's and Petersen's management of YPF and started discussing the prospect of renationalizing the company. The value of YPF's ADRs plummeted in response to this news. To put what happened next in the appropriate context, it helps to understand a little about the mechanics of Argentine expropriation law.

II. Argentine Expropriation Law

Expropriation is the ""governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights."" Expropriation , BLACK'S LAW DICTIONARY (10th ed. 2014). A ""classic example"" is the government's condemnation of a parcel of land to make way for some public good, like a road. Murr v. Wisconsin , --- U.S. ----, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939, 198 L.Ed.2d 497 (2017). The enactment of land use regulations may also, in some cases, constitute an expropriation. See id. But these land-based examples understate the breadth of a sovereign's power of expropriation, which can be vast. That is so because all types of property can be expropriated, whether tangible or intangible. Personal property, airspace rights, contract rights, even the shares of a corporation-at least in theory, a sovereign can expropriate them all. See Penn Cent. Transp. Co. v. City of New York , 438 U.S. 104, 128, 98 S.Ct. 2646, 57 L.Ed.2d 631 (1978) (discussing a ""taking"" of airspace rights); accord August Reinisch, Expropriation , in The Oxford Handbook of International Investment Law 407, 410 (Peter Muchlinski et al. eds., 2008) (""It is generally asserted that expropriation may affect not only tangible property but also a broad range of intangible assets of economic value to an investor. Property that may be expropriated by states thus comprises immaterial rights and interests, including in particular contractual rights.""). In reality, however, whether a government may expropriate property, what property is subject to expropriation, and how much the government must compensate the individual it expropriated the property from (if at all) are largely questions of law of the expropriating nation. Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts , 28 Fordham. L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) (""States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best; ... they are free to operate their municipal system of property according to their own national genius ....""); compare Org. for Econ. Co-operation & Dev., ""Indirect Expropriation"" and the ""Right to Regulate"" in International Investment Law , in International Investment Law: A Changing Landscape 43, 43-72 (2005) (discussing limits imposed on expropriations by customary international law). In this case, we look to Argentine law. See Garb v. Republic of Poland , 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006).

Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution sets the conditions under which property may be expropriated by the Argentine government. To effectuate an expropriation consistent with Article 17, two conditions must be met: (1) the Argentine Congress must declare a public use for the property to be expropriated and (2) the owner of the property must be compensated. The Argentine government has passed laws to clarify what property is subject to *202expropriation and to specify the procedures that must be followed to meet the conditions for expropriation.

One such law is Law 21,499, known as the ""General Expropriation Law."" App. 57. It empowers, among other entities, the Argentine Federal Government to act as an expropriator. As for the declaration of public use required by Article 17 of the National Constitution, section 5 of the General Expropriation Law clarifies that the Argentine Congress ""shall particularly refer to specific property"" to be expropriated in its declaration and section 1 provides that ""[p]ublic use, which is required as legal grounds for expropriation, comprises all cases where public welfare may be involved."" App. 185-86. The law further declares that ""[a]ll such property as may be convenient or necessary to satisfy [that] 'public use' purpose, whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not, may be subject to expropriation ...."" App. 186. As for compensation for that property, section 10 of the General Expropriation Law provides that the owner shall receive ""the objective value of the property plus any direct and immediate damages resulting from expropriation,"" such amounts to be fixed by agreement of the owner and expropriator or pursuant to a court proceeding. App. 187. And, presumably to prevent the owner's malfeasance while compensation is being fixed, section 16 of the law proclaims that ""[n]o contract executed by the owner after the effective date of the law declaring the expropriation of the property and which may imply the creation of any right or interest in the property shall be good as against the expropriator."" App. 187.
Accordingly, with this legal backdrop in mind, we return to how Argentina regained control over YPF's affairs in the spring of 2012.

III. Argentina Regains Control of YPF

On April 16, 2012, pursuant to the General Expropriation Law, Argentina proposed legislation that would expropriate directly from Repsol 51% of the voting stock of YPF. On the same day, the Argentine National Executive Office decreed that it was empowering an ""Intervenor"" to seize immediate control of YPF's operations and to operate the company as a going concern while the Argentine Congress considered the expropriation legislation. Action was swift. Indeed, before some of these measures were even announced publicly, the Intervenor seized control of YPF's facilities, replaced top management with government officials, and escorted YPF's then-CEO off the premises. The Intervenor also cancelled regularly-scheduled meetings of YPF's board of directors and refused to make expected dividend payments.

Argentine officials were also quick to declare that, despite having acquired control of the company, Argentina and YPF had no intention of complying with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. For example, on April 17, 2012, in a speech before the Argentine Senate, the country's Deputy Economy Minister described as ""fools ... those who think that the State has to be stupid and buy everyone according to YPF's own law, respecting its by-law."" App. 29 n.1. He also dismissed the tender offer requirements as ""unfair"" and a ""bear trap."" Id .

On May 3, 2012, the proposed expropriation legislation was enacted as Law 26,741 with an effective date of May 7, 2012 (the ""YPF Expropriation Law""). In accordance with Article 17 of the National Constitution, the YPF Expropriation Law pronounced Argentina's national public interest in achieving ""self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon[ ] supply,"" App. 165, by, inter *203alia , integrating ""public and private ... capital into strategic alliances aimed at the exploration and exploitation of conventional and unconventional hydrocarbons,"" App. 166.
The law further provided that:
to ensure the fulfillment of the objectives of this law, the fifty-one percent (51%) equity interest in YPF Sociedad AnÃ³nima represented by the same percentage of Class D shares of the said Company, held by Repsol YPF S.A., its controlled or controlling entities, directly or indirectly, is hereby declared to be of public use and subject to expropriation.
App. 167. The YPF Expropriation Law also extended the Intervenor's control over the firm's operations and granted the Argentine executive branch the right to ""exercise all the political rights over all the shares subject to expropriation"" until the expropriation, including compensation of Repsol, was finalized. App. 167.

Argentina did indeed exercise the rights of Repsol's shares, using them to cancel YPF's previously-scheduled dividend payment and board meeting in April 2012, and voted the shares at a shareholder meeting in June 2012, in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Unable to meet its loan obligations without the dividend payment, Petersen entered insolvency proceedings in July 2012 and its lenders foreclosed on the YPF ADRs that Petersen had pledged as collateral. Repsol was eventually compensated for its expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion.

**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honor, this is not a case about sovereign expropriation. It is about a foreign sovereign’s breach of private, commercial contractual obligations—obligations that were created, publicized, and enforced in the United States for the express purpose of attracting investment capital to a publicly traded company. The defendants seek to shield themselves behind sovereign immunity and the act of state doctrine for conduct that is indistinguishable from that of any private corporation. But the law is unequivocal: when a sovereign engages in commercial activity, it forfeits its immunity. And when a claim arises from a private contract, the act of state doctrine has no application.  

### **I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act Does Not Bar This Suit Because the Claims Arise from Commercial Activity**  
The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act ("FSIA") grants foreign states immunity from suit in U.S. courts—*unless* the claims fall within one of the Act’s enumerated exceptions. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. Here, the commercial activity exception applies. Under § 1605(a)(2), immunity is waived when the action is "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "based upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere" or "based upon an act outside the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States."  

The defendants’ obligations under YPF’s bylaws were not sovereign acts but commercial ones. When Argentina privatized YPF in 1993, it did not act as a sovereign exercising governmental authority. It acted as a private seller of securities—issuing shares on the New York Stock Exchange as American Depository Receipts (ADRs), filing a prospectus with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and amending YPF’s corporate bylaws to include investor protections that would make the offering attractive to U.S. investors. As the Supreme Court held in *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), "the question is whether the particular action taken by the foreign sovereign is one that a private person would also take." Here, Argentina did not merely issue bonds or regulate a market; it entered into a binding contractual framework for a publicly traded company, complete with specific obligations to investors—obligations that mirrored those of any corporate issuer under U.S. securities law.  

The bylaws’ tender offer provisions were not sovereign policy but commercial terms. The prospectus explicitly stated that "under [YPF’s] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF’s] capital stock . . . the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws." App. 23. It further warned that "any Control Acquisition carried out by the Argentine Government other than in accordance with [that] procedure . . . will result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired." *Id.* These were not abstract policy statements; they were precise contractual promises designed to reassure investors. When Argentina expropriated Repsol’s shares in 2012, it did not follow this procedure. Instead, as the Deputy Economy Minister openly declared, it dismissed the tender offer requirement as a "bear trap" and refused to comply. App. 29 n.1.  

This breach had a "direct effect" in the United States. The complaint alleges that YPF’s refusal to enforce the bylaws’ penalty provisions—specifically Section 7(h), which prohibits voting or dividend rights for shares acquired in breach—directly caused harm to U.S. investors. Argentina’s illegal exercise of voting rights canceled scheduled dividend payments to U.S.-based ADR holders. This cancellation precipitated Petersen’s default on U.S. loan obligations, and its ADRs—held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City—were foreclosed upon. These effects were not incidental; they were the direct, foreseeable consequences of Argentina’s and YPF’s actions. As this Court held in *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 359 (1993), "a foreign state’s commercial activity has a 'direct effect' in the United States when it has a substantial impact on U.S. persons or property." Here, the cancellation of dividends to U.S. investors and the foreclosure of ADRs in New York City are textbook examples of such effects.  

The defendants cannot escape liability by claiming this was a "sovereign act." In *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 136 S. Ct. 390, 395 (2015), the Supreme Court reaffirmed that "the commercial activity exception applies only when the foreign state’s activity has a commercial character." But when a sovereign participates in the corporate governance of a publicly traded company—issuing shares on U.S. exchanges, filing SEC disclosures, and embedding contractual obligations in corporate bylaws—it is acting as a commercial entity. Argentina did not expropriate YPF through a unilateral governmental decree; it did so *while simultaneously breaching its own contractual promises to investors*. That breach is not shielded by immunity.  

### **II. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Apply Because This is a Private Contractual Dispute**  
Even if the defendants were correct that Argentina’s expropriation was a sovereign act, the act of state doctrine provides no refuge. That doctrine bars U.S. courts from questioning the validity of a foreign sovereign’s official acts *within its own territory*. But it does *not* apply to claims arising from private commercial contracts. As the Supreme Court held in *First National City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 623 (1983), "the act of state doctrine is not applicable to disputes involving private contractual rights."  

Here, Petersen’s claims do not challenge the validity of Argentina’s expropriation itself. They challenge Argentina’s and YPF’s breach of specific contractual obligations under YPF’s bylaws—obligations that were agreed upon in a commercial context, publicly disclosed in SEC filings, and enforced through U.S. securities law. The bylaws’ tender offer provisions were not part of Argentina’s sovereign expropriation scheme; they were private contractual terms that existed independently of the expropriation law. Indeed, as the district court recognized, the YPF Expropriation Law "does not prohibit a post-expropriation tender offer under YPF’s bylaws; indeed, it says absolutely nothing about Argentina’s acquisition of additional YPF shares in a subsequent market transaction." *Op. at 209*.  

This is precisely the type of case where the act of state doctrine has no place. In *OBB*, 136 S. Ct. at 396, the Court emphasized that the doctrine "does not apply to claims based on commercial contracts." Here, Petersen is not asking the Court to second-guess Argentina’s decision to expropriate; it is asking the Court to enforce the contractual terms that Argentina agreed to when it sold shares to U.S. investors. To allow sovereign immunity and the act of state doctrine to shield this conduct would eviscerate the commercial activity exception and turn the FSIA into a shield for foreign sovereigns who enter U.S. markets and then breach their private obligations.  

### **Conclusion**  
This case is simple: Argentina and YPF made a commercial deal with U.S. investors. They promised contractual protections, then broke them. The resulting harm—canceled dividends, foreclosed ADRs, and lost investments—occurred squarely in the United States. The law is clear: when a foreign sovereign acts as a commercial player, it cannot hide behind sovereign immunity. And when a claim arises from a private contract, the act of state doctrine has no role. The district court correctly denied the defendants’ motions to dismiss. We urge this Court to affirm.  

Thank you, Your Honor.

### INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

Appellants Argentine Republic and YPF S.A. respectfully submit this opening brief in defense of the district court's denial of their motions to dismiss. The Court should affirm the district court's ruling on the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act issue and dismiss the appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

The plaintiff-appellees have characterized this case as a simple commercial dispute involving breach of private contractual obligations. This characterization fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the claims and the applicable legal framework. At its core, this case involves the Argentine Republic's exercise of its sovereign power of expropriation—a quintessential governmental function—pursuant to constitutionally mandated procedures under Argentine law. The plaintiff attempts to dress this sovereign act in commercial clothing by pointing to the private contractual arrangements that existed between the parties prior to the expropriation. However, the FSIA and the act of state doctrine were designed precisely to prevent courts from adjudicating disputes that would require examination of the validity of foreign sovereign acts. This Court should reject the plaintiff's effort to circumvent these well-established protections and affirm the district court's order.

---

### I. ARGUMENT

#### A. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act Bars This Suit Because Argentina's Conduct Constitutes Sovereign Activity, Not Commercial Activity

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act provides that foreign states are "immune from the jurisdiction of the courts of the United States" unless one of the statutory exceptions applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The burden of proving that an exception applies rests upon the plaintiff. *See Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 488 U.S. 1, 17 (1983). Here, the plaintiff has failed to carry this burden.

**1. Argentina's Expropriation Was a Sovereign Act, Not Commercial Activity**

The commercial activity exception under 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2) applies only when the foreign state acts "as a private participant in the marketplace." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992). The Supreme Court has consistently held that "the commercial activity exception applies only to those portions of the foreign state's activities that are 'commercial' in nature." *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 136 S. Ct. 390, 395 (2015). The critical inquiry is whether the particular action taken by the foreign sovereign is "the type of action by which a private party might engage in the marketplace." *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618.

Expropriation is the quintessential exercise of sovereign authority. As this Court recognized in *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 594 (2d Cir. 2006), "whether a government may expropriate property, what property is subject to expropriation, and how much the government must compensate the individual it expropriated the property from (if at all) are largely questions of law of the expropriating nation." No private party can exercise the power of eminent domain. No private party can enact legislation declaring property to be of public use and subject to taking. No private party can compel a private corporation to transfer ownership of its shares through legislative decree. These are inherently sovereign functions that exist only because of the state's constitutional authority.

The plaintiff argues that Argentina's conduct was commercial because it involved the privatization of YPF in 1993 and the issuance of securities on the New York Stock Exchange. This argument conflates distinct temporal periods and ignores the fundamental distinction between Argentina's commercial conduct in 1993 and its sovereign conduct in 2012. In 1993, Argentina acted as a private seller of securities when it conducted the IPO. However, in 2012, when Argentina enacted the YPF Expropriation Law and exercised its constitutional power of expropriation, it acted as a sovereign exercising governmental authority. The commercial activity exception focuses on the specific act upon which the claim is based. *See Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 356 (1993). Here, the claim is based upon Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares—a sovereign act.

**2. The Direct Effect Test Is Not Satisfied**

Even assuming *arguendo* that some portion of Argentina's conduct could be characterized as commercial, the plaintiff cannot satisfy the "direct effect" requirement of § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has held that a foreign state's commercial activity has a "direct effect" in the United States only when it has "a direct effect in the United States on the plaintiff." *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 359 (emphasis in original). The plaintiff argues that Argentina's exercise of voting rights caused the cancellation of dividend payments to U.S. investors and precipitated the foreclosure of ADRs held in New York. However, these alleged effects are too attenuated to satisfy the direct effect requirement.

The critical fact is that Argentina's expropriation was accomplished through the YPF Expropriation Law—a formal legislative act declaring 51% of YPF's shares to be of public use and subject to expropriation. Any effects on U.S. investors flowed from this sovereign act, not from any separate commercial activity. The plaintiff cannot circumvent the sovereign immunity doctrine by characterizing the inevitable consequences of sovereign action as "direct effects" of commercial activity. As the Supreme Court held in *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 360, the "direct effect" requirement demands more than "an indirect effect flowing from the sovereign act."

Moreover, the plaintiff's theory would render the sovereign immunity doctrine meaningless. Under the plaintiff's reasoning, any sovereign act that ultimately affects U.S. investors or property would be subject to U.S. jurisdiction. This interpretation would eviscerate the FSIA's protections and transform American courts into forums for adjudicating the contractual arrangements of foreign states. The Court should reject this expansive interpretation.

**3. YPF Is Entitled to Sovereign Immunity**

YPF, although a commercial entity, is entitled to sovereign immunity because it was acting as an instrumentality of the Argentine state when it engaged in the conduct complained of. Under the FSIA, an "agency or instrumentality of a foreign state" is immune from suit unless it is "engaged in a commercial activity in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). Here, YPF's conduct in connection with the expropriation was directed by the Argentine government through the Intervenor appointed pursuant to the YPF Expropriation Law. YPF did not act independently; it acted as an arm of the sovereign state. Under these circumstances, YPF is entitled to the same immunity as Argentina itself. *See Jam v. International Finance Corp.*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 769 (2019) (holding that agencies and instrumentalities of foreign states are entitled to immunity under the FSIA).

#### B. The Act of State Doctrine Bars Adjudication of This Dispute

The act of state doctrine provides that "the acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory are not subject to adjudication in United States courts." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). This doctrine reflects "the unique nature of theFSIA" and the "long-standing principle of international law that the courts of one country will not sit in judgment on the acts of another government taken within its own territory." *See W.S. Kirkegaard & Co. v. Mobilservicios Petroleros C.A.*, 762 F.3d 231, 238 (2d Cir. 2014).

**1. Argentina's Expropriation Was an Official Act of State**

The YPF Expropriation Law was enacted by the Argentine Congress in accordance with Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution. This law formally declared that Argentina had a national public interest in achieving self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon supply and that 51% of YPF's shares were of public use and subject to expropriation. This is precisely the type of official governmental action that the act of state doctrine was designed to shield.

The plaintiff argues that this case involves a private contractual dispute rather than a challenge to the validity of Argentina's sovereign acts. This argument is without merit. The plaintiff cannot enforce the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws without first establishing that Argentina's acquisition of YPF shares was not a valid expropriation. If the Court were to hold that Argentina was obligated to make a tender offer under the bylaws, it would necessarily be holding that Argentina's expropriation was invalid or insufficient. This the Court cannot do under the act of state doctrine.

**2. The Contractual Framework Does Not Override the Act of State Doctrine**

The plaintiff argues that the act of state doctrine does not apply because the claims arise from private contractual arrangements. This argument misconstrues the doctrine. In *First National City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 623 (1983), the Supreme Court held that the act of state doctrine "does not apply to disputes involving private contractual rights" only where the dispute does not require the court to "interpret or enforce an act of state." Here, interpreting the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws would require the Court to determine whether Argentina's expropriation complied with Argentine law and the Argentine Constitution. This is precisely the type of inquiry that the act of state doctrine prohibits.

Moreover, the bylaws themselves were subject to Argentine law and were embedded within the Argentine legal framework. The tender offer provisions were designed to operate within the context of Argentine corporate law and were subject to modification by Argentine governmental authority. The plaintiff cannot use these private contractual arrangements to circumvent the act of state doctrine.

**3. The Public Interest Requires Application of the Doctrine**

The act of state doctrine serves important interests of international comity. As the Supreme Court stated in *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428, "the act of state doctrine is a principle of decision binding on federal and state courts alike." Permitting the plaintiff to enforce the tender offer provisions against Argentina would require American courts to sit in judgment on the governmental acts of a foreign sovereign. This would "embarrass the executive branch in its conduct of foreign relations" and "might hinder the making of whatever accommodations might be possible." *Id.* at 423.

The plaintiff argues that the tender offer provisions were included in the bylaws specifically to protect investors from government action. However, this does not change the nature of the underlying governmental act. Argentina's expropriation remains a sovereign act, regardless of whether the plaintiff anticipated such action and attempted to contract around it.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before:** PRESHKA, Circuit Judge.

**Upon Consideration** of the opening arguments submitted by the parties, the record on appeal, and the applicable law, it is hereby ORDERED that the judgment of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York is **affirmed in part**, and the appeal is **dismissed in part**.

**I. Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act**

We first address the defendants' argument that the district court erred in denying their motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), 28 U.S.C. § 1601 *et seq.* We review de novo a district court's decision regarding subject matter jurisdiction under the FSIA. *See Haring v. Blumenthal*, 46 F.3d 1347, 1350 (2d Cir. 1995).

The FSIA provides that foreign states are immune from the jurisdiction of United States courts unless one of the Act's exceptions applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The defendants argue that their conduct falls outside the "commercial activity" exception set forth in 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). We disagree.

Under § 1605(a)(2), a foreign state is not immune from suit in any case "[b]ased upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "[b]ased upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere... and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The Supreme Court has instructed that in determining whether an activity is "commercial," we must look to the "nature" of the act rather than its "purpose." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). The relevant inquiry is "whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) are the type of actions by which a private party engages in... trade and traffic or commerce." *Id.* at 614-15.

Here, the plaintiffs' claims are based on the defendants' alleged breach of YPF's bylaws—specifically, the failure to make a tender offer upon acquiring a controlling interest and the unlawful exercise of voting rights. These bylaws were part of a contractual framework established during the 1993 privatization of YPF, a transaction expressly designed to attract investment in the United States through the sale of American Depository Receipts (ADRs) on the New York Stock Exchange. The obligation to make a tender offer and the penalties for failing to do so are quintessentially commercial terms. A private corporation issuing securities on a public exchange could certainly agree to such terms and could similarly breach them. Therefore, the actions giving rise to this suit—repudiation of the tender offer provisions and the stripping of voting rights—constitute commercial activity under the FSIA. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614.

Furthermore, the commercial activity exception requires that the act have a "direct effect" in the United States. *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 355-56 (1993). An effect is "direct" if it follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity." *Id.* at 358. The record demonstrates that the defendants' refusal to comply with the bylaws precipitated the cancellation of dividend payments to U.S. investors and directly caused Petersen's default on its loans. This default led to the foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs, which were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. These financial injuries to U.S. investors and the seizure of securities located in New York are direct effects of the defendants' commercial conduct. *See Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 359-60.

The defendants' contention that the expropriation itself is a sovereign act does not alter this analysis. While the enactment of the YPF Expropriation Law may have been a sovereign function, the claim is not based on the validity of the expropriation, but rather on the separate breach of the commercial contractual obligations contained in the bylaws. The FSIA's commercial activity exception applies to the specific acts upon which the claim is based. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. Because the defendants' repudiation of the tender offer provisions was commercial in nature and caused a direct effect in the United States, the district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss under the FSIA.

**II. Act of State Doctrine**

We next consider the defendants' appeal regarding the act of state doctrine. The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of the public acts a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). However, the doctrine is "confined to cases involving the validity of acts of foreign sovereigns." *First National City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 621 (1983).

The defendants argue that adjudicating the plaintiffs' contract claims would require this Court to sit in judgment on Argentina's sovereign expropriation. We need not reach the merits of this argument because, as the defendants themselves have requested, we dismiss the appeal on this issue. In their opening brief, the defendants-appellants expressly state: "The Court should... dismiss the appeal as to the act of state doctrine." We grant this unambiguous request. Consequently, we do not address the applicability of the act of state doctrine to the facts of this case.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the order of the district court denying the defendants' motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act is **AFFIRMED**. The defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine is **DISMISSED**.

Nelson, 507 U.S. 349, 356 (1993), where the Court emphasized that "the commercial activity exception focuses on the specific act upon which the claim is based." Here, the specific act upon which the claim is based is Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares pursuant to the YPF Expropriation Law—a sovereign act if ever there was one.

#### B. The "Direct Effect" Requirement Is Not Satisfied

Plaintiffs argue that the "direct effect" requirement is satisfied because Argentina's actions "precipitated Petersen's default" and "the foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs." This argument fails because the effects alleged by plaintiffs are too attenuated from the sovereign act of expropriation to satisfy the "direct effect" requirement.

Under Nelson, an effect is "direct" if it follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity." 507 U.S. at 358. The Supreme Court has emphasized that the "direct effect" requirement is demanding and requires more than "indirect" or "consequential" effects. See id. at 359-60.

Here, the chain of causation between Argentina's sovereign expropriation and plaintiffs' alleged injuries is attenuated in the extreme. Argentina enacted an expropriation law (sovereign act) → the Intervenor took control of YPF → YPF cancelled dividend payments → Petersen defaulted on its loans → lenders foreclosed on ADRs. Each step in this chain involves multiple independent actions by different parties. The expropriation itself did not cause Petersen to default; rather, it was the subsequent cancellation of dividends (a corporate decision) and Petersen's own financial arrangements that led to default.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that the "direct effect" requirement cannot be satisfied merely by showing that a foreign sovereign's act had economic consequences in the United States. See id. at 360 ("[T]he fact that the alleged misconduct involved acts in a foreign country does not, ipso facto, preclude the existence of a direct effect."). The effects here are precisely the type of indirect economic consequences that the FSIA was intended to exclude.

#### C. YPF Is Entitled to Sovereign Immunity as an Instrumentality of the Argentine State

Plaintiffs argue that YPF is not entitled to sovereign immunity because it was "engaging in commercial activity" when it "failed to enforce the bylaws." This argument ignores the well-established principle that an instrumentality of a foreign state retains sovereign immunity for acts taken in its official capacity, regardless of whether those acts might also be characterized as commercial.

YPF was, at all relevant times, an entity in which the Argentine government maintained significant ownership interests and exercised substantial control. The Argentine government retained Class A shares entitling it to elect board members, maintained veto rights over third-party acquisitions, and ultimately expropriated 51% of YPF's voting stock. When YPF failed to enforce the bylaws' tender offer requirements, it was acting in the context of and in coordination with the Argentine government's sovereign exercise of its expropriation authority.

Under the FSIA, "an agency or instrumentality of a foreign state" is defined as "any entity (1) which is a separate legal person, corporate stock of which is owned or controlled by a foreign state ... and (2) which is an organ of a foreign state or a political subdivision thereof, or a majority of whose shares or other ownership interest is owned by a foreign state." 28 U.S.C. § 1603(b). YPF clearly qualifies as such an instrumentality, and it retains sovereign immunity for acts taken in its official capacity.

### II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS THIS DISPUTE

#### A. Adjudication of This Dispute Requires Court to Determine Validity of Argentina's Sovereign Acts

Plaintiffs argue that the act of state doctrine does not apply because this is merely a "contract dispute" that does not require the Court to "determine whether Argentina's expropriation complied with Argentine law." This argument fundamentally misapprehends the nature of the claim and the act of state doctrine.

The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of the public acts a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." Sabbatino, 376 U.S. at 401. The doctrine applies not only when a plaintiff explicitly challenges the validity of a foreign sovereign's acts, but also when adjudication of the claim would require the court to "sit in judgment on" those sovereign acts. See First National City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba, 462 U.S. 611, 621 (1983).

Here, adjudication of plaintiffs' claims would necessarily require this Court to determine whether Argentina's expropriation complied with the procedural and substantive requirements of Argentine law—specifically, whether Argentina was required to comply with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws as a condition to validly expropriating the shares. The bylaws themselves purport to impose restrictions on Argentina's sovereign power of expropriation. To determine whether Argentina breached the bylaws, the Court would necessarily have to examine whether Argentina's acquisition of YPF shares was valid under Argentine law and whether the tender offer requirements apply to sovereign expropriations.

This is precisely the type of inquiry that the act of state doctrine prohibits. As the Supreme Court held in Sabbatino, 376 U.S. at 428, "the act of state doctrine requires that the acts of foreign sovereigns taken within their own jurisdiction shall be deemed valid by our courts." A court cannot determine whether Argentina breached its contractual obligations without first determining whether Argentina's acquisition of the shares was valid—and such a determination is precisely what the act of state doctrine forbids.

#### B. The Contract Framing Cannot Escape the Act of State Doctrine

Plaintiffs' attempt to characterize this as a "contract dispute" is a transparent effort to evade the act of state doctrine. The doctrine "is confined to cases involving the validity of acts of foreign sovereigns," First National City Bank, 462 U.S. at 621, and this case involves precisely such a question.

The bylaws purport to require Argentina to make a tender offer before acquiring a controlling interest in YPF. The YPF Expropriation Law authorized Argentina to expropriate 51% of YPF's shares directly from Repsol. These two provisions are in direct conflict. Determining whether Argentina breached the bylaws requires the Court to resolve this conflict—which provision of Argentine law governs? Does the tender offer requirement apply to sovereign expropriations, or does the Expropriation Law supersede the bylaws?

Answering these questions would require this Court to interpret Argentine law and determine the scope of Argentina's sovereign authority under its own legal system. This is exactly the type of inquiry that the act of state doctrine prohibits. As the Supreme Court held in Sabbatino, 376 U.S. at 428, "we decline to pronounce upon the validity of an act of state." The district court therefore erred in denying the motion to dismiss on act of state grounds.

### III. THE BYLAWS CANNOT CONSTRAIN ARGENTINA'S SOVEREIGN EXPROPRIATION POWER

Finally, even if this Court were to reach the merits, plaintiffs' claims fail on their face because the bylaws cannot be interpreted to constrain Argentina's sovereign power of expropriation under Argentine law.

The bylaws were adopted in 1993 as part of the privatization of YPF. They were never intended to—and could not—bind the Argentine Congress's future exercise of its sovereign authority to expropriate property for public use. Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution grants the government the power to expropriate property upon payment of just compensation. This is a fundamental attribute of sovereignty that cannot be contractually waived by a prior government.

The Argentine Congress enacted the YPF Expropriation Law in 2012, exercising its constitutional authority to declare the expropriation of YPF shares to be for public use. This law makes no mention of the tender offer requirements in the bylaws and expressly authorizes the expropriation. Under Argentine law, the later-in-time statute governs. The bylaws simply cannot be read to override the Argentine Congress's exercise of its sovereign expropriation power.

To the extent the bylaws could be interpreted to impose tender offer requirements on sovereign expropriations, such a reading would be inconsistent with Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution and would be invalid under Argentine law. This Court should not interpret the bylaws to create such an inconsistency, particularly where doing so would require passing judgment on the validity of Argentina's sovereign acts.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's order denying defendants' motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and should also address the act of state doctrine in favor of defendants. Argentina's exercise of its sovereign expropriation authority is precisely the type of governmental action that the FSIA and the act of state doctrine were designed to protect from scrutiny by U.S. courts.

**UPON CONSIDERATION** of the rebuttals submitted by the parties, the record on appeal, and the applicable law, it is hereby ORDERED that the judgment of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York is **AFFIRMED**.

**I. Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act**

We turn first to the defendants' challenge to the district court's denial of their motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), 28 U.S.C. § 1601 *et seq.* We review *de novo* a district court's subject matter jurisdiction ruling under the FSIA. *See Haring v. Blumenthal*, 46 F.3d 1347, 1350 (2d Cir. 1995).

**A. Commercial Activity**

The defendants argue that the "commercial activity" exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), does not apply because the acts upon which the plaintiffs' claims are based—specifically, the expropriation of YPF shares—are sovereign in nature, not commercial. We disagree.

Under the FSIA, a foreign state is immune from suit unless its actions fall within a statutory exception. The commercial activity exception removes immunity where the action is "based upon ... an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere ... and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has instructed that the "commercial activity" exception turns on the "nature" of the conduct rather than its "purpose." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). The relevant inquiry is "whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) are the type of actions by which a private party engages in ... trade and traffic or commerce." *Id.* at 614-15.

The defendants contend that because the expropriation was authorized by the Argentine Congress pursuant to its constitutional authority, it is a quintessential sovereign act. While the enactment of the YPF Expropriation Law may have been a sovereign function, the plaintiffs' claims are not based on the validity of the expropriation itself. Rather, the claims are based on the defendants' alleged breach of the contractual obligations contained in YPF's bylaws—specifically, the failure to make a tender offer upon acquiring a controlling interest and the unlawful exercise of voting rights in violation of section 7(h). These bylaws were part of a commercial framework established during the 1993 privatization, a transaction expressly designed to attract investment in the United States through the sale of American Depository Receipts (ADRs) on the New York Stock Exchange.

The obligation to make a tender offer and the penalties for failing to do so are commercial terms that a private corporation issuing securities on a public exchange could certainly agree to and breach. The defendants' repudiation of these obligations—explicitly declaring they would not comply with the bylaws' tender offer requirements—constitutes commercial activity under the FSIA. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. The fact that this repudiation occurred in the context of a sovereign expropriation does not transform the nature of the specific acts giving rise to the claim. *See Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 356 (1993) (noting the commercial activity exception focuses on the specific act upon which the claim is based).

**B. Direct Effect**

The defendants further argue that the "direct effect" requirement is not satisfied because the causal chain between the expropriation and the plaintiffs' injuries is too attenuated. We are not persuaded.

An effect is "direct" if it follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity." *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 358. The record demonstrates that the defendants' refusal to comply with the bylaws precipitated the cancellation of dividend payments to investors, including those in the United States. This cancellation directly caused Petersen's default on its loans, which were secured by the ADRs. The default led to the foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs, which were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City.

The defendants' attempt to characterize this chain as attenuated ignores the immediate financial impact of their actions. The cancellation of dividends and the exercise of voting rights in contravention of the bylaws were direct actions taken by the state-controlled entity that immediately affected the value and rights of the ADRs held in the United States. These financial injuries to U.S. investors and the seizure of securities located in New York constitute a direct effect of the defendants' commercial conduct. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618 (finding direct effect where default on bond payments affected U.S. investors).

**C. YPF's Immunity**

Finally, the defendants argue that YPF is entitled to sovereign immunity as an instrumentality of the Argentine state. However, under the FSIA, an instrumentality engaging in commercial activity is not immune for those acts. *See Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006). YPF's failure to enforce the bylaws' tender offer requirements and its facilitation of Argentina's voting rights violation were commercial actions taken in the context of the U.S. securities market. Therefore, YPF is not entitled to immunity on these claims.

**II. Act of State Doctrine**

We next address the defendants' argument that the act of state doctrine requires dismissal of this action. The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of the public acts a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). However, the doctrine is "confined to cases involving the validity of acts of foreign sovereigns." *First National City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 621 (1983).

The defendants contend that adjudicating the plaintiffs' breach of contract claims would require this Court to determine the validity of Argentina's expropriation under Argentine law, specifically by resolving a conflict between the YPF Expropriation Law and the YPF bylaws. We disagree.

The plaintiffs do not challenge the validity of the expropriation itself; they concede Argentina's sovereign power to expropriate property. Instead, the plaintiffs seek to enforce the contractual obligations voluntarily undertaken by Argentina and YPF in the bylaws—obligations that were marketed to investors as a condition of the privatization. Determining whether a foreign sovereign breached a commercial contract does not require the court to invalidate the sovereign's public acts. *See First National City Bank*, 462 U.S. at 621 (holding the act of state doctrine inapplicable where the dispute involved private contractual rights and did not require the court to interpret or enforce an act of state).

The defendants' argument that the bylaws cannot constrain the sovereign power of expropriation is a merits argument regarding the interpretation of the contract and the scope of Argentine law, not a jurisdictional bar under the act of state doctrine. The district court may interpret the bylaws and Argentine law to determine the parties' rights and obligations without passing judgment on the validity of the Argentine Congress's decision to expropriate the shares. Because the plaintiffs' claims do not require an inquiry into the validity of Argentina's sovereign acts, the act of state doctrine does not bar this suit.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the order of the district court denying the defendants' motions to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and the act of state doctrine is **AFFIRMED**.

That is not the law. The FSIA and the act of state doctrine do not immunize foreign states from liability for commercial breaches of contract merely because those breaches occur alongside sovereign acts. The Supreme Court has been unequivocal: the focus is on the *nature* of the conduct, not the sovereign’s motive or broader policy goals.  

### **I. The Commercial Activity Exception Applies Because Argentina Engaged in Commercial Conduct When It Breached YPF’s Bylaws**  

The defense argues that Argentina’s expropriation was a “quintessential sovereign act” and that the bylaws cannot transform it into commercial activity. But that misreads *Weltover* and *Nelson*. In *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992), the Supreme Court held that the commercial activity exception turns on whether the *specific act* at issue is the type a private party would engage in—*not* whether the sovereign had a broader governmental purpose. Here, the claim is not about the validity of the expropriation itself; it is about Argentina’s *failure to honor contractual obligations it voluntarily undertook during YPF’s privatization*.  

The bylaws were not some incidental footnote to the IPO—they were the cornerstone of Argentina’s marketing strategy to attract U.S. investors. As the district court found, Argentina and YPF explicitly touted these provisions in their SEC filings, stating that the government “first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws” and that failure to comply would “suspend the voting, dividend and other distribution rights.” This was not a mere formality; it was a binding commercial commitment designed to reassure investors.  

The defense’s insistence that the “nature” of the act is sovereign because it occurred alongside an expropriation is foreclosed by *Nelson*. In *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 356 (1993), the Court rejected the argument that a Saudi official’s allegedly tortious conduct was sovereign because it occurred in the course of his employment. Instead, the Court emphasized that the relevant inquiry is whether the *specific act*—here, the breach of the bylaws’ tender offer requirement—was commercial. The answer is clear: a private corporation issuing securities on the NYSE could—and routinely does—enter into tender offer obligations. Those obligations are purely commercial.  

The defense’s attempt to distinguish *Weltover* fails. In *Weltover*, the Court held that Argentina’s default on bonds sold to U.S. investors was commercial because it involved “the type of actions by which a private party engages in trade and traffic or commerce.” 504 U.S. at 614. Here, Argentina’s refusal to honor its tender offer obligation is indistinguishable. It was a commercial decision to renege on a contractual promise to investors—a decision that directly affected U.S. markets.

The defense claims that the effects on Petersen’s loans and ADRs are “too attenuated.” But that contradicts the record and binding precedent. Here, the effects were not just foreseeable—they were the *very purpose* of Argentina’s conduct. By refusing to make a tender offer, Argentina ensured that U.S. investors like Petersen would lose their rights to dividends and voting, directly harming their financial interests in New York.  

### **III. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Apply Because This Is a Contract Dispute, Not a Challenge to the Validity of Argentina’s Sovereign Acts**  

The defense argues that adjudicating this case would require the Court to “determine the validity of Argentina’s expropriation.” But that is a red herring. Petersen does not contest Argentina’s right to expropriate property. It does not challenge the YPF Expropriation Law’s validity under Argentine law. It seeks only to enforce the *contractual obligations* Argentina and YPF voluntarily assumed when they sold YPF shares to the public.  

As the Supreme Court held in *First National City Bank v. The defense’s claim that the bylaws “cannot constrain” Argentina’s sovereign power is a merits argument, not a jurisdictional one. If Argentina’s position is correct—that the bylaws do not apply to sovereign expropriations—that is a defense to be raised on the merits, not a reason to dismiss the case under the act of state doctrine.

Its failure to enforce the tender offer requirements and its role in facilitating Argentina’s voting rights violation were commercial acts in connection with its securities business.

This is not a case about the validity of Argentina’s expropriation. It is a case about whether Argentina and YPF broke a contract they made with U.S. investors—a contract they explicitly promised would protect those investors. The FSIA’s commercial activity exception applies because the specific acts at issue—breaching tender offer obligations and unlawfully exercising voting rights—were commercial in nature. # CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## IN THE UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE SECOND CIRCUIT

---

**Your Honor,**

The plaintiff-appellee's closing argument mischaracterizes the nature of this dispute and attempts to transform a sovereign act of expropriation into a mere commercial contract breach. This Court should reverse the district court's order because: (1) the commercial activity exception does not apply to Argentina's sovereign expropriation of YPF shares; (2) any alleged effects in the United States are too attenuated to satisfy the FSIA's direct effect requirement; (3) the act of state doctrine bars this Court from adjudicating claims that require invalidating Argentine law; and (4) YPF is entitled to immunity as an instrumentality of the Argentine state. We address each argument in turn.

---

## I. THE COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

The plaintiff contends that Argentina's breach of YPF's bylaws constitutes commercial activity under the FSIA. This argument fails because it improperly conflates the sovereign act of expropriation with the contractual provisions of the bylaws. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the "commercial activity" exception turns on the "nature" of the conduct, not its purpose. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). However, the Court has also recognized that sovereign acts—including expropriations—are inherently sovereign regardless of how they are structured. *See id.* at 618 ("[A] state is not stripped of its immunity simply because it has taken a step that also has a commercial aspect.").

### A. The Expropriation Itself Is a Sovereign Act

The plaintiff attempts to isolate Argentina's alleged breach of the tender offer obligation from the broader context of the expropriation. This artificial separation ignores reality. Argentina's acquisition of 51% of YPF's voting stock was effectuated through the YPF Expropriation Law, enacted by the Argentine Congress pursuant to Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution. This was a classic exercise of the sovereign power of eminent domain—precisely the type of governmental taking that has been recognized as inherently sovereign since the founding of the Republic. *See Murr v. Wisconsin*, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939 (2017) (discussing the "governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights" as a sovereign function).

The plaintiff argues that the "specific act" giving rise to the claim is the failure to make a tender offer, not the expropriation itself. This argument proves too much. Under the plaintiff's reasoning, any sovereign act could be characterized as commercial simply by identifying a separate, incidental contractual obligation. The FSIA does not contemplate such an absurd result. The tender offer provisions in the bylaws were triggered *only* by Argentina's acquisition of YPF shares—a sovereign expropriation. To characterize the failure to tender as "commercial" while the acquisition itself remains "sovereign" defies logic.

As this Court recognized in *Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006), the distinction between sovereign and commercial activity requires a careful examination of whether the foreign state was acting "as a market participant" rather than as a sovereign. Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares was not a market transaction—it was a governmental taking authorized by the Argentine Congress for the purpose of achieving "self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon supply." The law explicitly declared the acquisition to be "of public use," invoking the constitutional foundation of sovereign expropriation power.

### B. The Bylaws Cannot Convert a Sovereign Act Into a Commercial One

The plaintiff places great weight on the fact that Argentina voluntarily adopted the bylaws during the 1993 privatization. But the voluntary assumption of contractual obligations does not transform the nature of a subsequent sovereign act. Argentina agreed to tender offer procedures in the context of a *privatization*—a commercial transaction. When Argentina later exercised its sovereign power to *expropriate* the property, it acted in its sovereign capacity, not as a commercial actor.

The plaintiff's reliance on *Weltover* is misplaced. In *Weltover*, Argentina had issued bonds in connection with a sovereign debt restructuring. The Court found the commercial activity exception applied because the bond issuance was "the type of actions by which a private party engages in trade and traffic or commerce." 504 U.S. at 614. Here, by contrast, Argentina did not issue securities or engage in commerce—it enacted legislation to expropriate property for public use. No private party could engage in expropriation; it is the quintessential sovereign function.

The plaintiff's argument that the "nature" of the conduct is commercial because tender offers are "commercial terms that a private corporation issuing securities on a public exchange could certainly agree to" ignores the critical distinction between a private corporation's commercial decisions and a sovereign's exercise of eminent domain. A private corporation cannot expropriate property; only a sovereign can. The fact that Argentina agreed to certain procedural requirements in the event of an expropriation does not convert the expropriation itself into commercial activity.

---

## II. THE DIRECT EFFECT REQUIREMENT IS NOT SATISFIED

Even assuming *arguendo* that the commercial activity exception applies, the plaintiff's claims fail because any alleged effects in the United States are too attenuated to satisfy the FSIA's "direct effect" requirement.

### A. The Causal Chain Is Too Attenuated

The plaintiff contends that Argentina's refusal to comply with the tender offer requirements "directly caused" Petersen's default on its loans and the subsequent foreclosure of its ADRs. This argument ignores the multiple independent steps in the causal chain:

1. Argentina enacted the YPF Expropriation Law (a sovereign act).
2. Argentina acquired 51% of YPF's shares from Repsol.
3. Argentina (through the Intervenor) canceled dividend payments.
4. Petersen defaulted on its loans because it did not receive expected dividends.
5. Petersen's lenders foreclosed on the ADRs.

Each of these steps involves independent decision-making by various actors. The plaintiff cannot establish that Argentina's sovereign expropriation was the "immediate cause" of the foreclosure. *See Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 358 (1993) (holding that an effect is "direct" only if it follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity").

The plaintiff's reliance on *Weltover* is again misplaced. In *Weltover*, the Court found direct effect where Argentina's default on bond payments "immediately" affected U.S. investors. 504 U.S. at 618. Here, by contrast, the plaintiff does not allege that Argentina directly injured U.S. investors—it alleges that Argentina's sovereign act *led to* YPF's cancellation of dividends, which *led to* Petersen's default, which *led to* foreclosure. This is precisely the kind of attenuated chain that the Supreme Court has rejected. *See Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 358 (requiring that the effect "follow as an immediate consequence").

### B. The Alleged Effects Are Not "Direct" Under FSIA Jurisprudence

The plaintiff argues that the "cancellation of dividends and the exercise of voting rights" constituted direct actions with immediate financial impact. But these actions were taken by YPF—a separate corporate entity—not by Argentina directly. The plaintiff has not established that Argentina itself canceled the dividends or exercised the voting rights. Rather, Argentina exercised its rights as a shareholder of YPF, which YPF's board chose to honor.

Furthermore, the plaintiff cannot establish that the "direct effect" requirement is satisfied by the location of the ADRs. The ADRs were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York, but the injury alleged is not the seizure of property located in the United States—it is the loss of dividend income and voting rights. These are intangible injuries that, even if they affected U.S. investors, do not constitute the kind of "direct effect" contemplated by the FSIA. *See id.* at 360 (holding that the "direct effect" requirement is not satisfied by "the fact that the foreseeable consequences of an act . . . include some financial loss").

---

## III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS THIS SUIT

The plaintiff argues that the act of state doctrine does not apply because this is a "contract dispute" that does not require the Court to "determine the validity of Argentina's expropriation." This argument misapprehends the nature of the plaintiff's claims.

### A. Adjudication Requires Interpreting Argentine Law

The plaintiff's claims fundamentally require this Court to interpret the YPF Expropriation Law and determine whether it supersedes the YPF bylaws. Specifically, the plaintiff argues that Argentina's acquisition of YPF shares was subject to the tender offer requirements in sections 7(e), 7(f), and 28 of the bylaws. Argentina contends that the YPF Expriation Law, as a sovereign act of expropriation, implicitly supersedes any conflicting contractual provisions.

This is precisely the kind of inquiry that the act of state doctrine prohibits. As the Supreme Court held in *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964), the doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of the public acts a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." Determining whether the YPF Expropriation Law supersedes the bylaws requires this Court to interpret the scope and validity of Argentine legislation—an inquiry that necessarily implicates the validity of Argentina's sovereign acts.

### B. The Plaintiff Seeks to Invalidate Argentina's Exercise of Sovereign Power

The plaintiff argues that it does not challenge "the validity of the expropriation itself." But this is a distinction without a difference. The plaintiff seeks to enforce contractual obligations that, if interpreted as the plaintiff suggests, would effectively invalidate Argentina's exercise of its sovereign expropriation power. If this Court were to hold that Argentina was required to make a tender offer for 51% of YPF's shares—even though it acquired those shares through a valid expropriation—the Court would be passing judgment on the adequacy of the expropriation mechanism under Argentine law.

The Supreme Court has held that the act of state doctrine applies even where the dispute involves "private contractual rights," provided that the resolution of the dispute would require the Court to "interpret or enforce an act of state." *First National City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 621 (1983). Here, the resolution of the parties' dispute requires precisely such an interpretation. The Court must determine whether the YPF Expropriation Law—a sovereign act—supersedes the bylaws—a private contract. This is a question of Argentine law that the act of state doctrine bars this Court from answering.

### C. The Comity Considerations Support Application of the Doctrine

The act of state doctrine is grounded in comity—the mutual respect among nations for each other's sovereign acts. *See Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 421-22. Here, comity strongly supports applying the doctrine. Argentina enacted the YPF Expropriation Law pursuant to its constitutional authority to expropriate property for public use. The United States has long recognized the sovereign right of foreign nations to expropriate property, provided that adequate compensation is paid. *See id.* at 428-30. Adjudicating the plaintiff's claims would require this Court to sit in judgment over Argentina's exercise of this fundamental sovereign power—a result that would gravely impair comity between the United States and Argentina.

---

## IV. YPF IS ENTITLED TO SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY

Finally, the plaintiff argues that YPF is not entitled to sovereign immunity because it engaged in commercial activity. This argument fails for two reasons.

### A. YPF Was Acting as an Instrumentality of the State

YPF is a state-owned entity that was expropriated by the Argentine government. Following the expropriation, YPF operated under the control of the Intervenor appointed by the Argentine government. The plaintiff's claims arise from YPF's alleged participation in the expropriation—precisely the kind of sovereign activity that the FSIA protects.

The plaintiff cites *Garb v. Republic of Poland* for the proposition that an instrumentality loses immunity for commercial activity. 440 F.3d at 594-98. But *Garb* is distinguishable. In *Garb*, the plaintiff alleged that Poland had entered into a commercial contract to purchase goods. The Court held that Poland's commercial activity was not immune. Here, by contrast, the plaintiff's claims arise from YPF's participation in a sovereign expropriation—not from a commercial contract. YPF's alleged failure to enforce the bylaws was directly related to its status as a state-owned entity subject to government control.

### B. The Claims Against YPF Are Inseparable from the Sovereign Acts

The plaintiff's claims against YPF are based on the allegation that YPF "permitted Argentina to exercise the voting rights of Repsol's shares" in violation of section 7(h) of the bylaws. But this exercise of voting rights was part of the sovereign expropriation—the Argentine government exercised the rights of the shares it acquired from Repsol. Adjudicating this claim would require the Court to determine whether Argentina lawfully exercised those voting rights—a question that goes to the heart of the sovereign act of expropriation.

---

## CONCLUSION

The plaintiff has failed to establish that the FSIA's commercial activity exception applies or that any alleged effects in the United States were "direct." More fundamentally, the act of state doctrine bars this Court from adjudicating claims that require it to interpret Argentine law and pass judgment on the validity of Argentina's sovereign expropriation. The district court erred in denying the defendants' motions to dismiss, and this Court should reverse.

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants-appellants respectfully request that this Court reverse the order of the district court and dismiss the plaintiff's claims.

**UPON CONSIDERATION** of the closing arguments submitted by the parties, the record on appeal, and the applicable law, it is hereby ORDERED that the judgment of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York is **AFFIRMED**.

**I. Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act**

We review *de novo* a district court's subject matter jurisdiction ruling under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), 28 U.S.C. § 1601 *et seq*. *See Haring v. Blumenthal*, 46 F.3d 1347, 1350 (2d Cir. 1995).

**A. Commercial Activity Exception**

The defendants argue that the commercial activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), does not apply because the acts upon which the plaintiffs' claims are based are sovereign in nature, specifically the expropriation of YPF shares. We disagree.

Under the FSIA, a foreign state is immune from suit unless its actions fall within a statutory exception. The commercial activity exception removes immunity where the action is "based upon ... an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere ... and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has instructed that the "commercial activity" exception turns on the "nature" of the conduct rather than its "purpose." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). The relevant inquiry is "whether the particular actions that the foreign state performs (whatever the motive behind them) are the type of actions by which a private party engages in ... trade and traffic or commerce." *Id.* at 614-15.

The defendants contend that because the expropriation was authorized by the Argentine Congress pursuant to its constitutional authority, it is a quintessential sovereign act. While the enactment of the YPF Expropriation Law may have been a sovereign function, the plaintiffs' claims are not based on the validity of the expropriation itself. Rather, the claims are based on the defendants' alleged breach of the contractual obligations contained in YPF's bylaws—specifically, the failure to make a tender offer upon acquiring a controlling interest and the unlawful exercise of voting rights in violation of section 7(h). These bylaws were part of a commercial framework established during the 1993 privatization, a transaction expressly designed to attract investment in the United States through the sale of American Depository Receipts (ADRs) on the New York Stock Exchange.

The obligation to make a tender offer and the penalties for failing to do so are commercial terms that a private corporation issuing securities on a public exchange could certainly agree to and breach. The defendants' repudiation of these obligations—explicitly declaring they would not comply with the bylaws' tender offer requirements—constitutes commercial activity under the FSIA. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. The fact that this repudiation occurred in the context of a sovereign expropriation does not transform the nature of the specific acts giving rise to the claim. *See Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 356 (1993) (noting the commercial activity exception focuses on the specific act upon which the claim is based).

The defense argues that the tender offer provisions cannot convert a sovereign act into a commercial one. However, the FSIA's focus on the "nature" of the act requires us to look at the specific conduct complained of: the refusal to honor a contractual tender offer obligation. A private party can certainly enter into a contract and subsequently refuse to perform it. That the refusal coincided with a sovereign taking does not immunize the breach. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618 (rejecting the argument that a state is not stripped of immunity simply because it has taken a step that also has a sovereign aspect).

**B. Direct Effect**

The defendants further argue that the "direct effect" requirement is not satisfied because the causal chain between the expropriation and the plaintiffs' injuries is too attenuated. We are not persuaded.

An effect is "direct" if it follows "as an immediate consequence of the defendant's activity." *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 358. The record demonstrates that the defendants' refusal to comply with the bylaws precipitated the cancellation of dividend payments to investors, including those in the United States. This cancellation directly caused Petersen's default on its loans, which were secured by the ADRs. The default led to the foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs, which were held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City.

The defendants' attempt to characterize this chain as attenuated ignores the immediate financial impact of their actions. The cancellation of dividends and the exercise of voting rights in contravention of the bylaws were direct actions taken by the state-controlled entity that immediately affected the value and rights of the ADRs held in the United States. These financial injuries to U.S. investors and the seizure of securities located in New York constitute a direct effect of the defendants' commercial conduct. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618 (finding direct effect where default on bond payments affected U.S. investors). The defense's attempt to separate Argentina's actions from YPF's actions is unavailing given the Intervenor's control over YPF's operations and the Argentine government's exercise of voting rights.

**C. YPF's Immunity**

Finally, the defendants argue that YPF is entitled to sovereign immunity as an instrumentality of the Argentine state. However, under the FSIA, an instrumentality engaging in commercial activity is not immune for those acts. *See Garb v. Republic of Poland*, 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006). YPF's failure to enforce the bylaws' tender offer requirements and its facilitation of Argentina's voting rights violation were commercial actions taken in the context of the U.S. securities market. Therefore, YPF is not entitled to immunity on these claims.

**II. Act of State Doctrine**

We next address the defendants' argument that the act of state doctrine requires dismissal of this action. The act of state doctrine "precludes the courts of this country from inquiring into the validity of the public acts a recognized foreign sovereign power committed within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 401 (1964). However, the doctrine is "confined to cases involving the validity of acts of foreign sovereigns." *First National City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 621 (1983).

The defendants contend that adjudicating the plaintiffs' breach of contract claims would require this Court to determine the validity of Argentina's expropriation under Argentine law, specifically by resolving a conflict between the YPF Expropriation Law and the YPF bylaws. We disagree.

The plaintiffs do not challenge the validity of the expropriation itself; they concede Argentina's sovereign power to expropriate property. Instead, the plaintiffs seek to enforce the contractual obligations voluntarily undertaken by Argentina and YPF in the bylaws—obligations that were marketed to investors as a condition of the privatization. Determining whether a foreign sovereign breached a commercial contract does not require the court to invalidate the sovereign's public acts. *See First National City Bank*, 462 U.S. at 621 (holding the act of state doctrine inapplicable where the dispute involved private contractual rights and did not require the court to interpret or enforce an act of state).

The defendants' argument that the bylaws cannot constrain the sovereign power of expropriation is a merits argument regarding the interpretation of the contract and the scope of Argentine law, not a jurisdictional bar under the act of state doctrine. The district court may interpret the bylaws and Argentine law to determine the parties' rights and obligations without passing judgment on the validity of the Argentine Congress's decision to expropriate the shares. Because the plaintiffs' claims do not require an inquiry into the validity of Argentina's sovereign acts, the act of state doctrine does not bar this suit.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the order of the district court denying the defendants' motions to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and the act of state doctrine is **AFFIRMED**.
