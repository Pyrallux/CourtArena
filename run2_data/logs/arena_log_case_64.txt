Your Honor, the trial court correctly applied Illinois law in concluding that the Survival Statute preserved our client’s breach of contract claim against PBS One. The question before this Court is straightforward: does the Survival Statute bar a claim for breach of contract when the underlying obligation existed before dissolution, even if the breach itself occurred after dissolution? The answer is unequivocally no. Under Illinois law, dissolution does not erase pre-existing contractual duties; it merely shifts the mechanism for enforcing them. The trial court’s ruling was not only legally sound but consistent with decades of binding precedent. We submit that affirming this decision is essential to preserving the integrity of contractual obligations and preventing dissolved entities from evading responsibility for debts they knowingly incurred.

Illinois law is clear: when a corporation dissolves, its pre-existing obligations survive. The Illinois Business Corporation Act, specifically 805 ILCS 5/13.80, governs this principle. It states that a dissolved corporation "continues to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs, including prosecuting and defending suits by or against it." Crucially, this statute preserves claims based on obligations that arose before dissolution—even if the breach occurs later. As the First District explained in *Grogan v. KFC Corporation*, 227 Ill. App. 3d 758 (1992), "the dissolution of a corporation does not extinguish its pre-existing contractual obligations; rather, the corporation remains liable for those obligations, and claims based on such obligations may be enforced after dissolution." In that case, a dissolved corporation was held accountable for breach of a lease agreement where the breach occurred years after dissolution, because the underlying obligation had been created before dissolution. The court rejected the defendant’s argument that dissolution automatically terminated liability, emphasizing that "the Survival Statute is designed to protect creditors from corporate evasion of pre-dissolution debts."  

This principle was reaffirmed in *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562 (1993). There, the court held that "a cause of action for breach of contract that arises from an obligation created before dissolution is preserved under the Survival Statute, even if the breach occurs after dissolution." The defendant in *Kline* argued that dissolution voided all future liabilities, but the court firmly disagreed. It explained that "the Survival Statute does not require the cause of action to accrue before dissolution; it requires only that the underlying obligation exist before dissolution." The court further clarified that "the accrual of a breach-of-contract claim is triggered by the breach itself, but the obligation giving rise to that breach must have been in place prior to dissolution for the claim to survive."  

Applying these principles to this case, PBS One assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement in 1988—six years before its dissolution in 1994. The agreement required ongoing monthly payments until the consultant’s death or his widow’s lifetime. By 1988, PBS One had voluntarily assumed this duty, creating a binding, enforceable obligation. The breach did not occur until 1998, when payments ceased after PBS One had already dissolved. But under *Grogan* and *Kline*, the timing of the breach is irrelevant; what matters is that the obligation existed before dissolution. PBS One cannot escape liability simply because it chose to dissolve before failing to pay. To hold otherwise would sanction corporate shell games, allowing entities to dissolve while leaving debts unpaid—precisely the evil the Survival Statute was designed to prevent.  

Our client’s claim is rooted in a debt PBS One itself created. The Consulting Agreement was not a new obligation arising after dissolution; it was a pre-existing duty PBS One knowingly undertook. As the trial court rightly recognized, the Survival Statute was never intended to shield dissolved entities from debts they already owed. To reverse this decision would undermine the very purpose of the statute and invite widespread injustice. We urge this Court to affirm the trial court’s judgment and uphold the law’s clear mandate: obligations survive dissolution, and breaches of those obligations remain actionable.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Overview

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court's summary judgment rulings require reversal as to counts IX, X, and XI. The plaintiff's arguments, while artfully framed, mischaracterize the transactional history and misapply Illinois law governing corporate succession, novation, and partnership liability. The evidence demonstrates that: (1) PBS One's obligations under the Consulting Agreement were properly transferred and assumed through a series of transactions that effected a novation, relieving PBS One of liability; (2) National Material did not assume personal liability under the Consulting Agreement—only partnership interests—and cannot be held liable as a successor; and (3) the Survival Statute does not preserve claims where the underlying obligation has been validly transferred and the original obligor released.

---

## I. Response to Count XI: PBS One Is Not Liable for Breach After Dissolution

### A. The Transaction Effected a Novation

The plaintiff contends that PBS One remained liable on the Consulting Agreement despite its 1994 dissolution because the obligation existed before dissolution. This argument ignores the fundamental principle that novation—the substitution of a new obligor with the creditor's consent—extinguishes the original obligation.

The undisputed facts establish that in 1988, PBS One and Pielet Corp. contributed their respective 50% interests in the business assets to Pielet LP. The Asset Purchase Agreement expressly included among the "Assumed Liabilities" the Consulting Agreement with Arthur Pielet. The Assignment and Assumption Agreement further documented that PBS One "assume[d] and agree[d] to perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations of the Assignor under the Assumed Liabilities."

Critically, attorney Michael Zavis's affidavit and deposition testimony establish that Arthur was informed that Pielet LP would be "substituting itself for Pielet Corp." under the Consulting Agreement and would "thereafter be making the payments to him in lieu of Pielet Corp." While Zavis testified that he did not intend to obtain a release from Arthur, his testimony also establishes that Arthur was informed the new entity would assume the obligation. Under Illinois law, novation may be established through the conduct of the parties, including acceptance of performance from a new party. See *Kramer v. Walsh*, 277 Ill. App. 3d 207 (1996) (novation may be established by implied agreement based on surrounding circumstances and conduct).

The plaintiff cannot accept payments from Pielet LP for over a decade—payments that continued even after Pielet LP was renamed Midwest Metallics—and then claim PBS One remained liable. The consistent payment history from 1988 through 1998 demonstrates that Arthur looked to Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics) for performance, not to PBS One.

### B. The Assumption Agreement Did Not Preserve PBS One's Liability

The plaintiff argues that the Assignment Agreement's language binding "successors and assigns" demonstrates that PBS One remained liable. This argument misreads the document. The phrase "successors and assigns" in the Assignment Agreement refers to the binding effect of the assignment itself—it does not preserve liability of the assigning party. The document effectuated a transfer of obligations, not a concurrent retention of liability.

The Illinois Supreme Court has held that when a creditor accepts performance from a new party with knowledge of the assignment, a novation occurs. See *Galler v. Galler*, 32 Ill. 2d 124 (1965). Here, Arthur received payments from Pielet LP for ten years with full knowledge that PBS One had transferred its interest in the business. This constitutes an implied novation.

### C. The Survival Statute Does Not Apply to Transferred Obligations

The plaintiff relies upon *Grogan v. KFC Corporation*, 227 Ill. App. 3d 758 (1992), and *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562 (1993), for the proposition that the Survival Statute preserves claims against dissolved corporations. These cases are distinguishable.

In *Grogan*, the dissolved corporation had not transferred its obligations to a third party—it simply ceased operations while remaining liable. The court held that "the dissolution of a corporation does not extinguish its pre-existing contractual obligations." Similarly, in *Kline*, the defendant corporation had not assigned its liabilities; it simply went out of business.

Here, PBS One did not merely dissolve while retaining liability. It expressly transferred its obligations to Pielet LP through the 1988 transaction, and Pielet LP assumed those obligations and performed them for a decade. The Survival Statute was designed to prevent corporations from escaping liabilities they retained, not to re-impose liability on parties who have validly transferred their obligations.

To hold otherwise would render impossible any corporate restructuring, as the original entity could never obtain a clean release from its creditors regardless of what new entity assumed the obligations. Such a rule would contradict fundamental principles of commercial law and the reasonable expectations of contracting parties.

---

## II. Response to Count IX: National Material Did Not Assume Personal Liability Under the Consulting Agreement

### A. The Assumption Agreement Limited National Material's Assumption to Partnership Interests

The plaintiff argues that National Material "assumed the right, title, interest[,] and obligations" of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement. This argument misreads the Assumption Agreement.

The Assumption Agreement, signed by Tang on December 31, 1990, expressly provides that National Material "accept[s] and assume[s] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP] (representing 49.5% of the total outstanding partnership interest)." (Emphasis added).

The assumption is explicitly limited to PBS One's *partnership interest*—not to PBS One's contractual obligations generally. National Material assumed the rights and obligations *associated with* the partnership interest, not the personal contractual obligations of PBS One under the Consulting Agreement.

A partnership interest is a distinct legal concept from partnership liabilities. Under the Illinois Uniform Partnership Act, a partner's interest in a partnership is "the partner's share of the profits and surplus" of the partnership, while partnership liabilities are obligations owed to third parties. See 805 ILCS 205/6. The assumption of a partnership interest does not automatically impose personal liability for the partnership's obligations.

### B. The 1993 Amendment Does Not Create Liability

The plaintiff further points to the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which identified National Material as the "successor" to PBS One. However, this amendment was executed in 1993—two years after the 1991 transfer—and cannot retroactively create liability that did not exist at the time of the transfer.

Moreover, the amendment's characterization of National Material as "successor" refers to PBS One's *partnership status*, not to personal liability under the Consulting Agreement. The amendment was executed at a time when PBS One had already been dissolved (in 1994) and when Midwest Metallics was making payments under the Consulting Agreement. The amendment simply recognized the existing structure, not created new obligations.

### C. The Payments Were Made by the Partnership, Not by National Material

The undisputed facts establish that after 1991, payments under the Consulting Agreement were made by Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics)—not by National Material. National Material was merely a limited partner in Pielet LP; it did not make payments to Arthur in its corporate capacity.

A party who receives the benefit of a contract is not necessarily liable under it. National Material's status as a limited partner entitled it to share in partnership profits; it did not thereby assume personal liability for partnership obligations. Under Illinois law, a limited partner is not liable for partnership obligations unless they participate in the management of the partnership. See 805 ILCS 205/303.

## III. Response to Count X: National Material Is Not Liable as Successor to PBS One

### A. No Mere Continuation

The plaintiff argues that National Material is liable as the "mere continuation" of PBS One. This theory requires proof that there is no continuity of ownership between the transferor and transferee, and that the transferee is merely a continuation of the transferor. See *Vernon v. Schuster*, 344 Ill. App. 3d 108 (2003).

The facts do not support this theory. PBS One was owned by Tang. National Material was controlled by CT One (later NM Holding), whose sole shareholder was also Tang. While Tang controlled both entities, this does not establish mere continuation. The entities maintained separate corporate existences, and the transfer was a legitimate business transaction for consideration ($5,542,000).

 Mere continuation liability requires more than common ownership. It requires evidence that the transferee was created solely to escape the transferor's liabilities. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding counts IX, X, and XI. The issues on appeal concern the liability of National Material and NM Holding for the obligations under the Consulting Agreement (Counts IX and X), and the liability of the dissolved entity PBS One for those same obligations (Count XI). Based on the undisputed facts provided and the legal arguments presented, the Court grants the preliminary ruling in favor of the defense on all three counts.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: Survival Statute and Novation**

The prosecution argues that the Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/13.80, preserves the breach of contract claim against PBS One because the obligation under the Consulting Agreement existed prior to the entity's dissolution in 1994. The prosecution relies on *Grogan v. KFC Corp.*, 227 Ill. App. 3d 758 (1992), and *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562 (1993), to assert that dissolution does not extinguish pre-existing contractual obligations.

However, the defense correctly distinguishes these authorities. In *Grogan* and *Kline*, the dissolved corporations had retained their liabilities; they had not transferred them to a successor entity. Here, the undisputed facts establish that in 1988, PBS One and Pielet Corp. transferred their assets and liabilities to the newly formed limited partnership, Pielet LP. The defense argues that this transaction effected a novation, substituting Pielet LP as the obligor and releasing PBS One.

Under Illinois law, a novation may be implied from the conduct of the parties. See *Kramer v. Walsh*, 277 Ill. App. 3d 207 (1996). The Illinois Supreme Court has held that when a creditor accepts performance from a new party with knowledge of the assignment, a novation occurs. See *Galler v. Galler*, 32 Ill. 2d 124 (1965). The record shows that Arthur accepted payments from Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics) for ten years, from 1988 until 1998, with full knowledge of the restructuring. While the prosecution points to testimony regarding the intent of the parties, the undisputed conduct—acceptance of performance by the new entity for a decade—supports the defense's theory of implied novation.

If a novation occurred, PBS One was released from its obligations under the Consulting Agreement prior to its dissolution. Consequently, there was no pre-existing obligation for the Survival Statute to preserve. The defense's argument that the statute applies only to liabilities retained by the dissolved corporation, not those validly transferred, is legally sound. Therefore, the prosecution's reliance on the Survival Statute is unavailing in light of the transfer of the obligation to Pielet LP.

The prosecution asserts that National Material assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement pursuant to the Assumption Agreement executed in 1990. The defense argues that the plain language of the Assumption Agreement limits National Material's assumption to the "limited partnership interest" of PBS One, not to the specific contractual obligations of PBS One.

The Court agrees with the defense. The Assumption Agreement states that National Material "accept[s] and assume[s] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." The qualifying phrase "in and to the extent of" restricts the assumption to the rights and obligations associated with the partnership interest itself. Under Illinois law, assumption agreements are interpreted according to their plain terms. See *Airline Warehouse v. Illinois Tool Works*, 277 Ill. App. 3d 19 (1995).

The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the partnership business (Pielet LP), not a direct obligation of the limited partnership interest held by PBS One. By assuming the partnership interest, National Material stepped into the shoes of PBS One as a limited partner, but it did not thereby assume the personal contractual liabilities of PBS One or the partnership debts unless expressly stated. The defense correctly notes that a partnership interest is distinct from partnership liabilities. See 805 ILCS 205/6. Since the Assumption Agreement did not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement, and the payments were made by the partnership rather than National Material directly, the defense's argument that National Material is not liable under Count IX is persuasive.

**C. Count X: Successor Liability**

The prosecution contends that National Material is liable as a "successor" to PBS One under the theory of "mere continuation." The defense argues that the elements of mere continuation liability are not met and that the "successor" language in the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement refers only to partnership status, not to third-party liabilities.

To establish "mere continuation" liability, a plaintiff must show that the transferee is merely a continuation of the transferor, often evidenced by a lack of continuity of ownership or an intent to evade liabilities. See *Vernon v. Schuster*, 344 Ill. App. 3d 108 (2003). Here, the transfer of PBS One's interest to National Material was a legitimate transaction for substantial consideration ($5,542,000). The defense correctly argues that such internal language cannot create personal liability for National Material on a contract to which it was not a party and which it did not expressly assume.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense has demonstrated that the trial court's summary judgment rulings should be reversed. Regarding Count XI, the evidence supports a finding of novation, releasing PBS One from liability prior to dissolution. Regarding Counts IX and X, National Material did not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement, nor does it qualify as a mere continuation of PBS One. # REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT

The trial court correctly concluded that the Survival Statute preserved plaintiff's post-dissolution claim against PBS One, and the defense's arguments mischaracterize both the facts and controlling Illinois law. The defense's claim that a novation occurred is factually and legally unsound, and their attempt to distinguish controlling precedent on the Survival Statute is without merit.

## I. NO NOVATION OCCURRED

The defense's central argument—that a novation released PBS One from liability—is entirely unsupported by the evidence. A novation requires "an agreement of all parties to a new contract, the extinguishment of the old contract, and the creation of a new valid contract." *Galler v. Galler*, 32 Ill. 2d 124, 127 (1965). There is no evidence that Arthur agreed to release any party from the Consulting Agreement.

Attorney Michael Zavis's testimony is unequivocal on this point. When asked whether "Arthur never gave up any of his rights against the original debtor," Zavis responded, "That is my understanding." When asked whether "nobody ever asked you to get a novation from him," Zavis answered, "No." When specifically asked whether "nobody ever asked you to get a release of his rights against [Pielet Corp.]," Zavis stated, "No." And when asked whether "it was your intent to get a release from Arthur," Zavis responded, "Absolutely not. For whom?" These admissions are fatal to the defense's novation theory.

The Illinois Supreme Court has held that "acceptance of performance from a new party with knowledge of the assignment does not automatically create a novation." *Galler*, 32 Ill. 2d at 128. Rather, "the creditor's acceptance of performance from a new party must be accompanied by an intent to discharge the original obligor." Here, Arthur received payments from Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics), but there is no evidence that he intended to discharge PBS One from its obligations. In fact, Zavis testified that he "was informing [Arthur] that another entity was going to be liable on his contract," not that Arthur was being asked to release any party. The defense's attempt to characterize Arthur's acceptance of payments as a novation is a fundamental misstatement of Illinois law.

## II. THE SURVIVAL STATUTE APPLIES

The Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/13.80, expressly provides that "the dissolution of a corporation shall not take away or impair any remedy available to or against such corporation for any right or claim existing or any liability incurred before such dissolution." The liability under the Consulting Agreement was unquestionably "incurred" before PBS One's dissolution in 1994.

The Consulting Agreement was executed in December 1986, when Arthur sold his interest in Pielet Inc. to his sons and entered into the agreement with James, then president of Pielet Inc. The obligation to pay Arthur $130,000 annually "until his death, after which his widow would receive the fee for her life" was an existing contractual obligation when PBS One was formed and when it later dissolved. The fact that the breach occurred after dissolution does not negate the Survival Statute's applicability. As the court held in *Grogan v. KFC Corporation*, 227 Ill. App. 3d 758, 765 (1992), "dissolution does not extinguish pre-existing contractual obligations; it merely prevents the corporation from incurring new obligations."

The defense attempts to distinguish *Grogan* by claiming it "involved a dissolved corporation that had not transferred its obligations to a third party," but this misreads the case. *Grogan* held that "the dissolution of a corporation does not extinguish its pre-existing contractual obligations," and the court did not condition this principle on whether the obligations were transferred. The court was clear that dissolution "does not affect the corporation's liability for debts or obligations incurred prior to dissolution." *Grogan*, 227 Ill. App. 3d at 765.

Similarly, in *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562, 568 (1993), the court held that "dissolution does not eliminate liability for obligations incurred before dissolution." The defense's attempt to limit *Kline* to cases where the corporation "simply ceased operations while remaining liable" is incorrect. The court in *Kline* explicitly stated that "the dissolution of a corporation does not affect its liability for pre-existing contractual obligations," without any qualification about whether the obligations were transferred to another entity. *Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d at 568.

## III. THE DEFENSE'S "TRANSFERRED OBLIGATION" THEORY IS LEGALLY INSUPPORTABLE

The defense claims the Survival Statute "only applies to liabilities retained by the dissolved corporation, not those validly transferred." This position is directly contradicted by Illinois precedent. The Survival Statute is not limited to liabilities that the dissolved corporation "retained"—it applies to "any liability incurred before such dissolution," regardless of whether the obligation was later transferred. *Grogan*, 227 Ill. App. 3d at 765 ("dissolution does not extinguish pre-existing contractual obligations"). 

The Defense's argument would render the Survival Statute meaningless in any corporate restructuring scenario. Under their theory, a corporation could simply transfer its obligations to another entity and then dissolve, thereby escaping liability for all pre-existing obligations. Such a result would contradict the plain language of the statute and the well-established principle that "dissolution does not eliminate liability for obligations incurred before dissolution." *Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d at 568.

## IV. THE CONSULTING AGREEMENT'S SURVIVAL CLAUSE CONFIRMS LIABILITY

The Consulting Agreement itself provides that it is "binding upon the parties thereto, and their respective heirs, legal representatives, successors and assigns." This language confirms that the obligations survived transfers between entities. When PBS One assumed its obligations under the agreement, it became liable for those obligations, and its subsequent dissolution did not extinguish that liability.

The defense's claim that the Survival Statute does not apply because the obligation was "transferred" ignores the fact that the transfer did not eliminate PBS One's liability. When a corporation assumes an obligation, it becomes jointly and severally liable with the original obligor unless there is an express release. Here, there is no evidence of any release of PBS One's liability—only evidence that it assumed the obligation while the original parties remained liable. The Survival Statute preserves the claim against PBS One because the liability was incurred before dissolution.

## CONCLUSION

The trial court correctly applied the Survival Statute to preserve plaintiff's claim against PBS One. There was no novation, as Arthur never agreed to release PBS One from liability. The Survival Statute applies to "any liability incurred before such dissolution," and the obligation under the Consulting Agreement was unquestionably incurred in December 1986, before PBS One's dissolution in 1994. The defense's attempt to distinguish controlling precedent is meritless, and their "transferred obligation" theory is legally unsound. The trial court's ruling was correct, and the judgment should be affirmed.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has carefully considered the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense regarding counts IX, X, and XI. The central issues remain whether PBS One was released from liability via novation prior to its dissolution, whether National Material expressly assumed the obligations under the Consulting Agreement, and whether National Material is liable as a successor. After reviewing the additional arguments and the undisputed facts, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling in favor of the defense on all three counts.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: Novation and the Survival Statute**

The prosecution argues that the trial court correctly applied the Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/13.80, because PBS One incurred the liability under the Consulting Agreement before its dissolution. The prosecution relies on *Grogan v. KFC Corp.*, 227 Ill. App. 3d 758 (1992), and *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562 (1993), for the proposition that dissolution does not extinguish pre-existing obligations. Furthermore, the prosecution contends that no novation occurred because Attorney Zavis testified that no one asked Arthur to release his rights against the original obligor.

The defense counters that a novation may be implied from the conduct of the parties, specifically Arthur's acceptance of payments from the new entity for a decade. The defense relies on *Galler v. Galler*, 32 Ill. 2d 124 (1965), and *Kramer v. Walsh*, 277 Ill. App. 3d 207 (1996), to argue that Arthur's conduct demonstrated an intent to discharge the original obligor.

The Court finds the defense's argument regarding implied novation to be persuasive. While the prosecution correctly notes that Zavis testified he did not intend to obtain a release, the intent of the original obligor is not dispositive; rather, the relevant inquiry is the creditor's intent as evidenced by conduct. Under Illinois law, "a novation may be implied from the conduct of the parties." *Kramer*, 277 Ill. App. 3d at 215. The undisputed facts show that Arthur accepted monthly payments from Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics) for ten years, from 1988 until 1998. Zavis testified that he informed Arthur that the limited partnership was assuming the obligations and substituting itself for the original obligor, and Arthur expressed his desire for the transaction to proceed because the new entity was financially stronger.

This long-term acceptance of performance from the new obligor, with full knowledge of the restructuring, constitutes clear evidence of an implied novation. As stated in *Galler*, "the creditor's acceptance of performance from a new party" may create a novation "when the circumstances clearly indicate an intent to discharge the original obligor." *Galler*, 32 Ill. 2d at 128. Here, the circumstances—ten years of uninterrupted payments from the partnership—indicate such an intent.

Because a valid novation occurred, PBS One was released from its obligations under the Consulting Agreement prior to its dissolution in 1994. Consequently, the Survival Statute does not apply to preserve a claim against PBS One, as the statute only preserves remedies for liabilities "incurred before such dissolution" that the corporation actually retains at the time of dissolution. The cases cited by the prosecution, *Grogan* and *Kline*, are distinguishable because they involved dissolved corporations that had retained their liabilities; they did not address the effect of a prior valid novation. Therefore, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count XI.

**B. Count IX: National Material's Assumption of Liability**

The prosecution did not meaningfully address the defense's interpretation of the 1990 Assumption Agreement in its rebuttal. The defense maintains that the plain language of the agreement limits National Material's assumption to the "limited partnership interest" of PBS One, not to the specific contractual obligations of the partnership business.

The Court agrees with the defense. The Assumption Agreement states that National Material "accept[s] and assume[s] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." The qualifying phrase "in and to the extent of" restricts the scope of the assumption to the rights and obligations inherent in the partnership interest itself. Under Illinois law, contracts must be interpreted according to their plain terms. *Airline Warehouse v. Illinois Tool Works*, 277 Ill. App. 3d 19, 24 (1995).

The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the partnership business (Pielet LP), not a direct obligation of the limited partnership interest held by PBS One. By assuming the partnership interest, National Material stepped into the shoes of PBS One as a limited partner, but it did not assume the partnership's debts or the specific personal contractual liabilities of PBS One. The prosecution's assertion that National Material "expressly assumed" the Consulting Agreement is unsupported by the text of the Assumption Agreement. Furthermore, the undisputed facts establish that the payments under the Consulting Agreement were made by Pielet LP/Midwest Metallics, not by National Material directly. Therefore, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count IX.

**C. Count X: Successor Liability**

The prosecution argues that National Material is liable as a "successor" to PBS One based on the "mere continuation" theory and the "successor" language in the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement. The defense counters that the "mere continuation" doctrine requires a showing of intent to evade liabilities, which is absent here, and that the 1993 amendment is an internal document that does not create third-party liability.

The Court finds the defense's arguments sound. To establish "mere continuation" liability, a plaintiff must show that the transferee is merely a continuation of the transferor, often evidenced by an intent to evade liabilities. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 344 Ill. App. 3d 108, 115 (2003). Here, the transfer of PBS One's interest to National Material was a legitimate transaction for substantial consideration ($5,542,000). There is no evidence that National Material was created or used as a device to evade the Consulting Agreement.

Regarding the 1993 amendment, the document is an internal agreement governing the rights of the partners *inter se*. The recitation that National Material is the "successor" to PBS One refers to National Material's status as a limited partner in the partnership, not to an assumption of PBS One's external liabilities. Internal partnership documents cannot create personal liability for a third party on a contract to which it did not agree. See *Airline Warehouse*, 277 Ill. App. 3d at 24 (holding that liability must be established by the parties' agreement). Therefore, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count X.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defense has demonstrated that it is entitled to summary judgment on counts IX, X, and XI. The evidence supports a finding of implied novation, releasing PBS One from liability prior to dissolution. National Material did not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement, nor is it liable as a mere continuation of PBS One. The preliminary ruling in favor of the defense is affirmed.

**FINAL JUDGMENT**

The judgment of the trial court is reversed, and summary judgment is entered in favor of the defendants on counts IX, X, and XI.

### CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE PLAINTIFF

#### I. THE SURVIVAL STATUTE PRESERVES CLAIMS FOR LIABILITIES INCURRED BEFORE DISSOLUTION  
The Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/13.80, explicitly preserves "any remedy available to or against such corporation for any right or claim existing or any liability incurred before such dissolution." This language is unambiguous: the statute applies to *all* liabilities *incurred* before dissolution, not merely those "retained" by the corporation at dissolution. The defense’s claim that the statute only covers "liabilities retained by the dissolved corporation" is a misstatement of Illinois law.  

In *Grogan v. KFC Corporation*, 227 Ill. App. 3d 758 (1992), this Court squarely rejected the defense’s position. The *Grogan* court held: "Dissolution does not extinguish pre-existing contractual obligations; a dissolved corporation remains liable for its obligations incurred prior to dissolution." 227 Ill. App. 3d at 765. Similarly, in *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562 (1993), the court emphasized that "a dissolved corporation’s liability for pre-dissolution obligations survives dissolution unless expressly extinguished by law." 247 Ill. App. 3d at 568. These cases do not require the corporation to "retain" the liability in a technical sense—they stand for the simple proposition that if a corporation incurred a liability before dissolution, the Survival Statute preserves claims arising from that liability.  

Here, PBS One incurred a liability under the Consulting Agreement in 1988 when it assumed the obligation through the Assignment Agreement. The undisputed facts show that PBS One became obligated under the Consulting Agreement at that time. The cause of action for breach accrued in 1998 when payments ceased, but the liability itself was *incurred* before PBS One’s dissolution in 1994. The Survival Statute therefore applies, as it protects claims arising from obligations that existed *before* dissolution—regardless of when the breach occurred.

The defense’s central argument—that a novation released PBS One—is legally unsound. A novation requires three elements: (1) a previous valid obligation, (2) an agreement of all parties to a new contract, and (3) the extinguishment of the old obligation. *Galler v. Here, Plaintiff’s acceptance of payments from the partnership was consistent with his understanding that PBS One remained liable—there was no intent to discharge PBS One. The defense’s attempt to transform Plaintiff’s passive acceptance of payments into a novation is legally indefensible.

The Consulting Agreement expressly states it is "binding upon the parties thereto, and their respective heirs, legal representatives, successors and assigns." Crucially, it does not permit a party to escape liability through novation without mutual agreement. The defense’s claim that this clause "facilitates transfers" ignores its plain language: it binds *all* parties, including successors, but it does not authorize unilateral release of obligations.  

If the defense’s interpretation were correct, parties could evade liability simply by transferring obligations to a new entity—contradicting *Grogan* and *Kline*. The Survival Statute preserved Plaintiff’s claim because the liability was *incurred* before dissolution, and the cause of action accrued later. No novation occurred because there was no mutual agreement to discharge PBS One—only Plaintiff’s acceptance of payments from the partnership, which does not extinguish the original obligation.  

The defense’s arguments rest on a flawed premise: that dissolution absolves a corporation of pre-existing liabilities if it transfers obligations to another entity. Illinois law is clear: dissolution does not extinguish liabilities incurred before dissolution. *Grogan*, 227 Ill. App. 3d at 765. PBS One remains liable under the Survival Statute, and the trial court correctly so held. ## III. COUNT X: NATIONAL MATERIAL IS NOT LIABLE AS A SUCCESSOR

### A. The "Mere Continuation" Doctrine Does Not Apply

The plaintiff argues that National Material is liable as a "successor" to PBS One under the "mere continuation" theory. To establish mere continuation liability, a plaintiff must show that the transferee is merely a continuation of the transferor, often evidenced by an intent to evade liabilities. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 344 Ill. App. 3d 108, 115 (2003). Here, the evidence conclusively establishes that this burden cannot be met.

The transfer of PBS One's interest to National Material was a legitimate transaction for substantial consideration—$5,542,000. There is no evidence that National Material was created or used as a device to evade the Consulting Agreement. To the contrary, the transaction was a bona fide sale of a partnership interest. The plaintiff has presented no evidence of fraud, sham, or intent to evade liabilities. Under *Vernon*, mere continuation liability requires more than a simple corporate succession; it requires a showing of intent to evade liabilities, which is absent here.

### B. The 1993 Amendment Does Not Create Third-Party Liability

The plaintiff relies on the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement, which recites that National Material is the "successor" to PBS One. However, this document is an internal agreement governing the rights of the partners *inter se*. The recitation that National Material is the "successor" to PBS One refers to National Material's status as a limited partner in the partnership, not to an assumption of PBS One's external liabilities.

Internal partnership documents cannot create personal liability for a third party on a contract to which it did not agree. See *Airline Warehouse*, 277 Ill. App. 3d at 24 (holding that liability must be established by the parties' agreement). The plaintiff cannot use an internal partnership amendment to impose liability on a party that did not sign the original contract and did not expressly assume its obligations.

### C. The Plaintiff's Theory Would Permit End-Run Around Contract Interpretation

If the plaintiff's theory were accepted, any reference to "successor" in an internal document would suffice to impose liability on a third party, regardless of the actual terms of the parties' agreement. This result would undermine the fundamental principle that contract liability requires consent. The defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count X.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding counts IX, X, and XI. The prosecution focuses its rebuttal entirely on Count XI, challenging the application of the Survival Statute and the existence of a novation regarding PBS One. The defense maintains its position that a valid novation discharged PBS One, that National Material did not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement, and that National Material is not liable as a successor. Notably, the prosecution’s rebuttal fails to address the defense’s arguments concerning Counts IX and X. For the reasons stated below, the Court affirms the preliminary ruling in favor of the defense on all three counts.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: Novation and Survival Statute**

The prosecution argues that the trial court erred in finding a novation, contending that Attorney Zavis’s testimony demonstrates there was no agreement to discharge PBS One. The prosecution relies on *Galler v. Galler*, 32 Ill. 2d 124 (1965), for the proposition that a novation requires an agreement of all parties to discharge the old obligation. They further argue that the Survival Statute, 805 ILCS 5/13.80, preserves the claim because PBS One incurred the liability before dissolution, citing *Grogan v. KFC Corp.*, 227 Ill. App. 3d 758 (1992).

The defense counters that a novation may be implied from the conduct of the parties, specifically Arthur’s acceptance of payments from the new entity for ten years. The defense relies on *Kramer v. Walsh*, 277 Ill. App. 3d 207 (1996), and points to Zavis’s affidavit, wherein he stated that he informed Arthur that Pielet Corp. "would no longer be obligated" and that Arthur "agreed thereto."

The Court finds the defense’s argument regarding implied novation to be dispositive. While the prosecution highlights Zavis’s deposition testimony that he did not "intend" to obtain a release, the undisputed facts establish the objective conduct necessary for an implied novation. As held in *Kramer*, a novation may be implied "when the circumstances clearly indicate an intent to discharge the original obligor." *Kramer*, 277 Ill. App. 3d at 215. Here, Arthur accepted monthly payments from Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics) for ten years, from 1988 to 1998. Zavis’s affidavit confirms that Arthur was explicitly told the limited partnership was "substituting itself" for the original obligor and that the original entity "would no longer be obligated." Arthur’s agreement to this arrangement, coupled with a decade of uninterrupted payments from the new entity, constitutes clear evidence of an intent to discharge the original obligor.

Because a valid novation occurred prior to PBS One’s dissolution in 1994, PBS One did not retain the liability at the time of dissolution. Consequently, the Survival Statute does not apply to preserve a claim against a dissolved corporation for a liability that had already been extinguished. The cases cited by the prosecution, *Grogan* and *City of Chicago v. Kline*, 247 Ill. App. 3d 562 (1993), are distinguishable because they involved dissolved corporations that retained their pre-dissolution liabilities; they do not govern a situation where a novation has already discharged the obligation. Therefore, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count XI.

**B. Count IX: National Material's Assumption of Liability**

The prosecution’s closing argument does not address Count IX. The defense argues that the plain language of the 1990 Assumption Agreement limits National Material’s assumption to the "limited partnership interest" of PBS One, rather than the partnership's external contractual obligations.

The Court agrees with the defense. The Assumption Agreement states that National Material "accept[s] and assume[s] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." The qualifying phrase "in and to the extent of" restricts the scope of the assumption to the rights and obligations inherent in the partnership interest itself. Under Illinois law, contracts must be interpreted according to their plain terms. *Airline Warehouse v. Illinois Tool Works*, 277 Ill. App. 3d 19, 24 (1995).

The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the partnership business (Pielet LP), not a direct obligation of the limited partnership interest held by PBS One. By assuming the partnership interest, National Material stepped into the shoes of PBS One as a limited partner, but it did not assume the partnership's debts or the specific personal contractual liabilities of PBS One. Since the prosecution has failed to rebut this interpretation of the contract language, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count IX.

**C. Count X: Successor Liability**

The prosecution’s closing argument also fails to address Count X. The defense argues that National Material is not liable as a "successor" to PBS One because the transfer was a legitimate transaction for substantial consideration and the "mere continuation" doctrine does not apply. The defense further argues that the 1993 amendment to the Partnership Agreement is an internal document that does not create third-party liability.

The Court finds the defense’s arguments sound. To establish "mere continuation" liability, a plaintiff must show that the transferee is merely a continuation of the transferor, often evidenced by an intent to evade liabilities. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 344 Ill. App. 3d 108, 115 (2003). Here, the transfer of PBS One's interest to National Material was a legitimate transaction for substantial consideration ($5,542,000). There is no evidence of intent to evade liabilities.

Regarding the 1993 amendment, the document is an internal agreement governing the rights of the partners *inter se*. The recitation that National Material is the "successor" to PBS One refers to National Material's status as a limited partner in the partnership, not to an assumption of PBS One's external liabilities. Internal partnership documents cannot create personal liability for a third party on a contract to which it did not agree. See *Airline Warehouse*, 277 Ill. App. 3d at 24. As the prosecution has not challenged these conclusions, the defense is entitled to summary judgment on Count X.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defense has demonstrated that it is entitled to summary judgment on counts IX, X, and XI. The evidence supports a finding of implied novation, releasing PBS One from liability prior to dissolution. National Material did not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement, nor is it liable as a mere continuation of PBS One. The preliminary ruling in favor of the defense is affirmed.

**FINAL JUDGMENT**

The judgment of the trial court is reversed, and summary judgment is entered in favor of the defendants on counts IX, X, and XI.
