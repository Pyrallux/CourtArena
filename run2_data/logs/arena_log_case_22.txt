**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense. The issue presented is whether the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency (the Agency) is a "special district" under Article XIII A, section 4 of the California Constitution, such that the special sales tax it imposed required approval by a two-thirds vote of the electorate. The defense argues that the Agency is not a "special district" because it lacks property tax authority, relying on the majority holding in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197. The prosecution argues that the Agency is a "special district" subject to the supermajority requirement, emphasizing the "question left open" by *Richmond* and the Agency's status as an "empty shell" for the County Board of Supervisors.

Upon consideration of the arguments and the record, the Court finds that the Agency falls within the purview of Article XIII A, section 4. The tax is a "special tax" imposed by a "special district," and because it was approved by only a simple majority, it is invalid.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The "Question Left Open" by *Richmond* Permits a Functional Analysis**

The defense contends that *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 is binding precedent establishing that an entity without property tax authority cannot be a "special district." While the Court respects the holding of *Richmond*, the case background explicitly states that the present matter considers a "question previously left open" by that decision. (See *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 208.)

The majority in *Richmond* based its decision on the specific characteristics of the transportation commission before it and did not foreclose the possibility that other entities, particularly those created to circumvent constitutional protections, might be treated differently. The defense’s argument that the "question left open" language refers only to entities *with* property tax authority is unpersuasive. The language indicates that the Supreme Court recognized the potential for entities lacking property tax power to nonetheless function as "special districts" for purposes of section 4, a question we must now resolve.

**B. The Agency is a "Special District" Under the Constitution**

Article XIII A, section 4 provides that "Cities, counties and special districts may impose special taxes..." and that "No local government may impose any special tax without the approval of two-thirds of the qualified electors voting thereon." The defense argues that "special district" must be read to include only entities with property tax authority, citing the primary focus of Proposition 13 on property tax relief.

However, the constitutional text does not contain such a limitation. The Agency is a political subdivision of the state, created by the Legislature under Government Code sections 26250-26285, for the limited purpose of financing justice facilities. It possesses the power to levy a sales tax. By definition, it is a special district. To read a property tax requirement into the Constitution would be to engage in judicial amendment, effectively allowing the Legislature to define away constitutional protections.

**C. The "Empty Shell" Doctrine and the Prevention of Evasion**

The defense dismisses the prosecution’s concern regarding the circumvention of Proposition 13 as speculative. However, this argument is grounded in the factual findings of the Court of Appeal, which acknowledged that "the Agency is nothing more than an empty shell through which the Board of Supervisors of the County of San Diego can exercise its discretion."

This finding is dispositive. The Agency lacks independent governmental discretion; it is a conduit for the County. If the Court were to accept the defense’s position, the County could effectively impose special taxes for general county projects—such as the justice facilities here—by creating "shell" agencies with sales tax authority but no property tax authority. This would allow the County to levy special taxes with a simple majority vote, directly contravening the two-thirds requirement mandated by Proposition 13 for the County itself.

As Justice Mosk warned in his dissent in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 213 (Mosk, J., dissenting), interpreting section 4 to exclude such entities "would permit local governments to circumvent the two-thirds requirement by creating agencies designed solely to impose sales taxes." The Court must adopt an interpretation of the Constitution that effectuates the voters' intent to restrict tax increases, rather than one that facilitates evasion through structural manipulation.

**D. Statutory Authorization Cannot Override the Constitution**

The defense argues that the enabling legislation (Gov. Code, §§ 26271, 26273), which provided for a simple majority vote, controls. It is a fundamental principle of law that statutory provisions cannot override the Constitution. The sales tax imposed by the Agency is a "special tax" because it is levied for the specific purpose of financing justice facilities. (See *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 205.)

Because the Agency is a "special district" within the meaning of Article XIII A, section 4, the constitutional mandate applies. The Legislature cannot authorize a simple majority vote for a special tax where the Constitution demands a two-thirds supermajority. The fact that the Agency was created by the Legislature does not insulate it from constitutional requirements; rather, it makes the Legislature’s attempt to circumvent the Constitution more troubling.

For the foregoing reasons, the Court concludes that the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency is a "special district" subject to the supermajority voting requirements of Article XIII A, section 4 of the California Constitution. The sales tax imposed by the Agency is a "special tax" that required approval by two-thirds of the electorate. As the tax was approved by only a simple majority, it violates the California Constitution.

The defense’s reliance on *Richmond* is misplaced in light of the "question left open" by that decision and the unique, "empty shell" nature of the Agency. The prosecution’s arguments correctly identify the constitutional necessity of preventing the evasion of voter approval requirements.
