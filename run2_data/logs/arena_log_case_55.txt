# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Statement of the Case

The Defense respectfully submits this opening response to rebut the prosecution's arguments and to demonstrate that the search of the defendant's vehicle was constitutionally valid. The prosecution's argument rests on the premise that an unlawful pat-down occurred, which tainted subsequent consent. However, the facts establish that: (1) the initial encounter was a consensual interaction, not a seizure requiring Fourth Amendment justification; (2) even assuming arguendo that a seizure occurred, it was justified by legitimate investigative reasons; (3) the pat-down was consensual rather than compelled; and (4) the defendant's consent to search his vehicle was voluntarily given and sufficiently attenuated from any alleged prior illegality. Accordingly, this Court should reverse the suppression order.

---

## I. The Initial Encounter Was a Consensual Interaction, Not a Fourth Amendment Seizure

The prosecution incorrectly characterizes the initial encounter as a seizure requiring Fourth Amendment justification. The facts demonstrate otherwise.

### A. The Defendant Pulled Over Voluntarily

The officer's testimony establishes that he "did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, the car pulled over on its own." Suppression Tr. 13, 26, 30, 46, 59-60. The defendant was not compelled to stop by any show of authority. As the United States Supreme Court held in *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121 (1983), and subsequently in *California v. Hodari D.*, 499 U.S. 621 (1991), a seizure occurs only when there is a governmental application of physical force or submission to authority. Here, neither occurred.

The officer merely followed the defendant after observing minor traffic irregularities—an entirely lawful investigative action. The defendant's decision to pull over was entirely voluntary. In *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002), the Supreme Court emphasized that police may approach individuals and ask questions without a seizure occurring, so long as a reasonable person would feel free to leave. Here, the defendant was repeatedly told he was "free to go," reinforcing that no seizure had occurred.

### B. The Defendant Was Free to Leave

The officer explicitly testified that after explaining the citation and license forfeiture, defendant was "free to go." Suppression Tr. 19:8-10. The defendant confirmed he had a ride coming and was not intending to drive. Suppression Tr. 19:12-18, 21:5-12. This constitutes strong objective evidence that no seizure existed. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991) (holding that the test for a seizure is whether a reasonable person would feel free to decline the officer's requests or otherwise terminate the encounter).

---

## II. Even Assuming a Seizure Occurred, It Was Justified by Legitimate Grounds

Even if this Court finds that a seizure occurred, it was justified by independent, legitimate grounds.

### A. The Officer Had Reason to Investigate the Defendant's Driving

The officer observed the defendant commit traffic violations: the car was "in the center lane well past the marked stop line, and cocked to the right," and the car made an improper lane change by going to the end of the right lane and then turning into the straight lane. Suppression Tr. 11-12, 12. While the officer testified he did not initiate a traffic stop, these observations provided reasonable grounds for investigation. See *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968) (authorizing brief investigative stops when officer has reasonable suspicion of criminal activity).

### B. The License Check Revealed the Defendant Was Driving with a Suspended License

After requesting the defendant's license and registration, the officer ran a check and discovered the license was suspended. Suppression Tr. 17:17-19. This provided probable cause to cite the defendant for driving while license is suspended, a criminal offense. At that point, the officer had lawful authority to detain the defendant to complete the citation process. The detention was therefore justified under *Terry* and the automobile exception to the Fourth Amendment.

---

## III. The Pat-Down Was Consensual and Did Not Require Reasonable Suspicion of Weapons

The prosecution argues that the pat-down was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion of weapons. This argument fails because the pat-down was consensual.

### A. The Defendant Voluntarily Offered to Be Searched

The facts establish that after being told he was free to go, the defendant re-engaged the officer in conversation. The officer asked if he could ask a question, and the defendant consented. Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25. The officer then stated, "we have problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have," and asked if defendant "had anything that would concern me." Suppression Tr. 22:3-6. Rather than responding verbally, the defendant "patted himself down from top to bottom," then "placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search. And he said to me, you can search me." Suppression Tr. 22:7-15.

This sequence demonstrates voluntary, enthusiastic consent. The defendant initiated the physical gestures indicating willingness to be searched, unprompted by any force or coercion. The officer testified that "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2. The defendant's demeanor was "agreeable." Suppression Tr. 23:4-18.

### B. Consensual Searches Do Not Require Reasonable Suspicion

The Fourth Amendment requires that searches be reasonable. A consensual search is inherently reasonable when the consent is voluntarily given. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973). The prosecution's reliance on *Terry v. Ohio* is misplaced because *Terry* applies only to compelled searches. Here, the defendant affirmatively invited the search.

In *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002), the Supreme Court held that police may conduct a consensual search when the suspect voluntarily consents. The Court emphasized that "consent may be given in ways other than words," and that "an invariable requirement is that the consent must be voluntary." Here, the defendant's actions—pats down, placing hands on car, verbal affirmation—constituted voluntary consent.

---

## IV. The Consent to Search the Vehicle Was Voluntary and Attenuated from Any Alleged Illegality

Even assuming the pat-down was unlawful, the defendant's subsequent consent to search his vehicle was sufficiently attenuated to purge any taint.

### A. The Defendant Voluntarily Consented to the Search of His Vehicle

After the pat-down, the officer asked, "can I search your car?" Suppression Tr. 22:16-17. The defendant responded, "yes." Suppression Tr. 22:18-19. The officer "did not obtain any sort of written consent," Suppression Tr. 23:2-3, but oral consent is sufficient. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973).

The defendant was fully informed of the situation, was not under arrest, had been told he was free to go, and had a ride coming. His consent was given in a calm, cooperative atmosphere. There is no evidence of coercion, duress, or intimidation. The officer's weapon was never drawn. Suppression Tr. 24:3-5. The defendant was at all times "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative." Suppression Tr. 33:3-19.

### B. Attenuation Doctrine Applies

The prosecution invokes *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975), for the proposition that consent given immediately after an illegal seizure is per se tainted. However, *Brown* does not establish a per se rule. Rather, the Supreme Court in *Brown* identified several factors to consider: (1) the time elapsed between the illegality and the consent; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the initial misconduct; and (4) the degree to which the consent voluntarily given breaks the causal chain. See also *Utah v. Strieff*, 136 S. Ct. 2056 (2016) (holding that a valid warrant check intervening between an unlawful detention and consent can attenuate the taint).

Applying these factors here:

**Time and Intervening Circumstances**: Approximately twenty-five minutes elapsed from the initial stop to the discovery of evidence. Suppression Tr. 24-25. During this time, the defendant was told he was free to go, received his citation, arranged for his girlfriend to pick him up, and re-engaged the officer voluntarily. These are significant intervening events.

**Purpose and Flagrancy**: There was no flagrant misconduct. The officer's conduct was professional and courteous. The defendant was not arrested, handcuffed, or subjected to any force. The officer had legitimate reasons for the initial contact—traffic violations and the suspended license discovered during a lawful license check.

**Voluntary Nature of Consent**: The defendant initiated the interaction that led to the search. He offered to be searched, then consented to the vehicle search. His cooperation was enthusiastic, not coerced. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court has recognized that "the giving of a Miranda warning does not serve as a per se cure for all Fourth Amendment violations." Commonwealth v. McCrea, 551 Pa. 412, 421 (1999). However, here, the defendant was never detained in a manner that would render his subsequent consent involuntary.

The prosecution's reliance on *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), is misplaced. In *Royer*, the defendant was held in a small room with his luggage, without being told he was free to leave, before giving consent. That is not this case. Here, the defendant was told he was free to go, was not in custody, and voluntarily engaged the officer.

---

## V. The Officer Had Reasonable Suspicion to Justify the Pat-Down, Even Under Terry

Although the defense contends the pat-down was consensual, even under *Terry* analysis, the officer had reasonable suspicion.

### A. The Totality of Circumstances

The officer testified that he became suspicious based on: (1) where the defendant was coming from (Philadelphia); (2) the defendant's re-engagement after being told he was free to go; (3) the defendant's question about the license seizure form, which the officer found unusual; and (4) the defendant's "overly cooperative" behavior in offering to be searched. Suppression Tr. 56:21-57:9.

While some of these factors alone might not create reasonable suspicion, the totality of the circumstances must be considered. See *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411 (1981). The officer's fifteen years of experience in law enforcement provided a basis for interpreting the defendant's behavior. See *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968) (holding that officer experience is relevant to reasonable suspicion analysis).

### B. The Officer Had No Safety Concerns Does Not Foreclose Reasonable Suspicion

The prosecution emphasizes that the officer "had no safety concerns." However, the absence of subjective fear does not negate objective reasonable suspicion. The officer's testimony establishes that he formed suspicion during the encounter. Suppression Tr. 54:14 ("Again, I was suspicious"). The proper test is whether a reasonable officer in the same circumstances would have been suspicious, not whether this particular officer was fearful. See *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1 (1989) (holding that reasonable suspicion is based on the totality of the circumstances).

---

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the suppression order and deny the defendant's motion to suppress. The evidence establishes that:

1. The initial encounter was consensual, not a seizure;
2. Even if a seizure occurred, it was justified by legitimate investigative reasons;
3. The pat-down was consensual, given voluntarily by the defendant;
4. The defendant's consent to search his vehicle was voluntarily given and sufficiently attenuated from any alleged prior illegality;
5. Alternatively, the officer had reasonable suspicion to conduct a Terry pat-down.

The prosecution has failed to meet its burden of proving that the search was unconstitutional. The evidence discovered during the search should not be suppressed.

*Defense Counsel*

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, this Court finds that the search of the defendant's vehicle was constitutional. The defense has successfully demonstrated that the encounter leading to the search was consensual and that any potential taint from the initial detention was purged by the officer's clear communication that the defendant was free to leave. Consequently, the motion to suppress is denied, and the order of the lower court (if one exists granting suppression) is reversed.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Initial Encounter and Seizure**

The prosecution correctly identifies that the Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable seizures. However, the defense accurately points out that the initial interaction was justified by specific, articulable facts. The officer observed the defendant commit traffic violations, including being stopped past the stop line and making an improper lane change. See *Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 663 (1979) (stopping a vehicle is a seizure subject to Fourth Amendment scrutiny). Furthermore, the subsequent discovery that the defendant’s driver’s license was suspended provided probable cause for the issuance of a citation. Thus, the initial detention was lawful.

**II. The Pat-Down Was Consensual, Not a *Terry* Frisk**

The prosecution argues that the pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous, citing *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). The prosecution relies heavily on the officer’s testimony that he "had no safety concerns" and that his weapon was not drawn. While this testimony would indeed preclude a *compelled* pat-down under *Terry*, the defense’s argument that the pat-down was consensual is supported by the record.

The critical factual distinction lies in the sequence of events after the officer completed the paperwork for the suspended license citation. The officer testified that he told the defendant he was "free to go" and began walking back to his patrol car. At this juncture, the seizure had ended. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 435-36 (1991) (a person has been seized only if a reasonable person would not feel free to leave). The officer’s statement and physical act of retreating objectively communicated that the defendant was no longer detained.

It was only after this point that the defendant re-engaged the officer, asking again about the ID procedures, and subsequently offered to be searched. The facts indicate the defendant "patted himself down," "placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search," and verbally stated, "you can search me." This conduct constitutes clear, affirmative consent. See *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 204 (2002) (consent to a search can be implied from a person’s actions). Because the defendant was not seized at the time of the pat-down, the *Terry* standard does not apply; the search was valid based on consent alone.

**III. The Consent to Search the Vehicle Was Voluntary and Attenuated**

The prosecution contends that even if the pat-down were disregarded, the consent to search the vehicle was tainted by the prior illegality under the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine, *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975). This argument fails because the causal chain between the initial detention and the consent was broken by intervening circumstances.

The *Brown* court identified three factors to determine if consent is sufficiently attenuated from an illegal seizure: the temporal proximity of the illegality and the consent, the presence of intervening circumstances, and the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975).

Here, the temporal proximity factor weighs in favor of the defense. Approximately fifteen to twenty-five minutes elapsed from the initial stop to the search. More importantly, the "free to go" statement and the officer’s attempt to terminate the encounter serve as significant intervening circumstances. The defendant was not under arrest, was not being handcuffed, and had been explicitly told he was free to await his ride. The defendant’s voluntary re-engagement with the officer and his proactive offer to be searched demonstrate that his will was not overborne, nor was his consent merely a submission to authority. See *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 222-23 (1973) (consent must be voluntary, not the product of coercion).

The prosecution’s reliance on *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), is misplaced. In *Royer*, the defendant was not informed he was free to leave and was effectively detained in a small room without his luggage. Here, the defendant was on the side of the road, had been told he was free to go, and initiated the further contact that led to the search.

**IV. Conclusion**

The prosecution has not met its burden of showing that the search violated the Fourth Amendment. The officer lacked reasonable suspicion for a *Terry* frisk, but no frisk occurred; instead, the defendant voluntarily consented to a search after being told he was free to leave. The subsequent consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from the initial lawful detention. Therefore, the evidence discovered should not be suppressed.

**RULING**

The defense’s arguments are sustained. The search of the defendant’s vehicle was based on voluntary consent and did not violate the Fourth Amendment. The motion to suppress is denied.

# REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT

The prosecution must emphatically reject the defense's characterization of the pat-down as consensual and the subsequent vehicle search as valid. The officer's actions constituted an unconstitutional seizure and search under the Fourth Amendment because the pat-down was conducted without reasonable suspicion and therefore could not constitute valid consent.

## I. The Initial Encounter Constituted a Seizure Requiring Reasonable Suspicion

The defense incorrectly argues that the initial encounter was consensual. The facts establish that a seizure occurred when the officer activated his rotating lights behind the defendant's vehicle. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980), a seizure occurs when "in view of all the circumstances surrounding the incident, a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." When the officer activated his lights behind the defendant's vehicle, a reasonable person would not have felt free to drive away. The officer's testimony confirms he activated his lights "so the driver would know it was a police officer and as protection in case anyone would come up from behind" (Suppression Tr. 13), which objectively communicated a show of authority. This was not a mere voluntary interaction but a seizure requiring Fourth Amendment justification.

The defense cites *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121 (1983), and *California v. Hodari D.*, 499 U.S. 621 (1991), but these cases are inapposite. In *Hodari D.*, the Court specifically noted that "a person has been 'seized' within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment only if, in view of all the circumstances surrounding the incident, a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave." Here, the officer's activation of lights behind a stopped vehicle created precisely the situation where a reasonable person would not feel free to leave. The officer's testimony that the car "pulled over on its own" does not negate the seizure that occurred when the officer activated his lights behind the vehicle. The defendant had already pulled over when the officer activated his lights, making the officer's lights the triggering event for the seizure.

## II. The Pat-Down Was Not Consensual but a Compelled Search Without Reasonable Suspicion

The defense claims the pat-down was consensual because the defendant "patted himself down" and "placed his hands on the sides of the car." This characterization ignores the coercive environment created by the officer's actions. When the officer requested consent to search, the defendant was not free to leave. The officer had already discovered the suspended license, written a citation, and was in the process of completing the paperwork for the traffic violation. The officer testified that he told the defendant "he was free to go," but this statement was made while the defendant was surrounded by two police cars with rotating lights, and the officer had just completed a traffic stop involving a suspended license citation. A reasonable person in this situation would not feel free to decline the officer's requests.

The Supreme Court in *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491, 501-502 (1983), held that "when an officer, without reasonable suspicion or probable cause, approaches an individual, the individual has a right to ignore the police and go about his business." However, when the officer has lawfully detained a person, the individual is not free to leave and cannot meaningfully consent to a search. Here, the officer had already seized the defendant by activating his lights and initiating a traffic stop. The officer had just discovered the suspended license, which provided probable cause for a citation but did not provide reasonable suspicion for a pat-down for weapons.

The officer himself testified that he "had no safety concerns" and his "weapon was not drawn during the interaction" because he "had no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5, 32:2-4). This is critical. In *Terry v. The officer's own testimony establishes he had no reason to believe the defendant was armed or dangerous. Without reasonable suspicion of weapons, the pat-down cannot be justified as a Terry frisk.

The defense's claim that the defendant "voluntarily offered to be searched" ignores the coercive context. As the Supreme Court held in *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 226-227 (1973), "consent to search must be voluntary, and the prosecution bears the burden of proving voluntariness." When a person is detained by police and has not been informed they are free to refuse consent, the consent cannot be considered voluntary. Here, the officer did not advise the defendant he could refuse consent to the pat-down or vehicle search. The defendant was in the midst of a traffic stop, had just been told he would receive a citation for driving with a suspended license, and was surrounded by two police cars with rotating lights. In these circumstances, the defendant's compliance cannot be considered voluntary consent but rather submission to authority.

## III. The Consent to Search the Vehicle Was Tainted by the Prior Illegal Pat-Down

The defense argues that the consent to search the vehicle was "voluntary and sufficiently attenuated from any alleged prior illegality." This is incorrect. The pat-down was unconstitutional because it was conducted without reasonable suspicion of weapons. The Supreme Court in *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603-604 (1975), established that when consent is obtained following an illegal seizure, the prosecution bears the burden of proving the consent was sufficiently attenuated from the illegality.

The *Brown* factors clearly weigh against attenuation here. First, the temporal proximity between the illegal pat-down and the consent to search the vehicle was minimal—only moments elapsed between the pat-down and the request to search the vehicle. Second, there were no meaningful intervening circumstances. The defendant was still detained, surrounded by police cars, and had not been informed he could refuse consent. The defense cites *Utah v. Strieff*, 136 S. Ct. 2056 (2016), but this case is distinguishable. In *Strieff*, the Supreme Court found attenuation because there was an intervening circumstance—the discovery of a valid arrest warrant—between the unlawful stop and the discovery of evidence. Here, there was no intervening circumstance between the unconstitutional pat-down and the consent to search the vehicle. The officer had already completed the citation paperwork and was preparing to let the defendant go when he initiated the pat-down without reasonable suspicion. The subsequent consent to search was merely the direct result of the illegal pat-down.

## IV. The Officer's Subjective Belief of Suspicion Is Irrelevant

The defense argues that the officer's "fifteen years of experience in law enforcement provided a basis for interpreting the defendant's behavior" and that "the absence of subjective fear does not negate objective reasonable suspicion." This misstates the law. The Supreme Court in *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968), clearly held that "the officer must be able to point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant [the] intrusion." The officer's subjective belief alone is insufficient—there must be objective facts supporting reasonable suspicion.

Here, the officer testified he had "no safety concerns" and his "weapon was not drawn." The officer also testified he "observed no signs of defendant's being drunk, smelled no alcohol, and sensed no issue of intoxication" (Suppression Tr. 28:5-9, 44:21-45:15), "no indicia of intoxication or being under the influence of drugs" (Suppression Tr. 31:5-11), and "no burnt or fresh marijuana" (Suppression Tr. 38:14-17). Most critically, the officer testified he "observed no furtive or suspicious movements by defendant during the encounter" (Suppression Tr. 31:22-25, 33:16-19, 42:2-43:3).

These facts establish that there were no specific and articulable facts supporting reasonable suspicion for a pat-down. The officer's testimony that he was "suspicious" (Suppression Tr. 54:14) is irrelevant without objective facts to support that suspicion. As the Supreme Court held in *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119, 124 (2000), "unprovoked flight upon noticing the police" can support reasonable suspicion, but here there was no flight, no furtive movements, no suspicious behavior, and no objective indicia of danger.

## V. The Defense's Reliance on Drayton Is Misplaced

The defense cites *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002), but this case is inapplicable. In *Drayton*, the Supreme Court found consent voluntary because the officers did not detain the passengers, the passengers were not in a confined space, and the officers did not imply the passengers were not free to leave. Here, the defendant was in a traffic stop situation where he was surrounded by police cars with rotating lights, had just been cited for driving with a suspended license, and was not free to leave. The Court in *Drayton* specifically distinguished such situations, noting that "the police did not detain the passengers for questioning or otherwise restrict their freedom of movement" (536 U.S. at 204). Here, the defendant was clearly detained.

## Conclusion

The pat-down was unconstitutional because it was conducted without reasonable suspicion of weapons. The officer's own testimony establishes he had no safety concerns, no reason to believe the defendant was armed or dangerous, and no objective facts supporting reasonable suspicion for even a brief pat-down. The subsequent consent to search the vehicle was tainted by the prior unconstitutional pat-down and was not voluntary under the circumstances. The motion to suppress should be granted.

**II. The Pat-Down Was Consensual, Not a *Terry* Frisk**

The prosecution contends that the pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous, relying on *Terry v. Here, the defendant voluntarily consented to the pat-down. Therefore, the prosecution's argument that the pat-down was unconstitutional under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968), is inapplicable.

### B. Attenuation Analysis

Even assuming arguendo that the pat-down was unconstitutional, the consent to search the vehicle was sufficiently attenuated under *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975).

The *Brown* Court identified three factors: (1) temporal proximity between the illegal seizure and the consent; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct.

**Temporal Proximity:** Approximately fifteen to twenty-five minutes elapsed from the initial stop to the search. This is sufficient temporal distance. In *Utah v. Strieff*, 136 S. Ct. 2056, 2063 (2016), the Supreme Court found that a search occurring twenty minutes after an unlawful stop was sufficiently attenuated.

**Intervening Circumstances:** The officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go" and his physical retreat constitute powerful intervening circumstances. The defendant was not under arrest, was not being handcuffed, and had been explicitly told he was free to await his ride. The defendant's voluntary re-engagement with the officer demonstrates that his will was not overborne. In *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 40-41 (1996), the Court held that consent is valid when given under conditions of voluntariness, and that the knowledge of the right to refuse is not the sole determining factor.

**Purpose and Flagrancy:** There was no flagrant misconduct here. The officer conducted a lawful traffic stop, issued a citation, and properly informed the defendant he was free to go. The subsequent search followed the defendant's voluntary consent.

The prosecution's reliance on *Brown v. Illinois* is misplaced. In that case, the defendant was arrested without probable cause, was taken to the police station, and was questioned for an hour before giving consent. Here, by contrast, the defendant was told he was free to go, was not under arrest, and voluntarily re-engaged the officer. The prosecution's attempt to characterize this as an "illegal seizure" ignores the clear record facts.

---

## V. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

The prosecution argues that the defendant "remained in custody for the ongoing traffic violation." However, the record shows the officer completed the citation process and told the defendant he was free to go. The issuance of a citation does not constitute ongoing custody. The defendant was not arrested, was not handcuffed, and was not told he could not leave.

The prosecution argues that the defendant's "self-patting was not voluntary." However, the prosecution cites no evidence for this assertion. The officer testified that the defendant was "agreeable" and that he "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.) The defendant was at all times fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative. (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19.) There is no evidence that the defendant's will was overborne.

The prosecution argues that "the officer re-initiated contact by asking about things that would concern me." However, this re-initiation occurred only after the defendant had already asked about ID procedures and after the officer had told the defendant he was free to go. The defendant was not obligated to engage with the officer, but chose to do so voluntarily.

The prosecution argues that "there was no such intervening event" as in *Utah v. Strieff*. However, the officer's statement that the defendant was free to go—followed by the defendant's voluntary re-engagement—constitutes an intervening circumstance. In *Strieff*, the Court found that an outstanding warrant was an intervening event that broke the causal chain. Here, the defendant's voluntary re-engagement—after being told he was free to go—similarly breaks the chain.

## VI. CONCLUSION

The prosecution has not met its burden of showing that the search violated the Fourth Amendment. The record establishes that:

1. The officer had reasonable suspicion to initiate a traffic stop based on observed traffic violations.

2. The officer told the defendant he was "free to go" and began walking back to his patrol car, objectively ending the detention.

3. The defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer and consented to being patted down.

4. The defendant voluntarily consented to a search of his vehicle.

5. The officer had no safety concerns and no reasonable suspicion to conduct a *Terry* frisk—but no frisk was necessary because the defendant consented.

6. Any consent was sufficiently attenuated from the initial lawful detention.

The prosecution's arguments rest on a mischaracterization of the facts and a misapplication of precedent. The "free to go" statement was not a "legal fiction"—it was a clear communication that the detention had ended. The pat-down was not an unconstitutional frisk—it was a consensual encounter. The vehicle search was not the fruit of an poisonous tree—it was the product of voluntary consent.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm its ruling denying the motion to suppress.

**FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, and upon a thorough review of the factual findings and applicable legal precedents, this Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The motion to suppress is denied. The search of the defendant's vehicle was constitutional, as the defendant's consent to search was voluntary and not the product of an unconstitutional seizure.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Seizure Lawfully Concluded Prior to the Pat-Down**

The prosecution argues that the officer’s statement that the defendant was "free to go" was a "legal fiction" because the defendant was awaiting a ride and the officer's lights remained activated. The prosecution relies on *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), to assert that a defendant remains detained during a traffic stop until the citation process is fully concluded. However, the prosecution’s reliance on *Royer* is misplaced in light of the specific facts found by the court.

In *Royer*, the police officers did not tell the defendant he was free to leave and effectively retained control over his luggage and person. 460 U.S. at 501-502. Here, the record clearly establishes that the officer completed the citation process, explained the license forfeiture procedures, and explicitly told the defendant he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). Crucially, the officer testified that he "started walking back to my car" immediately thereafter. (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). This physical withdrawal is a significant objective factor indicating the end of the seizure.

Under *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 435-436 (1991), a seizure occurs only if a reasonable person would not feel free to leave. While the presence of rotating lights and the issuance of a citation contribute to the totality of the circumstances, they do not render the officer’s explicit statement of freedom and physical retreat meaningless. The defendant was not under arrest, was not handcuffed, and was not physically restrained. The fact that he chose to wait for a ride rather than walk away does not transform a consensual encounter into a seizure. See *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 554 (1980). Therefore, the Court finds that the seizure had concluded before the defendant re-engaged the officer.

**II. The Pat-Down Was Consensual, Not an Unconstitutional *Terry* Frisk**

The prosecution correctly notes that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a *Terry* frisk, as the officer admitted he had "no safety concerns." *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). However, the prosecution’s argument fails because the encounter in question was not a *Terry* frisk; it was a consensual interaction.

The sequence of events is dispositive. After the officer told the defendant he was free to go and walked away, the defendant re-engaged the officer by asking again about the ID procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The officer then asked if he could ask a question, to which the defendant agreed. Only then did the officer ask if the defendant had anything that would concern him. In response, the defendant "patted himself down," "placed his hands on the sides of his car, spread his legs with the intent of me to search," and verbally stated, "you can search me." (Suppression Tr. 22:7-12).

These actions constitute clear, affirmative consent. As held in *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 204 (2002), consent to a search can be implied from a person’s actions. The defendant’s proactive gestures—patting himself down and assuming the position—demonstrate a willingness to cooperate rather than a submission to authority. Because the defendant was not seized at the time of the pat-down, the *Terry* standard does not apply. The prosecution’s argument that the defendant’s actions were a response to "police dominance" is unsupported by the record, which shows the defendant was polite, cooperative, and the sole initiator of the renewed contact.

**III. The Consent to Search the Vehicle Was Voluntary and Attenuated**

The prosecution contends that the consent to search the vehicle was the fruit of the poisonous tree, citing *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975). This argument fails because the pat-down was not illegal, and even if it were, the consent to search the vehicle was sufficiently attenuated from any prior illegality.

The *Brown* court identified three factors to determine attenuation: temporal proximity, the presence of intervening circumstances, and the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. 422 U.S. at 603-04. These factors weigh heavily in favor of the defense.

First, approximately twenty-five minutes elapsed from the initial stop to the discovery of the evidence. Second, the officer’s statement that the defendant was "free to go," his physical retreat, and the defendant’s voluntary re-engagement constitute significant intervening circumstances. See *Utah v. Strieff*, 136 S. Ct. 2056, 2063 (2016) (recognizing that an intervening circumstance can break the causal chain between an illegal stop and the discovery of evidence). Here, the defendant’s own actions—asking another question and offering to be searched—served as the intervening act that purged any taint of the initial detention.

Third, there was no flagrant misconduct. The officer conducted a lawful traffic stop based on observed violations, *Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 663 (1979), and lawfully issued a citation for the suspended license. The subsequent search was conducted only after the defendant voluntarily consented. Under the totality of the circumstances, *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 40-41 (1996), the consent was valid.

**RULING**

The defense’s arguments are sustained. The search of the defendant's vehicle was based on voluntary consent that was not the product of an unconstitutional seizure. The motion to suppress is denied.
