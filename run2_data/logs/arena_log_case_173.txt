## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts of this case are straightforward and undisputed. On March 7, 2008, J.M.Y. filed suit against R.R., alleging that R.R. was the biological father of a child born during J.M.Y.'s marriage. J.M.Y. sought reimbursement from R.R. for child support payments he had made. On April 9, 2008, J.M.Y. amended his petition to add A.F.Y. (the mother) as a defendant, seeking reimbursement for past child support payments from both R.R. and A.F.Y., as well as future child support payments from both defendants.

Critically, J.M.Y. never filed a disavowal action to terminate his parental rights. He never sought a judicial declaration that he is not the legal father of the child. Despite this, he filed suit seeking to shift his support obligations to R.R. and A.F.Y. The trial court granted exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action, dismissing J.M.Y.'s suit. This appeal followed.

---

## II. THE PLAINTIFF LACKS A RIGHT OF ACTION

A right of action is the legal interest or capacity a party must possess to invoke the jurisdiction of the court and obtain relief. As this Court recognized in the updated ruling, J.M.Y. lacks this essential standing because he remains the presumed father under Louisiana Civil Code Article 209.

The presumption of legitimacy under Article 209 is not a mere procedural formality; it is a substantive rule that creates binding legal obligations. In *LeBlanc v. LeBlanc*, 2003-2142 (La. 6/27/05), 906 So.2d 1102, the Louisiana Supreme Court explicitly held that a presumed father cannot seek reimbursement from a biological father without first successfully disavowing his own paternity. The Court stated that the right to reimbursement is "inextricably tied to the prior termination of one's own parental duty." J.M.Y. has never initiated a disavowal proceeding, never obtained a judicial declaration of non-paternity, and therefore retains all legal obligations as the child's father.

In *Hudson v. Hudson*, 2005-1037 (La. App. 1 Cir. 12/28/05), 925 So.2d 657, this Court held that a plaintiff must have a "legal interest" in the subject matter of the suit. J.M.Y. cannot demonstrate such an interest because his obligation to support the child remains legally enforceable. He cannot selectively accept the benefits of paternal status while rejecting its burdens. The plaintiff argues that he merely seeks reimbursement, not termination of parental rights—but this distinction is legally inconsequential. Under Louisiana law, a presumed father cannot maintain an action for reimbursement while retaining his parental status.

---

## III. THE PLAINTIFF LACKS A CAUSE OF ACTION

Even if J.M.Y. had standing, his petition fails to state a valid cause of action. A cause of action exists when the petition states a valid legal theory entitling the plaintiff to relief. Here, Louisiana law provides no mechanism for the relief sought.

J.M.Y. contends that Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 creates a standalone right to reimbursement from the biological father. This argument has been explicitly rejected by the Louisiana Supreme Court. In *Broussard v. Broussard*, 2000-0538 (La. 1/19/01), 775 So.2d 1067, the Court held that Article 227 does not authorize a presumed father to bypass the disavowal process. The statute defines the obligation of biological fathers only after paternity is judicially determined. It does not create a cause of action for presumed fathers to seek reimbursement without first extinguishing their own obligation.

The plaintiff cannot create a legal theory where none exists. The right to reimbursement arises only after the presumed father's duty has been judicially terminated through the disavowal process. J.M.Y. has never pursued this remedy. His attempt to characterize his claim as "only seeking reimbursement, not termination of parental rights" fails as a matter of law. *Broussard* makes clear that reimbursement is not an independent remedy—it is contingent upon the prior termination of the presumed father's obligation.

---

## IV. THE CLAIMS AGAINST A.F.Y. ARE WITHOUT MERIT

The plaintiff's claims against A.F.Y., the mother, are equally deficient and should be dismissed. Under Louisiana law, the mother has no obligation to reimburse a presumed father for support payments. The obligation to support a child rests with the legal parents, and until J.M.Y. successfully disavows his paternity, he remains the legal father.

J.M.Y. asserts claims for future child support on behalf of the child against both R.R. and A.F.Y. However, he has no standing to assert claims on behalf of the child. Child support is owed to the child, not to another parent. Until J.M.Y. is no longer the legal father, he cannot circumvent his own obligations by seeking payment from the mother.

---

## V. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT AFFIRMANCE

The plaintiff's arguments, if accepted, would undermine the integrity of Louisiana's family law framework. As the *LeBlanc* Court warned, permitting presumed fathers to circumvent the disavowal process would render Article 209 "a meaningless formality." The law exists to protect children from uncertainty and to ensure the finality of court orders.

Allowing J.M.Y.'s claim would destroy the finality of divorce decrees and child support orders. It would permit presumed fathers to make strategic calculations: retain parental status to avoid liability while simultaneously seeking reimbursement from others. This result is precisely what Louisiana law forbids.

The plaintiff chose not to pursue the disavowal process. He cannot now seek to rewrite the rules to suit his convenience. The law provides a clear path for a presumed father who believes he is not the biological father: file a disavowal action, obtain a judicial declaration, and then seek reimbursement. J.M.Y. skipped this step. His failure to follow the mandatory procedure bars his suit.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Honorable Court affirm the trial court's judgment dismissing J.M.Y.'s suit with prejudice. The plaintiff has failed to demonstrate either a right of action or a cause of action. His suit is barred by the mandatory disavowal process required under Louisiana law.

The trial court correctly granted the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. No legal principle permits a presumed father to shift his support obligations to another without first terminating his own duties through judicial decree. The judgment should be affirmed.
