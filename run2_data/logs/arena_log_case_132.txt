FACTS:
The State of Hawaii appeals a circuit court order granting a motion to suppress evidence filed by defendant David A. Haili before trial. Defendant was charged with possession of prohibited firearm in violation of Hawaii Revised Statutes § 134-8 (1976).

At approximately 10:30 p.m. on May 5, 1979, a security guard posted at the Kapaa Quarry heard 6-7 gunshots and also heard a bullet hit a building next to his. He immediately reported the incident to the police and added that he felt that the shots were coming from the unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway. The guard furnished no other information.

A police bulletin of the incident was broadcasted. Within minutes, Officer Charles Davis was at the designated portion of the freeway and stood with his rifle at the chain link fence which serves to barricade the uncompleted end of the freeway. A few minutes later, Officer Davis observed a car travelling at a reasonable speed on the uncompleted freeway approaching with headlights on. He stopped the car. From a distance of sixty feet, he ordered the four occupants out of the car. 
At this point, additional officers arrived and they conducted a “pat down” of the four occupants. The four then sat on the ground and were guarded by one officer. The other officers scanned the interior of the car with a flashlight and spotted a .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor. They also saw a black case on the rear floor partially concealed by the front seat. Upon pushing the front seat forward, the officers saw a ukulele case, unlatched although the cover was down. Officer Davis opened the case and found a modified rifle and plastic boxes containing .22-caliber rifle ammunition.

**Opening Argument for the Prosecution**  

Your Honor, the evidence obtained from the warrantless flashlight scan of the vehicle’s interior and the subsequent search was entirely constitutional. The officers acted within their authority under the Fourth Amendment, and the facts known to them at the time unequivocally established probable cause to believe a crime was being committed. We submit that the circuit court erred in suppressing this evidence, and this Court should reverse that order.  

### I. The Flashlight Scan Was Not a Constitutional "Search"  
The Fourth Amendment does not prohibit an officer from using a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle when the officer is lawfully positioned and the object is plainly visible. In *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), the Supreme Court squarely held that shining a flashlight into a car at a traffic checkpoint to observe contraband in plain view does not constitute a search requiring a warrant. The Court emphasized: "The use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of an automobile, when the officer is lawfully positioned and the object is plainly visible, does not intrude upon any reasonable expectation of privacy." Id. at 740–41. Here, Officer Davis was lawfully present at the barricaded, uncompleted freeway—exactly where the security guard reported gunshots had occurred—and his flashlight scan merely revealed what was already visible from outside the vehicle. The rifle casing on the front floor was plainly observable under the flashlight’s illumination, and no physical intrusion occurred. This is precisely the scenario *Texas v. Brown* sanctioned.  

### II. Probable Cause Existed for the Warrantless Vehicle Search  
The officers had ample probable cause to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime—specifically, the firearm used in the reported shooting. Probable cause exists when "the facts and circumstances within the officers’ knowledge and of which they had reasonably trustworthy information [are] sufficient to warrant a prudent man in believing that the [suspect] had committed or was committing an offense." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 231 (1983). The "totality of the circumstances" must be assessed, including the reliability of the informant and the corroboration of details by the officers. Id. at 233.  

Here, the security guard provided specific, trustworthy information: he heard six to seven gunshots and a bullet striking a building adjacent to his post, and he explicitly identified the source as the unfinished portion of the H-3 freeway. This was not a vague or anonymous tip but a contemporaneous report from a witness directly observing criminal activity. Officer Davis then immediately verified the guard’s account by arriving at the *exact location* within minutes and observing a vehicle traveling on the *uncompleted, barricaded freeway*—an area not open to public traffic, where no legitimate reason existed for a car to be present, especially at 10:30 p.m. The fact that the car was on a restricted construction site, barricaded by chain-link fencing, and precisely where gunshots were reported, created an immediate and reasonable inference that the vehicle was connected to the crime. As the Court stated in *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 418 (1981), probable cause "requires only a probability or substantial chance of criminal activity, not an actual showing of such activity." The circumstances here—gunshots reported at a specific location, followed by a vehicle found on the barricaded, unfinished freeway at the precise time and place—created a "fair probability" that the car contained evidence of the shooting.  

This is not a case of "reasonable suspicion" alone; it is probable cause. The Supreme Court has long recognized that vehicles may be searched without a warrant when probable cause exists due to their inherent mobility. In *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 153 (1925), the Court held that "for the purpose of searching for and seizing contraband or stolen goods . . . a search of an automobile without a warrant is reasonable where there is probable cause to believe that it contains such articles." Here, the officers had probable cause to believe the vehicle contained a firearm used in the shooting—evidenced by the rifle casing visible after the flashlight scan, the location, and the suspicious circumstances. The warrantless search was thus justified under the automobile exception.  

### III. The Officers’ Actions Were Lawful and Reasonable  
The officers’ conduct was consistent with established Fourth Amendment principles. After stopping the vehicle at a safe distance (sixty feet), they ordered the occupants out—a permissible safety measure under *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049–50 (1983), given the imminent threat of firearms indicated by the gunfire report. The subsequent flashlight scan was not a search but a lawful observation of evidence in plain view, as confirmed in *Texas v. Brown*. The discovery of the rifle casing and the black ukulele case containing a modified rifle and ammunition provided direct, corroborating evidence of the crime.  

In sum, the facts known to the officers at the time—specific, reliable information about gunshots at a precise location, followed by the immediate corroboration of a vehicle on a barricaded, unfinished freeway where no lawful traffic occurred—established probable cause. The flashlight scan was constitutionally permissible because it merely illuminated evidence already visible from outside the vehicle. The warrantless search of the vehicle was fully justified under the automobile exception. The circuit court’s suppression order was legally erroneous, and this Court should reverse it to ensure that evidence of a serious crime is not excluded.

# DEFENDANT'S OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE SUPREME COURT OF THE STATE OF HAWAII

### STATE OF HAWAII, Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Appellee

---

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits that the circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress evidence. The prosecution's argument fails for multiple reasons: (1) the initial stop of the vehicle was unlawful absent reasonable articulable suspicion; (2) the subsequent pat-down and vehicle search exceeded the scope of any lawful authority; and (3) the "plain view" doctrine does not justify the warrantless search of the closed ukulele case. This Court should affirm the circuit court's suppression order.

### I. The Initial Stop of the Vehicle Was Unlawful

The Fourth Amendment requires that a investigatory stop of a vehicle be supported by reasonable articulable suspicion that criminal activity is occurring. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). The prosecution incorrectly conflates the low threshold of reasonable suspicion with the higher requirement of probable cause, but the facts here fail to establish even reasonable suspicion.

Officer Davis arrived at the H-3 freeway construction site based solely on a security guard's report that gunshots had been heard from that general area. The guard provided no description of any vehicle, no license plate number, no information about the number of shooters, and no indication that any vehicle was involved in the shooting. The guard merely stated he "felt" the shots were coming from the unfinished portion of the freeway. This amounted to no more than a vague allegation that gunshots had occurred somewhere in the vicinity of a large construction site.

When Officer Davis arrived, he observed a single vehicle traveling on the uncompleted freeway with its headlights on. The prosecution argues this was suspicious because the area was barricaded. However, the fact that a vehicle was on an unfinished freeway—without more—does not establish reasonable suspicion of criminal activity. Unfinished freeways, even when partially barricaded, may be accessed for legitimate purposes such as construction work, authorized entry, or emergency situations. The officer observed no traffic violations, no erratic driving, and no other indicia of criminal conduct.

In Brown v. Texas, 443 U.S. 47, 51 (1979), the Supreme Court held that a stop was unlawful where the officer had no specific basis for believing the defendant was involved in criminal activity. Similarly, here, the mere presence of a vehicle on a road—without any additional suspicious circumstances—cannot justify an investigatory stop. The prosecution has cited no authority supporting the proposition that a vehicle on an unfinished roadway at 10:30 p.m. constitutes sufficient grounds for a stop. This factor alone requires affirmance of the suppression order.

### II. The Pat-Down of the Occupants Was Unlawful

Even assuming arguendo that the initial stop was lawful, the officers had no justification for conducting a pat-down of all four occupants. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 24 (1968), permits a frisk only when the officer has reasonable belief that the person is armed and dangerous. The prosecution argues this was justified because of the report of gunfire, but this reasoning proves too much.

The officers had no information suggesting that any of the four occupants were armed or dangerous. They had no description of any suspect. They observed no weapons, no suspicious movements, and no behavior indicating danger. The report of gunshots, standing alone, does not create reasonable suspicion that every person in the area is armed. As the Court held in Ybarra v. Illinois, 444 U.S. 85, 92-93 (1979), a police officer's authority to frisk a patron of a bar does not extend to all customers present based solely on the officer's belief that a crime occurred on the premises.

Here, the officers ordered all four occupants out of the car and conducted pat-downs without any individualized suspicion that any particular occupant was armed. This was a generalized search of all persons present, unsupported by the requisite particularized suspicion. The pat-downs were therefore unconstitutional, and any evidence derived therefrom should be suppressed.

### III. The Flashlight Scan of the Vehicle Interior Was a Search Requiring Probable Cause

The prosecution relies heavily on Texas v. Brown, 460 U.S. 730 (1983), to argue that the flashlight scan was not a search. This argument misapprehends the holding of that case and ignores critical distinctions in the facts.

In Texas v. Brown, the officer was conducting a lawful traffic checkpoint when he observed a green nylon pouch protruding from the defendant's pocket. The Court held that the officer's use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of the vehicle did not transform a lawful observation into an unlawful search. However, the critical factor in Brown was that the officer was lawfully present at a checkpoint and the contraband was actually in plain view—not merely potentially visible.

Here, the situation is materially different. Officer Davis was not conducting a lawful traffic stop or checkpoint. He had unlawfully stopped the vehicle. More importantly, the officers did not simply observe what was in plain view from their lawful position outside the vehicle. Rather, they actively scanned the interior with a flashlight, moving from the front to the back of the vehicle, and it was only through this active illumination that they observed the rifle casing on the front floor.

The prosecution's argument that the rifle casing was "plainly visible" is undermined by the fact that the casing was on the floor of the vehicle—hardly an obvious location visible from outside without active search. The officers had to deliberately use a flashlight to illuminate the interior to see the casing. This constitutes a search. See Arizona v. Hicks, 480 U.S. 321, 324-25 (1987) (holding that moving stereo equipment to read serial numbers constituted a search, even after lawful entry).

Even if the flashlight scan were somehow permissible, it could not create probable cause where none existed before. The discovery of a single rifle casing—without more—does not establish probable cause to believe the vehicle contains evidence of a crime. The casing could have been deposited there long ago, could have been placed there by someone else, or could have numerous innocent explanations.

### IV. The Search of the Ukulele Case Exceeded Any Lawful Authority

The most egregious violation occurred when Officer Davis pushed the front seat forward to better view the interior, discovered a black case, and then opened it to find the modified rifle and ammunition. This was a warrantless search of a closed container inside a vehicle, requiring both probable cause and exigent circumstances—neither of which existed.

The prosecution invokes the automobile exception to argue that a warrantless search of a vehicle is permissible upon probable cause. However, the automobile exception permits a search of the vehicle itself, not a warrantless search of closed containers found within the vehicle. In United States v. Ross, 456 U.S. 798, 823 (1982), the Court held that a warrantless search of a vehicle is permissible only if probable cause exists to believe the vehicle contains contraband—and even then, the search must be limited to areas where contraband might be found. However, the Court has also held that closed containers within a vehicle require additional justification. See Florida v. Jardines, 569 U.S. 1, 11 (2013) (holding that a dog sniff of a closed porch was a search requiring probable cause).

Here, the ukulele case was closed—albeit unlatched—and its cover was down. Officer Davis had no warrant, no probable cause to believe the case contained contraband, and no exigent circumstances justifying immediate action. The mere fact that a case was in a vehicle does not authorize its warrantless opening. The prosecution has cited no authority permitting the warrantless opening of a closed musical instrument case based on the facts presented here.

The prosecution argues that the discovery of the rifle casing provided probable cause. This is incorrect. A single rifle casing on the floor of a vehicle does not establish probable cause to believe that additional evidence—specifically, the firearm itself—will be found in a closed case inside the vehicle. Probable cause requires a "fair probability" that evidence of a crime will be found in the particular place to be searched. See Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). The prosecution has not established this standard with respect to the ukulele case.

### V. The Totality of Circumstances Did Not Establish Probable Cause

The prosecution's probable cause argument rests on a chain of speculation: gunshots were reported, a vehicle was found on an unfinished freeway, therefore the vehicle must contain the firearm. This reasoning fails for several reasons.

First, the guard's report established only that gunshots had been fired in the vicinity of the H-3 freeway construction site. The guard did not see who fired the shots, did not observe any vehicle, and provided no description of any suspect or vehicle. The guard's statement that he "felt" the shots were coming from the unfinished portion of the freeway was speculative, not based on observation.

Second, the presence of a vehicle on the unfinished freeway does not create an inference of criminal activity. As noted above, there are numerous legitimate reasons for a vehicle to be on a construction site, including authorized entry, emergency access, or construction-related purposes. The prosecution's argument that "no legitimate reason existed" is speculative and unsupported by any evidence in the record.

Third, even assuming probable cause existed to search the vehicle, it did not extend to the closed ukulele case. The automobile exception permits a search of the vehicle and its contents only to the extent justified by the probable cause. See United States v. Ross, 456 U.S. at 824 (holding that the scope of a warrantless search is defined by the probable cause justification). Opening a closed case to search its contents exceeds this scope.

### VI. The Circuit Court's Order Should Be Affirmed

For the foregoing reasons, the defense submits that the circuit court correctly granted the motion to suppress evidence. The initial stop was unlawful for lack of reasonable suspicion. The pat-down of all four occupants was unlawful for lack of particularized suspicion of danger. The flashlight scan of the vehicle interior was a search requiring probable cause, which did not exist. And the warrantless opening of the closed ukulele case was unconstitutional.

The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that any of the officers' actions were justified under established Fourth Amendment doctrine. The circuit court's suppression order was correct, and this Court should affirm it.

Respectfully submitted,

**Defense Counsel**

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF HAWAII, Appellant, v. DAVID A. HAILI, Appellee**

**NO.**

**APPEAL FROM THE CIRCUIT COURT OF THE FIRST CIRCUIT**

**PREAMBLE**

This matter comes before the Court on the State of Hawaii’s appeal from the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence. The State argues that the warrantless search of the vehicle and the seizure of the modified rifle and ammunition were justified under the Fourth Amendment. After reviewing the facts provided and the arguments presented by both the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Initial Stop of the Vehicle Was Supported by Reasonable Suspicion**

The Fourth Amendment protects individuals against unreasonable seizures, and an investigatory stop constitutes a seizure. *Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 653 (1979). To conduct such a stop, an officer must have reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968).

The Defense argues that the stop was unlawful because the security guard’s report lacked a description of a vehicle or suspect, and the mere presence of a car on an unfinished freeway is insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion, citing *Brown v. Texas*, 443 U.S. 47 (1979). While *Brown v. Texas* holds that mere presence in a high-crime area does not justify a stop, the facts here distinguish this case. The vehicle was not merely present in a general area; it was observed on the "uncompleted portion of the H-3 freeway," an area specifically identified by the security guard as the source of recent gunfire. Furthermore, the facts indicate this area was barricaded by a chain-link fence. The presence of a vehicle on a barricaded, uncompleted freeway at 10:30 p.m. suggests unauthorized entry or trespass, providing an independent basis for reasonable suspicion. When coupled with the specific, contemporaneous report of gunshots originating from that exact location, the officers had reasonable suspicion to believe the vehicle’s occupants were involved in criminal activity. Therefore, the stop was lawful.

**II. The Pat-Down of the Occupants Was Justified**

Once a lawful stop is made, an officer may order the occupants out of the vehicle and conduct a pat-down search for weapons if the officer reasonably believes the occupants are armed and dangerous. *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049–50 (1983). The Defense contends that the pat-down violated *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1979), which prohibits frisking all patrons in a bar without individualized suspicion. However, *Ybarra* is factually distinct; it involved a raid on a public establishment where the connection between the patrons and the criminal activity was tenuous. Here, the officers had stopped the only vehicle at the scene of a reported shooting involving multiple gunshots. Given the specific report of gunfire, it was entirely reasonable for the officers to believe the occupants might be armed and dangerous. The pat-down was a valid protective measure under *Terry* and *Long*.

**III. The Flashlight Scan Did Not Constitute a Search**

The Defense argues that the flashlight scan was a search requiring probable cause, relying on *Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321 (1987). However, *Hicks* involved the movement of equipment to view a serial number, which constituted a "search" because it involved a physical intrusion that revealed otherwise hidden information. The use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle from a lawful vantage point does not constitute a search. *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983). As established in Section I, Officer Davis was lawfully present at the scene. The rifle casing on the front floor was observed from outside the vehicle using a flashlight. This falls squarely within the "plain view" doctrine, as the officer did not physically intrude into the vehicle to see the casing. Therefore, the scan was constitutional.

**IV. The Search of the Ukulele Case Was Justified by the Automobile Exception**

The discovery of the rifle casing provided the officers with probable cause to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime—specifically, the firearm used in the reported shooting. Under the automobile exception to the Fourth Amendment, police may search a vehicle without a warrant if they have probable cause to believe it contains contraband. *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 153 (1925). This probable cause justifies a search of every part of the vehicle and its contents that may conceal the object of the search. *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 823 (1982).

The Defense argues that a single rifle casing was insufficient to establish probable cause to search the closed ukulele case, citing *Florida v. Jardines*, 569 U.S. 1 (2013). However, *Jardines* concerned the use of a drug-sniffing dog on a home’s curtilage, an area with the highest expectation of privacy. A vehicle traveling on a public highway—or a barricaded freeway—does not share the same sanctity. Furthermore, under *Ross*, if probable cause justifies the search of a vehicle, it justifies the search of any container within that vehicle that could hold the contraband. A ukulele case is a container capable of holding a rifle. Given the report of gunshots, the presence of the vehicle at the scene, and the discovery of the rifle casing, there was a "fair probability" that the firearm would be found in the vehicle. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). Consequently, the search of the ukulele case was lawful.

**CONCLUSION**

The circuit court erred in granting the motion to suppress. The officers acted with reasonable suspicion in stopping the vehicle, were justified in ensuring their safety through pat-downs, and lawfully observed evidence in plain view. The subsequent discovery of the rifle casing established probable cause, justifying the full search of the vehicle and its contents under the automobile exception.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is **REVERSED**.

## INTRODUCTION

## I. THE INITIAL STOP WAS UNLAWFUL FOR LACK OF REASONABLE SUSPICION

The prosecution contends that the stop was justified because the security guard reported gunshots emanating from the specific location where the vehicle was observed. This argument fails because it conflates general suspicion about a location with individualized suspicion about a particular vehicle and its occupants.

In *Brown v. Texas*, 443 U.S. 47, 51 (1979), the Supreme Court held that "the officers stopped defendant only because he was present in an area which had recently been the scene of reported crimes, without any articulable facts suggesting that he had been involved in any criminal activity." The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Brown* by arguing that the H-3 freeway was not merely a high-crime area but a restricted construction site. However, this distinction does not change the fundamental analytical framework. The security guard provided no description of a vehicle or suspect—he merely reported hearing gunshots from a general area. The mere fact that a vehicle was subsequently found in that area does not establish reasonable suspicion that its occupants were involved in criminal activity.

The prosecution argues that the vehicle's presence on a "barricaded, uncompleted roadway at 10:30 p.m." provided independent grounds for suspicion. This argument is unpersuasive for several reasons. First, the facts indicate that the vehicle was "travelling at a reasonable speed on the uncompleted freeway approaching with headlights on." This behavior is entirely consistent with lawful use of the roadway—however unauthorized—rather than flight from police or criminal activity. Second, the mere fact of unauthorized entry to a construction site, without more, does not justify a stop based on suspicion of firearms offenses. The officers had no information connecting the vehicle to the reported gunshots beyond geographic proximity.

The prosecution's reliance on *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), is misplaced. *Terry* requires "reasonable, articulable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot." Here, the officers had no description of a suspect, no description of a vehicle, and no information suggesting that the particular vehicle they stopped was involved in the shooting. The stop was therefore unlawful.

---

## II. THE PAT-DOWN WAS UNCONSTITUTIONAL UNDER *YBARRA*

The prosecution invokes *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049–50 (1983), to justify the pat-down of all four occupants. However, *Long* does not authorize a blanket frisk of all occupants of a vehicle merely because the vehicle was stopped in connection with a reported crime.

In *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85, 92–93 (1979), the Supreme Court held that "a 'patdown' search, or 'frisk,' is a limited search designed to discover instruments of assault which might be used against the policeman." The Court emphasized that "the police must have individualized suspicion to conduct a patdown of a particular person." The prosecution argues that *Ybarra* is distinguishable because it involved a public bar where the connection between patrons and criminal activity was "tenuous." However, the prosecution overstates the connection in this case. The mere fact that a vehicle was found in the vicinity of reported gunshots does not create individualized suspicion that each occupant was armed and dangerous.

The prosecution's argument that the officers "reasonably believed the occupants might be armed and dangerous" because "the vehicle was located at the precise scene of a shooting involving multiple gunshots" proves too much. This reasoning would justify pat-downs of every person found anywhere near any reported crime, which directly contradicts the individualized suspicion requirement of *Ybarra*. There were no facts suggesting that any of the four occupants were involved in the shooting or possessed weapons. The pat-down was therefore unconstitutional.

---

## III. THE FLASHLIGHT SCAN EXCEEDED PERMISSIBLE PLAIN VIEW OBSERVATION

The prosecution correctly notes that *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983), held that the use of a flashlight to illuminate a vehicle's interior does not, by itself, constitute a search. However, the prosecution fails to acknowledge the critical distinction between observing what is in plain view from a lawful vantage point and the subsequent physical intrusion that occurred here.

While the initial observation of the rifle casing from outside the vehicle may have been permissible, the officers did not stop there. The facts indicate that the officers "scanned the interior of the car with a flashlight and spotted a .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor. They also saw a black case on the rear floor partially concealed by the front seat. Upon pushing the front seat forward, the officers saw a ukulele case, unlatched although the cover was down."

The act of "pushing the front seat forward" to obtain a better view constitutes a physical intrusion into the vehicle. In *Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321, 324–25 (1987), the Supreme Court held that moving objects to better view them constitutes a search requiring probable cause. The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Hicks* by arguing that it involved "moving objects to discover evidence, not merely using light to see what was already in plain sight." However, the officers here did not merely use light—they physically manipulated the vehicle's interior by pushing the seat forward. This physical intrusion transformed the observation into a search.

---

## IV. PROBABLE CAUSE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SEARCH THE UKULELE CASE

The prosecution argues that the discovery of the rifle casing established probable cause to search the vehicle and its contents under the automobile exception. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, a single rifle casing, without more, does not establish probable cause to believe that a firearm is located in the vehicle. The casing could have been deposited at any time by any person. The prosecution asks this Court to infer, from the mere presence of a casing, that the vehicle currently contains a rifle used in the recent shooting. This inference is speculative and does not meet the "fair probability" standard articulated in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983).

Second, even if probable cause existed to search the vehicle, it did not extend to the ukulele case. The prosecution relies on *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 823 (1982), for the proposition that probable cause justifies searching any container that could hold the contraband. However, *Ross* does not stand for the proposition that any container found in a vehicle may be searched. Probable cause must still exist to believe that the particular container holds the evidence. Here, there was no reason to believe that the ukulele case contained the rifle used in the shooting. A ukulele case is designed to hold a musical instrument, not a rifle. The fact that a rifle was ultimately found inside does not retroactively establish that probable cause existed to search that particular container.

Third, the prosecution's reliance on *Florida v. Jardines*, 569 U.S. 1, 11 (2013), is misplaced, but the reasoning of *Jardines* is instructive. In *Jardines*, the Court emphasized that the curtilage of a home receives the highest Fourth Amendment protection because it is an area where reasonable expectations of privacy are greatest. While a vehicle generally carries a reduced expectation of privacy compared to a home, a closed container within a vehicle—such as a ukulele case—carries a heightened expectation of privacy. The interior of a closed case is not part of the vehicle's passenger compartment where items are typically exposed to view. The search of the ukulele case was therefore unconstitutional.

This matter comes before the Court on the State of Hawaii’s appeal from the circuit court’s order granting Defendant David A. Haili’s motion to suppress evidence. **ANALYSIS**

**I. The Initial Stop of the Vehicle Was Supported by Reasonable Suspicion**

The Defense argues that the stop was unlawful because the security guard’s report lacked a description of a vehicle or suspect, and the mere presence of a car on an unfinished freeway is insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion, relying on *Brown v. Texas*, 443 U.S. 47 (1979). The Defense contends that the vehicle was travelling at a reasonable speed with headlights on, behavior consistent with lawful use rather than criminal activity.

While *Brown v. Texas* holds that mere presence in a high-crime area does not justify a stop, the facts here distinguish this case. The vehicle was not merely present in a general area; it was observed on the "uncompleted portion of the H-3 freeway," the specific location identified by the security guard as the source of contemporaneous gunfire. The area was barricaded by a chain-link fence, indicating restricted access. The presence of a vehicle on a barricaded, uncompleted freeway at 10:30 p.m., immediately following a report of gunshots from that exact location, provides reasonable suspicion of unauthorized entry and involvement in the reported criminal activity. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). The officers had specific, articulable facts connecting the vehicle to the scene of the crime, satisfying the requirements of *Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 653 (1979). Therefore, the stop was lawful.

**II. The Pat-Down of the Occupants Was Justified**

The Defense contends that the pat-down violated *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1979), arguing that the officers lacked individualized suspicion that each occupant was armed and dangerous. The Defense asserts that the mere presence of the vehicle near the scene of a shooting does not justify a blanket frisk of all occupants.

However, *Ybarra* is factually distinct; it involved a raid on a public establishment where the connection between the patrons and the criminal activity was tenuous. Here, the officers had stopped the only vehicle at the scene of a reported shooting involving multiple gunshots. Given the specific report of gunfire and the vehicle's presence at the source of those shots, it was reasonable for the officers to believe the occupants might be armed and dangerous. *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049–50 (1983). The pat-down was a valid protective measure under *Terry* and *Long*, and the officers were not required to wait for specific confirmation that each individual was armed before ensuring their safety.

**III. The Search of the Vehicle Was Justified by Probable Cause**

The Defense argues that the officers exceeded permissible plain view observation when they "pushed the front seat forward" to view the ukulele case, citing *Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321 (1987). The Defense correctly notes that *Hicks* held that moving objects to view otherwise hidden information constitutes a search. However, the Defense overlooks the sequence of events.

The officers first observed a .22-caliber rifle casing on the front floor of the vehicle using a flashlight. As established in *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730, 740 (1983), the use of a flashlight to illuminate the interior of a vehicle from a lawful vantage point does not constitute a search. The discovery of the rifle casing provided the officers with probable cause to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime—specifically, the firearm used in the reported shooting. *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 153 (1925). Once probable cause attached, the officers were authorized to search the vehicle under the automobile exception. *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 823 (1982). Consequently, the act of pushing the seat forward was a lawful part of the vehicle search justified by the probable cause derived from the plain view observation of the casing.

**IV. The Search of the Ukulele Case Was Justified**

The Defense argues that a single rifle casing was insufficient to establish probable cause to search the closed ukulele case, citing *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), and that a ukulele case, designed for a musical instrument, carries a heightened expectation of privacy similar to the curtilage issues in *Florida v. Jardines*, 569 U.S. 1 (2013).

The Court disagrees. Under the totality of the circumstances test in *Gates*, the report of gunshots at the scene, the vehicle's presence at that specific location, and the discovery of the rifle casing inside the vehicle established a "fair probability" that the vehicle contained the weapon. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). Furthermore, under *Ross*, if probable cause justifies the search of a vehicle, it justifies the search of any container within that vehicle that could conceal the object of the search. *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 823 (1982). A ukulele case is a container capable of holding a rifle. The Defense's reliance on *Jardines* is misplaced; that case concerned the sanctity of the home and its curtilage, not a container within a mobile vehicle on a public roadway. The search of the ukulele case was lawful.

**CONCLUSION**

The circuit court erred in granting the motion to suppress. The officers acted with reasonable suspicion in stopping the vehicle, were justified in ensuring their safety through pat-downs, and lawfully observed evidence in plain view. The subsequent discovery of the rifle casing established probable cause, justifying the full search of the vehicle and its contents, including the manipulation of the seat and the search of the ukulele case.

The Fourth Amendment requires "specific and articulable facts" beyond mere presence at a crime scene to justify a Terry stop. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968).

The security guard reported hearing gunshots emanating from the unfinished H-3 freeway. This is precisely the type of arbitrary enforcement the Fourth Amendment was designed to prevent. The defense's position is supported by *Brown v. ## II. THE PAT-DOWN VIOLATED THE FOURTH AMENDMENT

The prosecution argues that *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1979), is distinguishable because the occupants here had a stronger connection to the crime scene than patrons in a bar. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the connection the prosecution asserts is precisely the same type of circumstantial link that *Ybarra* rejected. Here, by contrast, the officers had no information that any occupant was involved in the shooting or possessed weapons. The vehicle was traveling at a reasonable speed. No one fled. ## III. THE SEARCH OF THE VEHICLE EXCEEDED PERMISSIBLE PLAIN VIEW DOCTRINE

The prosecution contends that the use of a flashlight to illuminate the vehicle interior was permissible under *Texas v. Brown*, 460 U.S. 730 (1983). The defense does not dispute that using a flashlight to observe what is in plain view is permissible. However, the prosecution's argument conflates two distinct actions: (1) the initial flashlight observation, and (2) the officer's affirmative act of pushing the front seat forward to view the ukulele case.

Even accepting that the initial observation of the rifle casing was lawful, the act of pushing the front seat forward crosses the line into a search. In *Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321 (1987), the Supreme Court held that moving objects to view what is hidden constitutes a search requiring probable cause. Here, the officers could not see the ukulele case from their vantage point until they physically manipulated the vehicle by pushing the front seat forward. ## IV. THE SEARCH OF THE UKULELE CASE LACKED PROBABLE CAUSE

The prosecution invokes *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983), and its totality-of-the-circumstances test to argue that probable cause existed to search the ukulele case. This argument fails.

The *Gates* test requires a "fair probability" that evidence of a crime will be found in a particular location. Here, the prosecution cannot establish this standard. The prosecution's argument rests on the assumption that because some evidence of a crime was visible, all containers in the vehicle must be searched. This is precisely the type of "generalized suspicion" that the Fourth Amendment prohibits.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798 (1982), is also misplaced. In *Ross*, the Court held that if probable cause justifies a search of a vehicle, it justifies a search of containers within that vehicle. However, *Ross* explicitly required that the probable cause be "particularized" to the container being searched. Here, there was no particularized probable cause connecting the ukulele case to evidence of a crime. The prosecution has not explained why a ukulele case—which has no connection to firearms—would contain the weapon used in the shooting.

The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Florida v. Jardines*, 569 U.S. 1 (2013), by noting it concerned the curtilage of a home. The discovery of the rifle casing provided the officers with probable cause to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime—specifically, the firearm used in the reported shooting. *Carroll v. Jardines*, 569 U.S. 1 (2013).

The Court disagrees. Under the totality of the circumstances test in *Gates*, the report of gunshots at the scene, the vehicle's presence at that specific location, and the discovery of the rifle casing inside the vehicle established a "fair probability" that the vehicle contained the weapon. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). Furthermore, under *Ross*, if probable cause justifies the search of a vehicle, it justifies the search of any container within that vehicle that could conceal the object of the search. *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 823 (1982). A ukulele case is a container capable of holding a rifle. The Defense's reliance on *Jardines* is misplaced; that case concerned the sanctity of the home and its curtilage, not a container within a mobile vehicle on a public roadway. The search of the ukulele case was lawful.

**CONCLUSION**

The circuit court erred in granting the motion to suppress. The officers acted with reasonable suspicion in stopping the vehicle, were justified in ensuring their safety through pat-downs, and lawfully observed evidence in plain view. The subsequent discovery of the rifle casing established probable cause, justifying the full search of the vehicle and its contents, including the manipulation of the seat and the search of the ukulele case.

**ORDER**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the circuit court’s order granting the motion to suppress is **REVERSED**.
