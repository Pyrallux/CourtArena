FACTS:
Defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic (""Argentina"") and YPF S.A. (""YPF"") (together, ""defendants"") appeal an order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New *199York (Preska, J .), denying defendants' motions to dismiss under (1) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on grounds of foreign sovereign immunity and (2) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) pursuant to the act of state doctrine. We affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

BACKGROUND
Unless otherwise noted, the facts herein are undisputed. They are drawn from the complaint and the documents submitted by the parties in reference to defendants' motions to dismiss.

I. YPF Becomes a Publicly Traded Company

YPF is a petroleum company that was wholly owned and operated by the Argentine government until 1993. That year, in accordance with broader efforts to reform its economy, Argentina decided to privatize the petrol firm through an initial public offering (""IPO"") of nearly 100% of YPF's voting stock (the ""shares"").1 Argentina and YPF took a number of steps to entice investors to participate in the IPO and thereby ensure its success, two of which are particularly relevant to this case. First, they arranged for YPF to offer shares in the United States as American Depository Receipts (""ADRs"") listed on the New York Stock Exchange (""NYSE""). Second, they amended YPF's bylaws-that is, the contract governing the relationship among YPF, Argentina (in its capacity as a shareholder), and other YPF shareholders. In particular, the bylaws were amended to incorporate protections for investors from (1) hostile takeovers and (2) attempts by Argentina to renationalize the company. These takeover protections form the basis of this breach of contract dispute, and so we describe them in some detail.

Section 7(d) of the amended bylaws prohibits (with certain exceptions inapplicable here) the direct or indirect acquisition of YPF shares if the acquisition results in the acquirer controlling 15% or more of the shares, unless the acquirer makes a tender offer for all of the outstanding shares in accordance with certain procedures and at a price determined by a formula in the bylaws. Among the prescribed procedures, section 7(f) requires that any such tender offer comply with the rules and regulations imposed by the governments and stock exchanges where YPF's shares are listed. Because YPF's securities were to be listed on the NYSE, those conducting tender offers in accordance with these shareholder protection measures would be compelled by section 7(f) to comply with NYSE and Securities and Exchange Commission (""SEC"") rules and regulations. Section 7(f)(iv) further obligates the acquirer to publish notice of its tender offer ""in the business section of the major newspapers ... in the City of New York, U.S.A. and any other city where the shares [of YPF]
*200shall be listed."" App. 340. Perhaps most importantly for purposes of this appeal, section 28(A) of the bylaws extends the tender offer requirement of sections 7(e) and 7(f) to:
all acquisitions made by the [Government of Argentina], whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument, of shares or securities of [YPF], 1) if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the [Government] becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of [YPF], which, in addition to the prior holdings thereof of any class of shares, represent, in the aggregate, at least 49% of the capital stock [of YPF]; or 2) if the [Government] acquires at least 8% of class D outstanding shares of stock, while withholding class A shares of stock amounting at least to 5% of the capital stock.
App. 432.

The penalties for breaching these provisions are drastic. Section 7(h) provides that ""[s]hares of stock and securities acquired in breach of [the tender offer requirements] shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends."" App. 342. And section 28(C) extends such treatment to shares acquired by Argentina, unless its breach is accidental. In that case, ""[t]he penalties provided for in subsection (h) of Section 7 shall be limited ... to the loss of the right to vote."" App. 355. At bottom, these shareholder protection measures appear to promise investors a compensated exit from their ownership position in the firm if Argentina were to decide to renationalize YPF.

Argentina and YPF touted these protections in the prospectus filed with the SEC in connection with the IPO. That document stated that ""[u]nder [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws."" App. 23. The prospectus further stated that ""any Control Acquisition carried out by the Argentine Government other than in accordance with th[at] procedure ... will result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired."" Id. (alteration in original).

By all accounts, Argentina's marketing efforts worked. YPF launched a successful IPO on June 29, 1993. Through the sale of YPF securities, Argentina raised billions of dollars in investment capital with the largest share (more than $1.1 billion in total) coming from the sale of ADRs in the United States on the NYSE. A firm called Repsol S.A. (""Repsol"") emerged from the IPO as YPF's majority shareholder. Even after the IPO, however, Argentina continued to participate in YPF's corporate governance as a commercial actor. It remained a holder of YPF's Class A shares, entitling it to elect at least one member of the firm's board of directors. Argentina also retained a veto right over certain third-party acquisitions of YPF's capital stock. After the IPO, YPF's shares, via the ADRs, were traded publicly on the NYSE and other exchanges.

Plaintiffs-appellees Petersen EnergÃ­a Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen EnergÃ­a, S.A.U. (together, ""Petersen"") entered the picture in 2008. Between 2008 and 2011, Petersen conducted a series of acquisitions and came to own approximately 25% of YPF's shares, held in the form of ADRs issued by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. All of Petersen's acquisitions were made in accordance with YPF's bylaws, including the tender offer provisions in section 7. The bulk of Petersen's shares were purchased from Repsol and their purchase was financed by Repsol and various financial institutions, which maintained *201a security interest in the stock as collateral. As part of a shareholder agreement with Petersen, Repsol agreed to cause YPF to make biannual distributions of 90% of its profits to shareholders via dividends in accordance with section 25 of the bylaws. Petersen often used these dividends to make payments on the loans it used to finance the purchase of YPF stock.

All of that changed in 2012. Early that year, members of the Argentine government began publicly criticizing Repsol's and Petersen's management of YPF and started discussing the prospect of renationalizing the company. The value of YPF's ADRs plummeted in response to this news. To put what happened next in the appropriate context, it helps to understand a little about the mechanics of Argentine expropriation law.

II. Argentine Expropriation Law

Expropriation is the ""governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights."" Expropriation , BLACK'S LAW DICTIONARY (10th ed. 2014). A ""classic example"" is the government's condemnation of a parcel of land to make way for some public good, like a road. Murr v. Wisconsin , --- U.S. ----, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939, 198 L.Ed.2d 497 (2017). The enactment of land use regulations may also, in some cases, constitute an expropriation. See id. But these land-based examples understate the breadth of a sovereign's power of expropriation, which can be vast. That is so because all types of property can be expropriated, whether tangible or intangible. Personal property, airspace rights, contract rights, even the shares of a corporation-at least in theory, a sovereign can expropriate them all. See Penn Cent. Transp. Co. v. City of New York , 438 U.S. 104, 128, 98 S.Ct. 2646, 57 L.Ed.2d 631 (1978) (discussing a ""taking"" of airspace rights); accord August Reinisch, Expropriation , in The Oxford Handbook of International Investment Law 407, 410 (Peter Muchlinski et al. eds., 2008) (""It is generally asserted that expropriation may affect not only tangible property but also a broad range of intangible assets of economic value to an investor. Property that may be expropriated by states thus comprises immaterial rights and interests, including in particular contractual rights.""). In reality, however, whether a government may expropriate property, what property is subject to expropriation, and how much the government must compensate the individual it expropriated the property from (if at all) are largely questions of law of the expropriating nation. Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts , 28 Fordham. L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) (""States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best; ... they are free to operate their municipal system of property according to their own national genius ....""); compare Org. for Econ. Co-operation & Dev., ""Indirect Expropriation"" and the ""Right to Regulate"" in International Investment Law , in International Investment Law: A Changing Landscape 43, 43-72 (2005) (discussing limits imposed on expropriations by customary international law). In this case, we look to Argentine law. See Garb v. Republic of Poland , 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006).

Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution sets the conditions under which property may be expropriated by the Argentine government. To effectuate an expropriation consistent with Article 17, two conditions must be met: (1) the Argentine Congress must declare a public use for the property to be expropriated and (2) the owner of the property must be compensated. The Argentine government has passed laws to clarify what property is subject to *202expropriation and to specify the procedures that must be followed to meet the conditions for expropriation.

One such law is Law 21,499, known as the ""General Expropriation Law."" App. 57. It empowers, among other entities, the Argentine Federal Government to act as an expropriator. As for the declaration of public use required by Article 17 of the National Constitution, section 5 of the General Expropriation Law clarifies that the Argentine Congress ""shall particularly refer to specific property"" to be expropriated in its declaration and section 1 provides that ""[p]ublic use, which is required as legal grounds for expropriation, comprises all cases where public welfare may be involved."" App. 185-86. The law further declares that ""[a]ll such property as may be convenient or necessary to satisfy [that] 'public use' purpose, whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not, may be subject to expropriation ...."" App. 186. As for compensation for that property, section 10 of the General Expropriation Law provides that the owner shall receive ""the objective value of the property plus any direct and immediate damages resulting from expropriation,"" such amounts to be fixed by agreement of the owner and expropriator or pursuant to a court proceeding. App. 187. And, presumably to prevent the owner's malfeasance while compensation is being fixed, section 16 of the law proclaims that ""[n]o contract executed by the owner after the effective date of the law declaring the expropriation of the property and which may imply the creation of any right or interest in the property shall be good as against the expropriator."" App. 187.
Accordingly, with this legal backdrop in mind, we return to how Argentina regained control over YPF's affairs in the spring of 2012.

III. Argentina Regains Control of YPF

On April 16, 2012, pursuant to the General Expropriation Law, Argentina proposed legislation that would expropriate directly from Repsol 51% of the voting stock of YPF. On the same day, the Argentine National Executive Office decreed that it was empowering an ""Intervenor"" to seize immediate control of YPF's operations and to operate the company as a going concern while the Argentine Congress considered the expropriation legislation. Action was swift. Indeed, before some of these measures were even announced publicly, the Intervenor seized control of YPF's facilities, replaced top management with government officials, and escorted YPF's then-CEO off the premises. The Intervenor also cancelled regularly-scheduled meetings of YPF's board of directors and refused to make expected dividend payments.

Argentine officials were also quick to declare that, despite having acquired control of the company, Argentina and YPF had no intention of complying with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. For example, on April 17, 2012, in a speech before the Argentine Senate, the country's Deputy Economy Minister described as ""fools ... those who think that the State has to be stupid and buy everyone according to YPF's own law, respecting its by-law."" App. 29 n.1. He also dismissed the tender offer requirements as ""unfair"" and a ""bear trap."" Id .

On May 3, 2012, the proposed expropriation legislation was enacted as Law 26,741 with an effective date of May 7, 2012 (the ""YPF Expropriation Law""). In accordance with Article 17 of the National Constitution, the YPF Expropriation Law pronounced Argentina's national public interest in achieving ""self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon[ ] supply,"" App. 165, by, inter *203alia , integrating ""public and private ... capital into strategic alliances aimed at the exploration and exploitation of conventional and unconventional hydrocarbons,"" App. 166.
The law further provided that:
to ensure the fulfillment of the objectives of this law, the fifty-one percent (51%) equity interest in YPF Sociedad AnÃ³nima represented by the same percentage of Class D shares of the said Company, held by Repsol YPF S.A., its controlled or controlling entities, directly or indirectly, is hereby declared to be of public use and subject to expropriation.
App. 167. The YPF Expropriation Law also extended the Intervenor's control over the firm's operations and granted the Argentine executive branch the right to ""exercise all the political rights over all the shares subject to expropriation"" until the expropriation, including compensation of Repsol, was finalized. App. 167.

Argentina did indeed exercise the rights of Repsol's shares, using them to cancel YPF's previously-scheduled dividend payment and board meeting in April 2012, and voted the shares at a shareholder meeting in June 2012, in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Unable to meet its loan obligations without the dividend payment, Petersen entered insolvency proceedings in July 2012 and its lenders foreclosed on the YPF ADRs that Petersen had pledged as collateral. Repsol was eventually compensated for its expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion.

**Opening Argument for the Plaintiff/Prosecution**  

Your Honor, this case is not about sovereign expropriation—it is about a foreign sovereign’s deliberate breach of a binding commercial contract it entered into to attract foreign investment. Argentina promised investors that if it sought to reacquire control of YPF, it would honor its contractual obligations by making a tender offer to all shareholders. When it expropriated Repsol’s shares in 2012, Argentina publicly declared it would ignore those obligations. It then exercised voting rights over those shares while denying dividends to investors, directly harming U.S. citizens and causing cascading financial consequences in New York City. The district court correctly denied dismissal because the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) does not shield Argentina for this commercial misconduct, and the act of state doctrine cannot insulate claims based on private contractual disputes.  

### **I. Argentina’s Breach of Contract is Commercial in Nature and Triggered the FSIA’s Commercial Activity Exception**  
Argentina’s actions in this case were not sovereign acts but commercial conduct indistinguishable from what a private entity could perform. When Argentina privatized YPF in 1993, it did not act as a regulator of Argentina’s energy market—it acted as a seller of securities in a global capital market. It issued American Depository Receipts (ADRs) on the New York Stock Exchange, filed a prospectus with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, and explicitly incorporated contractual protections into YPF’s bylaws to reassure investors. As the district court recognized, these bylaws were part of a commercial transaction designed to attract capital from U.S. investors, including Petersen, who purchased YPF shares through NYSE-listed ADRs.  

The FSIA’s commercial activity exception applies when a foreign state’s conduct “is the kind of activity in which a private person could engage.” *First Nat’l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 624 (1983). Here, Argentina issued bonds, listed securities on U.S. exchanges, and entered into contractual terms with investors—actions any private corporation could perform. Its breach of those terms, including refusing to make a tender offer and violating bylaw provisions that strip voting rights for unauthorized acquisitions, is purely commercial. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992) (holding that a foreign state’s default on commercial bonds issued to international investors constitutes commercial activity under the FSIA).  

Crucially, Argentina’s breach caused a “direct effect in the United States,” satisfying the FSIA’s jurisdictional threshold. *28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2)*. Argentina’s refusal to make a tender offer led to the cancellation of dividend payments owed to U.S.-based ADR holders, including Petersen. It also precipitated Petersen’s default on loans secured by NYSE-listed ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City—directly impacting U.S. financial markets and U.S. citizens. In *Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina*, 965 F.2d 699, 713 (9th Cir. 1992), the court held that “direct effects” exist when a foreign state’s actions cause “substantial harm to U.S. investors through instruments traded on U.S. exchanges.” This case fits squarely within that standard: the canceled dividends and foreclosure of NYSE-listed collateral occurred in New York, harming U.S. residents.  

### **II. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Bar This Claim Because It Concerns Private Contractual Rights, Not Sovereign Acts**  
The act of state doctrine is a rule of comity, not a jurisdictional bar, and it does not apply when a claim arises from private commercial conduct. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964) (explaining that the doctrine “does not apply to claims based on private contracts”). Here, Petersen’s claim is not an attack on Argentina’s sovereign power to expropriate property under Argentine law. Instead, it is a straightforward breach of contract claim seeking enforcement of contractual obligations Argentina voluntarily adopted to attract investment.  

Argentina’s public statements—such as its Deputy Economy Minister calling tender offer requirements “foolish” and a “bear trap”—confirm this was a deliberate violation of commercial terms, not a sovereign policy decision. When a foreign state enters into a commercial agreement with private parties, its breach of that agreement is not shielded by the act of state doctrine. *Kellogg Brown & Root Servs., Inc. v. Republic of Iraq*, 700 F.3d 527, 536 (D.C. Cir. 2012) (holding that the act of state doctrine “does not apply to claims based on private commercial contracts” because such claims “do not require questioning the validity of a foreign sovereign’s official acts”). The bylaws were not a sovereign decree; they were contractual terms embedded in a commercial transaction with U.S. investors. To apply the act of state doctrine here would immunize Argentina from liability for breaking promises made to lure capital to its markets—a result this Court has repeatedly rejected.  

### **Conclusion**  
This is not a case about Argentina’s sovereign right to expropriate—it is about Argentina’s breach of a commercial contract it created to secure U.S. investment. Its actions caused direct harm in New York City, triggering the FSIA’s commercial activity exception. And its violation of private contractual terms falls outside the scope of the act of state doctrine. The district court’s denial of dismissal was correct, and this Court should affirm it.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE UNITED STATES COURT OF APPEALS FOR THE SECOND CIRCUIT

**Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic and YPF S.A. (together, "Defendants") submit this opening response in opposition to the Plaintiff's argument and in support of their appeal.**

---

## INTRODUCTION

Defendants respectfully submit that the district court erred in denying their motions to dismiss. The Plaintiff's attempt to recast Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation as a mere "breach of contract" fundamentally mischaracterizes the nature of the claims at issue. Argentina's exercise of its eminent domain power under Argentine law—whether characterized correctly or not—is a sovereign act that falls squarely within the protections of the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and the act of state doctrine. The contractual bylaw provisions upon which the Plaintiff relies cannot transform a sovereign expropriation into a commercial activity, nor can they create an exception to sovereign immunity where none exists. This Court should reverse the district court's denial of Defendants' motions to dismiss.

---

## I. ARGUMENT

### A. The FSIA Bars This Action Because Argentina's Conduct Constitutes Sovereign Expropriation, Not Commercial Activity

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act provides that foreign states are "presumptively immune" from the jurisdiction of United States courts unless one of the statutory exceptions applies. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992). The Plaintiff bears the burden of demonstrating that an exception applies. *Verhoeven v. Republic of Brazil*, 380 F.3d 163, 165-66 (5th Cir. 2004). The district court erred in finding that the commercial activity exception applies to this case.

#### 1. Argentina's Expropriation of YPF Is a Sovereign Act, Not Commercial Activity

The FSIA's commercial activity exception applies only when a foreign state's conduct is "the kind of activity by which a private party may engage in." *28 U.S.C. § 1603(d)*. The distinction between sovereign and commercial acts is fundamental to the FSIA. *See Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614 ("The theory of the FSIA is that a foreign state is not immune ... when it acts not as regulator of a market or participant in commercial transactions, but as a private player in the market.").

Expropriation is the quintessential sovereign act. *See Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964) ("[T]he act of state doctrine is applicable to ... expropriations."). When a sovereign takes property for public use pursuant to its constitutional and statutory authority, it exercises a power that no private party possesses. *See Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts*, 28 Fordham L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) ("States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best.").

The YPF Expropriation Law (Law 26,741) was enacted by the Argentine Congress pursuant to Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution, which expressly authorizes expropriation for public use upon payment of compensation. The law explicitly declared "public use" and "public interest" in achieving "self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon supply" through the expropriation of 51% of YPF's equity held by Repsol. This is the exercise of sovereign eminent domain power—not commercial activity.

The Plaintiff's argument that Argentina's conduct was "commercial" because it issued securities in 1993 ignores the fundamental distinction between Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation in 2012 and its earlier commercial participation in the securities market. The 1993 IPO was a separate transaction involving the sale of securities to raise capital. The 2012 expropriation was a fundamentally different act—the exercise of sovereign power to take private property for a public purpose. The FSIA does not permit plaintiffs to bootstrap a sovereign act onto a prior commercial transaction to create jurisdiction.

In *Alfred Dunhill of London, Inc. v. Republic of Cuba*, 425 U.S. 682, 695 (1976), the Supreme Court held that "it is the nature of the activity, not the status of the actor," that determines whether conduct is sovereign or commercial. The expropriation of corporate shares is inherently sovereign—it is an exercise of the government'seminent domain power that no private entity can perform. The fact that Argentina previously issued securities does not transform its subsequent sovereign taking into commercial conduct.

#### 2. The "Direct Effect" Analysis Does Not Override the Sovereign Act Distinction

The Plaintiff relies heavily on the "direct effect" prong of the commercial activity exception, arguing that the cancellation of dividends and Petersen loan default constituted sufficient nexus with the United States. This argument fails because the FSIA requires that the underlying conduct itself be commercial—the "direct effect" provision is merely a jurisdictional nexus requirement, not a substitute for the commercial activity requirement.

The Supreme Court in *Weltover* emphasized that the commercial activity exception requires both that the conduct be commercial and that it have a direct effect in the United States. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618. Where the underlying conduct is sovereign expropriation, no amount of "direct effect" in the United States can transform it into commercial activity.

Moreover, the Plaintiff's reliance on *Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina*, 965 F.2d 699 (9th Cir. 1992), is misplaced. In that case, the court found commercial activity where Argentina had allegedly tortured the plaintiffs in retaliation for their commercial business activities—not where Argentina had exercised its sovereign expropriation power. The instant case involves the opposite: Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation, not commercial conduct.

The Plaintiff cannot circumvent the FSIA's immunity by characterizing the consequences of sovereign action as "commercial." The expropriation itself is the conduct at issue, and expropriation is not commercial activity within the meaning of the FSIA.

#### 3. The Commercial Activity Exception Does Not Apply to Claims Arising from Sovereign Expropriation

Even assuming arguendo that Argentina engaged in some commercial activity in 1993, the Plaintiff's claims do not "arise out of" that commercial activity as required by the FSIA. The claims arise from Argentina's 2012 exercise of its sovereign expropriation power—a distinct and separate act.

The FSIA's commercial activity exception requires a direct causal connection between the commercial activity and the claims asserted. *See 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2)*. The Plaintiff's claims do not arise from the 1993 IPO or the issuance of ADRs; they arise from the 2012 expropriation, which was a sovereign act. The temporal and substantive gap between the commercial activity and the challenged conduct is too great to support jurisdiction.

---

### B. The Act of State Doctrine Bars adjudication of This Dispute

Independent of the FSIA, the act of state doctrine requires dismissal of the Plaintiff's claims. The act of state doctrine provides that "the acts of foreign sovereigns taken within their own jurisdiction shall not be questioned in the courts of the United States." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428. This doctrine applies with particular force to expropriations. *Id.*

#### 1. The Expropriation Is a Valid Act of State

The act of state doctrine applies to "the external and internal acts of a sovereign government." *Id.* at 421. Expropriation of private property for public use is the paradigmatic act of state. The Argentine Congress enacted Law 26,741 declaring the expropriation of 51% of YPF's shares to be for public use and in the public interest. This is a formal legislative act of a sovereign state—exactly the type of conduct the act of state doctrine protects.

The Plaintiff argues that the doctrine does not apply because the claim is based on "private contract." This argument misconstrues the nature of the claim. The Plaintiff is not seeking to enforce a contract against a private party; it is seeking to challenge the legality and validity of Argentina's sovereign expropriation. The Plaintiff's claim requires this Court to determine that Argentina's exercise of its eminent domain power was improper because it failed to comply with private contractual provisions (the bylaws). This is precisely the type of "questioning" of sovereign acts that the act of state doctrine prohibits.

In *Sabbatino*, the Court held that the doctrine applies where "the claim seeks to attach the act of the foreign state itself." *Id.* at 428. Here, the Plaintiff's entire claim is that Argentina's expropriation was improper because it did not comply with tender offer requirements in YPF's bylaws. This is a direct challenge to Argentina's sovereign act.

#### 2. The Act of State Doctrine Applies Regardless of Whether Compensation Was Paid

The Plaintiff suggests that the act of state doctrine might not apply because Argentina compensated Repsol for its expropriated shares. This argument lacks merit. The act of state doctrine does not turn on the adequacy or existence of compensation—it turns on whether the underlying act is a sovereign act within the foreign state's jurisdiction. *See First Nat'l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 623 (1983) (holding that the act of state doctrine applies to "public acts" of foreign sovereigns).

Moreover, the Plaintiff's argument conflates the separate interests of Petersen and Repsol. Petersen did not own the shares that were expropriated from Repsol. Petersen's claim is that it was harmed by Argentina's failure to make a tender offer to minority shareholders—not that it was personally expropriated. The adequacy of compensation to Repsol is irrelevant to whether Argentina's sovereign act may be questioned.

#### 3. The Act of State Doctrine Reflects Comity and Separation of Powers Concerns

The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that the act of state doctrine serves important principles of international comity and separation of powers. *See Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 423-24. Courts should not sit in judgment of the validity of foreign sovereign acts, particularly expropriations, absent clear Congressional authorization. *See id.* at 428.

Here, there is no Congressional authorization for this Court to adjudicate the validity of Argentina's expropriation. To the contrary, Congress enacted the FSIA to provide a "comprehensive framework" for determining when foreign states are subject to suit in United States courts. *See H.R. Rep. No. 94-1487, at 6 (1976), reprinted in 1976 U.S.C.C.A.N. 6604, 6605.** The FSIA's express determination that sovereign expropriation is immune from suit reflects Congress's judgment that such claims should not proceed in American courts.

The Plaintiff urges this Court to second-guess Argentina's determination that the expropriation served the public interest—a policy judgment committed to the Argentine Congress under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution. This Court lacks authority to make such judgments, and the act of state doctrine properly bars such adjudication.

---

### C. The Bylaw Provisions Cannot Create an Exception to Sovereign Immunity

The Plaintiff attempts to circumvent sovereign immunity by arguing that Argentina "contracted away" its sovereign immunity through the bylaws. This argument fails for several reasons.

#### 1. Foreign Sovereigns Cannot Waive Immunity Through Private Contracts

The FSIA provides that a foreign state may waive its immunity only through a "written waiver" that meets the statutory requirements. *See 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(1)*. A foreign state's mere entry into a private commercial transaction does not constitute a waiver of sovereign immunity. *See Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618-19 (holding that Argentina did not waive immunity by issuing bonds because the bonds did not contain a clear waiver).

The bylaws were adopted in 1993 as part of YPF's corporate governance structure—not as a bilateral contract between Argentina and future investors. Even assuming the bylaws could be construed as a contract, they contain no express waiver of Argentina's sovereign immunity. The Plaintiff cannot manufacture a waiver where none exists.

#### 2. The Bylaws Do Not Bind Argentina's Sovereign Powers

Even if the bylaws constituted a binding contract, they cannot bind Argentina's exercise of its sovereign eminent domain power. The principle of sovereign immunity reflects the fundamental inability of private parties to constrain sovereign governmental authority through private agreements. *See nformation to satisfy public use purpose, whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not, may be subject to expropriation."* This demonstrates that the Argentine Congress specifically determined that corporate shares are subject to expropriation—a determination that supersedes any private contractual arrangements.

A foreign sovereign cannot contract away its sovereign powers, and no private bylaw can limit a sovereign's eminent domain authority. *See United States v. 50 Acres of Land*, 351 U.S. 70, 75 (1956) (holding that the government's power of eminent domain cannot be limited by private contracts). The bylaws were not intended to—and could not—bind Argentina's future exercise of its sovereign expropriation power.

### D. The District Court's Analysis Was Flawed

The district court erred in several respects in its analysis of the FSIA and act of state issues.

#### 1. The District Court Incorrectly Applied the Commercial Activity Exception

The district court focused on the "commercial" nature of the 1993 IPO and the NYSE listing of ADRs, but this analysis improperly conflates distinct transactions. The Plaintiff's claims arise from the 2012 expropriation, not the 1993 IPO. The commercial activity exception requires that the specific conduct giving rise to the claim be commercial. *See 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2)*. The expropriation of corporate shares is not commercial activity.

The district court also erred in finding that the "direct effect" in the United States was sufficient to establish jurisdiction. As discussed above, the "direct effect" provision requires both commercial activity and a direct effect—the absence of either defeats jurisdiction.

#### 2. The District Court Incorrectly Applied the Act of State Doctrine

The district court erroneously concluded that the act of state doctrine did not apply because the Plaintiff's claim was "based on private contract." This analysis misapprehends the nature of the claim. The Plaintiff is not seeking to enforce a contract between private parties; it is seeking to challenge Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. The fact that the Plaintiff relies on bylaw provisions to characterize the expropriation as "wrongful" does not transform the claim into a private contract dispute.

The act of state doctrine applies to "the underlying sovereign act"—here, the expropriation of YPF shares. *See Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428. The Plaintiff's attempt to parse the "commercial" aspects of the transaction from the "sovereign" aspects fails because the central act at issue—the taking of property for public use—is quintessentially sovereign.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the district court's order denying the defendants' motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA). The Court further dismisses the defendants' appeal regarding the act of state doctrine.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The District Court Properly Exercised Jurisdiction Under the FSIA**

The defendants contend that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction because Argentina's conduct constituted a sovereign act of expropriation rather than commercial activity. This argument is unavailing.

The FSIA establishes the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the United States. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992). Under the "commercial activity" exception, a foreign state is not immune from suit in any case "based upon ... an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere ... and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The determination of whether an act is "commercial" turns on the nature of the act, not its motive. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. An act is commercial if it is "the kind of activity by which a private party may engage in." 28 U.S.C. § 1603(d). The defendants argue that the expropriation of YPF shares pursuant to Law 26,741 is a sovereign act of eminent domain that no private party could perform. While the power of eminent domain is uniquely sovereign, the specific conduct upon which the plaintiff's claims are based is not the validity of the expropriation decree itself, but rather the alleged breach of the contractual obligations contained in YPF's bylaws.

The plaintiff alleges that Argentina, having acquired control of YPF, refused to make the required tender offer to all shareholders and exercised voting rights in contravention of sections 7 and 28 of the bylaws. These bylaws were part of a commercial transaction—the 1993 IPO—designed to attract investment. Argentina's participation in this market, including the issuance of ADRs and the agreement to be bound by corporate governance rules, was commercial in nature. *First Nat’l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 624 (1983). A private corporation could certainly agree to bylaws requiring a tender offer upon a change of control and could be sued for breaching those provisions. Therefore, the refusal to honor these contractual obligations constitutes commercial activity.

Furthermore, the plaintiff has established the requisite "direct effect in the United States." The cancellation of dividend payments directly impacted U.S. investors holding ADRs, and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs occurred in New York. *Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina*, 965 F.2d 699, 713 (9th Cir. 1992). Because the claims are based upon commercial conduct that caused a direct effect in the United States, the commercial activity exception applies, and the district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Bar the Plaintiff's Claims**

The defendants also argue that the act of state doctrine requires dismissal of this case. The act of state doctrine is a principle of comity that precludes courts from inquiring into the validity of public acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964). However, the doctrine "does not apply to claims based on private contracts." *Kellogg Brown & Root Servs., Inc. v. Republic of Iraq*, 700 F.3d 527, 536 (D.C. Cir. 2012).

The plaintiff's claims arise from the breach of YPF's bylaws, which are contractual provisions governing the relationship between the company and its shareholders. The plaintiff does not seek to invalidate Argentina's expropriation law or challenge Argentina's sovereign authority to nationalize assets for public use. Rather, the plaintiff seeks to enforce the specific contractual terms that Argentina voluntarily adopted to facilitate the privatization of YPF. Enforcing these terms does not require the Court to question the validity of the expropriation itself; it merely requires determining whether the manner in which the acquisition was effected complied with the pre-existing contractual obligations.

The defendants' assertion that the bylaws cannot bind Argentina's sovereign power is misplaced. While a sovereign cannot contract away its power to expropriate, it can agree to the commercial terms governing the acquisition of shares, including the obligation to pay for them. The act of state doctrine is not a shield for breaches of commercial contracts, even when those contracts involve a foreign sovereign. Consequently, the district court properly denied the motion to dismiss on this ground.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants' arguments regarding the application of the FSIA and the act of state doctrine are unpersuasive. The district court's order is affirmed in part, and the appeal is dismissed in part.

The plaintiff cites *Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina*, 965 F.2d 699 (9th Cir. 1992), but that case involved torture committed in retaliation for commercial disputes—a fundamentally different factual scenario. Here, the alleged "effects" flow directly from a sovereign expropriation, not from independent commercial conduct.

The foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs and the cancellation of dividends, while regrettable, were consequences of the sovereign act of expropriation—not independent commercial activities that caused the expropriation to occur. Argentina's exercise of the voting rights of the expropriated shares was an incident of its sovereign ownership, not a commercial transaction. The plaintiff cannot circumvent sovereign immunity by characterizing the downstream consequences of expropriation as "commercial activity."

### III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS ADJUDICATION OF THESE CLAIMS

#### A. The Claims Require Judicial Inquiry into the Validity of Argentina's Sovereign Acts

The act of state doctrine precludes courts from "inquiring into the validity of public acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory." *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964). The plaintiff argues that this doctrine does not apply because the claims are based on "private contracts." However, this argument ignores that enforcement of the bylaws would require this Court to determine whether Argentina's acquisition of YPF shares was proper—and that inquiry necessarily implicates the validity of the expropriation itself.

Section 28 of the bylaws purports to impose obligations on Argentina "whenever" Argentina acquires shares that would result in it owning or controlling at least 49% of YPF's capital stock. But Argentina acquired these shares through the YPF Expropriation Law, which declared the acquisition to be "for public use" and authorized the taking. To determine whether Argentina breached section 28 by failing to make a tender offer, this Court would be required to evaluate whether the expropriation was conducted in accordance with the bylaws—a determination that would necessarily involve examining the legality and propriety of Argentina's sovereign act. This is precisely what the act of state doctrine forbids. *See Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428 ("[T]he act of state doctrine reflects the 'unique and essential' role of judicial review of sovereign acts.").

#### B. The Bylaws Cannot Bind Argentina's Sovereign Power of Expropriation

The plaintiff argues that Argentina "voluntarily" adopted the bylaws and therefore cannot now claim sovereign immunity. This argument proves too much. While a sovereign may bind itself to certain commercial terms, it cannot contract away its inherent sovereign powers. Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution grants Argentina the power of expropriation; no corporate bylaws can limit or condition that constitutional authority. The act of state doctrine exists precisely to prevent foreign courts from siting in judgment over how a sovereign exercises its constitutional powers.

The plaintiff's argument that "a sovereign can agree to commercial terms governing the acquisition of shares" conflates two distinct concepts. Argentina can certainly agree to acquire shares through commercial negotiations—as it did in 1993 when it privatized YPF. But when Argentina subsequently exercises its sovereign power of eminent domain through legislation, that act is not subject to the conditions of prior private agreements. To hold otherwise would allow private contracts to constrain sovereign authority—a result incompatible with fundamental principles of international law.

#### C. Comity and Separation of Powers Require Application of the Doctrine

The act of state doctrine serves vital interests of comity and separation of powers. *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 423-24. By adjudicating the validity of Argentina's expropriation, this Court would effectively sit in judgment of Argentina's domestic legal regime—an intrusion upon Argentina's sovereignty that the doctrine exists to prevent. The plaintiff argues that enforcing the bylaws does not "second-guess" Argentina's policy decisions. But asking whether Argentina properly complied with section 28 of the bylaws necessarily requires evaluating whether Argentina's exercise of its eminent domain power was proper—a quintessential policy judgment reserved to the sovereign.

The plaintiff's reliance on *Kellogg Brown & Root Services, Inc. v. Republic of Iraq*, 700 F.3d 527 (D.C. Cir. 2012), is unavailing. In that case, the court found the act of state doctrine inapplicable where the underlying contract was a purely commercial construction agreement entered into voluntarily by Iraq, not legislation enacted under the sovereign's constitutional powers. Here, by contrast, Argentina acquired YPF shares through the YPF Expropriation Law—a sovereign act of general application, not a bilateral commercial contract.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court's order denying the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act and dismiss the claims as barred by the act of state doctrine. The plaintiff seeks to transform a sovereign act of expropriation into a mere breach of contract claim, but the FSIA and the act of state doctrine were designed to prevent exactly this type of end-run around sovereign immunity. Argentina's exercise of its constitutional power of eminent domain is precisely the type of sovereign conduct that remains immune from suit in U.S. courts.

**FINAL RULING**

For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the district court's order denying the defendants' motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA). The Court further dismisses the defendants' appeal regarding the act of state doctrine.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The District Court Properly Exercised Jurisdiction Under the FSIA**

The defendants contend that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction because the claims arise from Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation rather than commercial activity. We disagree.

The FSIA provides the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the United States. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992). Under the "commercial activity" exception, a foreign state is not immune from suit in any case "based upon ... an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere ... and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The determination of whether an act is "commercial" turns on the nature of the act, not its motive. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. An act is commercial if it is "the kind of activity by which a private party may engage in." 28 U.S.C. § 1603(d). The defendants argue that the expropriation of YPF shares pursuant to Law 26,741 is a sovereign act of eminent domain that no private party could perform, citing *West v. Deutsche Bank AG*, 887 F.3d 1084, 1092 (9th Cir. 2018). While the power of eminent domain is uniquely sovereign, the specific conduct upon which the plaintiff's claims are based is not the validity of the expropriation decree itself, but rather the alleged breach of the contractual obligations contained in YPF's bylaws.

The plaintiff alleges that Argentina, having acquired control of YPF, refused to make the required tender offer to all shareholders and exercised voting rights in contravention of sections 7 and 28 of the bylaws. These bylaws were part of a commercial transaction—the 1993 IPO—designed to attract investment. Argentina's participation in this market, including the issuance of ADRs and the agreement to be bound by corporate governance rules, was commercial in nature. *First Nat’l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 624 (1983). A private corporation could certainly agree to bylaws requiring a tender offer upon a change of control and could be sued for breaching those provisions. Therefore, the refusal to honor these contractual obligations constitutes commercial activity. The defense's attempt to characterize the entire dispute as a sovereign act because the shares were acquired via legislation ignores the fact that the claims are "based upon" the subsequent commercial conduct of breaching the bylaws.

Furthermore, the plaintiff has established the requisite "direct effect in the United States." The cancellation of dividend payments directly impacted U.S. investors holding ADRs, and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs occurred in New York. *Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina*, 965 F.2d 699, 713 (9th Cir. 1992). Because the claims are based upon commercial conduct that caused a direct effect in the United States, the commercial activity exception applies, and the district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Bar the Plaintiff's Claims**

The defendants also argue that the act of state doctrine requires dismissal of this case. The act of state doctrine is a principle of comity that precludes courts from inquiring into the validity of public acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964). However, the doctrine "does not apply to claims based on private contracts." *Kellogg Brown & Root Servs., Inc. v. Republic of Iraq*, 700 F.3d 527, 536 (D.C. Cir. 2012).

The plaintiff's claims arise from the breach of YPF's bylaws, which are contractual provisions governing the relationship between the company and its shareholders. The plaintiff does not seek to invalidate Argentina's expropriation law or challenge Argentina's sovereign authority to nationalize assets for public use. Rather, the plaintiff seeks to enforce the specific contractual terms that Argentina voluntarily adopted to facilitate the privatization of YPF. Enforcing these terms does not require the Court to question the validity of the expropriation itself; it merely requires determining whether the manner in which the acquisition was effected complied with the pre-existing contractual obligations.

The defendants' assertion that the bylaws cannot bind Argentina's sovereign power is misplaced. While a sovereign cannot contract away its power to expropriate, it can agree to the commercial terms governing the acquisition of shares, including the obligation to pay for them or follow specific procedures. The act of state doctrine is not a shield for breaches of commercial contracts, even when those contracts involve a foreign sovereign. The defense's reliance on *Sabbatino* is inapposite because that case involved a challenge to the validity of a foreign sovereign's expropriation decree itself, whereas this case involves a breach of contract claim that can be adjudicated without declaring the expropriation invalid. Consequently, the district court properly denied the motion to dismiss on this ground.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants' arguments regarding the application of the FSIA and the act of state doctrine are unpersuasive. The district court's order is affirmed in part, and the appeal is dismissed in part.

**FINAL RULING**

For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms the district court's order denying the defendants' motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA). The Court further dismisses the defendants' appeal regarding the act of state doctrine.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The District Court Properly Exercised Jurisdiction Under the FSIA**

The defendants contend that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction because the claims arise from Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation rather than commercial activity. We disagree.

The FSIA provides the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the United States. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992). Under the "commercial activity" exception, a foreign state is not immune from suit in any case "based upon ... an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere ... and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The determination of whether an act is "commercial" turns on the nature of the act, not its motive. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. An act is commercial if it is "the kind of activity by which a private party may engage in." 28 U.S.C. § 1603(d). The defendants argue that the expropriation of YPF shares pursuant to Law 26,741 is a sovereign act of eminent domain that no private party could perform, citing *West v. Deutsche Bank AG*, 887 F.3d 1084, 1092 (9th Cir. 2018). While the power of eminent domain is uniquely sovereign, the specific conduct upon which the plaintiff's claims are based is not the validity of the expropriation decree itself, but rather the alleged breach of the contractual obligations contained in YPF's bylaws.

The plaintiff alleges that Argentina, having acquired control of YPF, refused to make the required tender offer to all shareholders and exercised voting rights in contravention of sections 7 and 28 of the bylaws. These bylaws were part of a commercial transaction—the 1993 IPO—designed to attract investment. Argentina's participation in this market, including the issuance of ADRs and the agreement to be bound by corporate governance rules, was commercial in nature. *First Nat’l City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 624 (1983). A private corporation could certainly agree to bylaws requiring a tender offer upon a change of control and could be sued for breaching those provisions. Therefore, the refusal to honor these contractual obligations constitutes commercial activity. The defense's attempt to characterize the entire dispute as a sovereign act because the shares were acquired via legislation ignores the fact that the claims are "based upon" the subsequent commercial conduct of breaching the bylaws.

Furthermore, the plaintiff has established the requisite "direct effect in the United States." The cancellation of dividend payments directly impacted U.S. investors holding ADRs, and the subsequent foreclosure of Petersen's ADRs occurred in New York. *Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina*, 965 F.2d 699, 713 (9th Cir. 1992). Because the claims are based upon commercial conduct that caused a direct effect in the United States, the commercial activity exception applies, and the district court correctly denied the motion to dismiss.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine Does Not Bar the Plaintiff's Claims**

The defendants also argue that the act of state doctrine requires dismissal of this case. The act of state doctrine is a principle of comity that precludes courts from inquiring into the validity of public acts of a foreign sovereign within its own territory. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 423 (1964). However, the doctrine "does not apply to claims based on private contracts." *Kellogg Brown & Root Servs., Inc. v. Republic of Iraq*, 700 F.3d 527, 536 (D.C. Cir. 2012).

The plaintiff's claims arise from the breach of YPF's bylaws, which are contractual provisions governing the relationship between the company and its shareholders. The plaintiff does not seek to invalidate Argentina's expropriation law or challenge Argentina's sovereign authority to nationalize assets for public use. Rather, the plaintiff seeks to enforce the specific contractual terms that Argentina voluntarily adopted to facilitate the privatization of YPF. Enforcing these terms does not require the Court to question the validity of the expropriation itself; it merely requires determining whether the manner in which the acquisition was effected complied with the pre-existing contractual obligations.

The defendants' assertion that the bylaws cannot bind Argentina's sovereign power is misplaced. While a sovereign cannot contract away its power to expropriate, it can agree to the commercial terms governing the acquisition of shares, including the obligation to pay for them or follow specific procedures. The act of state doctrine is not a shield for breaches of commercial contracts, even when those contracts involve a foreign sovereign. The defense's reliance on *Sabbatino* is inapposite because that case involved a challenge to the validity of a foreign sovereign's expropriation decree itself, whereas this case involves a breach of contract claim that can be adjudicated without declaring the expropriation invalid. Consequently, the district court properly denied the motion to dismiss on this ground.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendants' arguments regarding the application of the FSIA and the act of state doctrine are unpersuasive. The district court's order is affirmed in part, and the appeal is dismissed in part.
