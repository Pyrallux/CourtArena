## INTRODUCTION

## I. THE PROSECUTION'S ATTEMPT TO PRESERVE THE SUBSTANTIVE ADDITUR DETERMINATION IS IMPROPER

The prosecution argues that this Court should "affirm the trial court's substantive determination that the jury's award was inadequate" while reversing only on procedural grounds. This request fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the error committed and the proper remedy on remand.

The trial court's decision to grant additur was a single, unified ruling that cannot be bifurcated into separable substantive and procedural components. The court made no findings on the record explaining why the jury's noneconomic damages award was inadequate, nor did it articulate the basis for the specific $15,000 figure. In *Rizzo v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 178 Conn. 351, 423 A.2d 1 (Conn. 1979), the Connecticut Supreme Court emphasized that additur requires a careful judicial assessment of the evidence to determine whether the jury's award was grossly inadequate. Here, the trial court granted additur without any articulated justification in the record, and immediately rendered judgment without providing the defense the statutory choice. This procedural failure infects the entire additur determination.

Furthermore, the prosecution's reliance on *D'Amico v. Board of Education*, 218 Conn. 1, 587 A.2d 1014 (Conn. 1991), is misplaced. That case stands for the unremarkable proposition that appellate courts may decline to reach substantive issues when procedural defects require reversal. It does not stand for the proposition that a substantive determination made through a fundamentally flawed process should be preserved. To the contrary, when a trial court commits reversible procedural error in rendering a judgment, the proper remedy is a full remand that allows the parties to litigate all aspects of the dispute anew.

## II. THE DEFENSE MUST BE AFFORDED THE OPPORTUNITY TO CHALLENGE THE SUFFICIENCY OF THE ADDITUR ON REMAND

The prosecution's request would deny the defense the ability to challenge the substantive basis for additur. This would be fundamentally unfair and contrary to the principles of due process. The defense was deprived of the opportunity to object to the additur amount, to present evidence regarding the adequacy of the jury's award, or to elect a new trial when the additur was imposed without proper procedure.

On remand, the defense must be permitted to argue that the jury's original verdict was supported by the evidence and that additur was improper. The defense may present evidence that the plaintiff's injuries were minimal, that the jury's noneconomic damages award was reasonable, or that the additur amount bears no rational relationship to the evidence presented at trial. The trial court, in turn, must make specific findings explaining its determination that the jury's award was inadequate and must provide a reasoned basis for any additur amount.

In *Berman v. Berman*, 179 Conn. 373, 427 A.2d 409 (Conn. 1980), the Connecticut Supreme Court recognized that additur is an extraordinary remedy that implicates fundamental fairness. The Court should not permit the prosecution to circumvent the protections afforded to defendants by preserving a substantive determination made through a process that violated basic tenets of fair procedure.

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT REGARDING THE ADEQUACY OF THE JURY'S VERDICT IS PREMATURE AND UNSUPPORTED

The prosecution cites *Rizzo v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. The jury awarded $3,285 in economic damages, which suggests limited medical expenses and wage loss. The prosecution has presented no evidence—because the trial court made no findings—that the plaintiff's injuries were more severe than the jury's verdict suggested.

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the judgment of the trial court following a jury verdict in favor of the plaintiff, Rita A. Wallace. The defendants, Hector L. Haddock and Subaru Leasing Corporation, appeal the trial court's order granting an additur in the amount of $15,000 and immediately entering judgment without providing the defendants the statutory alternative of a new trial. The defendants also challenge the propriety of the trial court's substantive determination regarding the inadequacy of the jury's award.

The relevant procedural facts are undisputed. On January 7, 2002, the jury awarded the plaintiff $3,285 in economic damages and $1,715 in noneconomic damages. The court then "immediately rendered judgment for the plaintiff, including the additur," without offering the defendants the option to reject the increased award and elect a new trial.

Both the prosecution and the defense agree that the trial court committed reversible error by failing to offer the defendants the choice between accepting the additur or proceeding to a new trial. This Court agrees. In *Berman v. Berman*, 179 Conn. 373, 427 A.2d 409 (Conn. 1980), the Supreme Court of Connecticut held that "the procedure for additur requires that the trial court offer the defendant the alternative of a new trial if the additur is not accepted. Failure to do so is reversible error." This principle was reaffirmed in *Kozouff v. Kozouff*, 189 Conn. 568, 457 A.2d 1016 (Conn. 1983). The trial court's failure to adhere to this mandatory procedure under General Statutes §§ 52-216a and 52-228b necessitates reversal.

The central dispute on appeal concerns the scope of the remand. The prosecution requests that this Court affirm the trial court's substantive determination that the jury's award was inadequate, while reversing solely on procedural grounds and remanding solely to offer the defendants the choice between the $15,000 additur and a new trial. The defense opposes this request, arguing that the trial court's procedural failure infects the entire ruling and that the court made no findings to support the additur amount.

The Court finds the defense's arguments persuasive regarding the scope of the remand. The record reflects that the trial court granted the additur "immediately" and without making specific findings of fact on the record explaining why the jury's noneconomic damages award of $1,715 was grossly inadequate. In *Rizzo v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 178 Conn. 351, 423 A.2d 1 (Conn. 1979), the Supreme Court established that additur is appropriate only when the jury's award is "so grossly inadequate as to shock the judicial conscience." This standard requires a careful judicial assessment of the evidence.

Here, the trial court did not articulate the basis for its conclusion that the verdict shocked the judicial conscience, nor did it explain its reasoning for the specific $15,000 figure. Without such findings, this Court cannot determine whether the trial court properly applied the *Rizzo* standard. The prosecution's reliance on *D'Amico v. Board of Education*, 218 Conn. 1, 587 A.2d 1014 (Conn. 1991), to preserve the substantive additur determination is misplaced. While *D'Amico* holds that appellate courts may decline to reach substantive issues when procedural defects require reversal, it does not authorize this Court to affirm a substantive determination that lacks a sufficient factual basis in the record.

The defense correctly argues that the trial court's ruling was a unified decision that was fundamentally flawed by the lack of findings and the denial of the defendants' statutory rights. To affirm the $15,000 additur amount without a record supporting it would constitute an impermissible "bootstrap" of a substantive determination onto a procedurally invalid judgment. See *Berman v. Berman*, 179 Conn. 373, 427 A.2d 409 (Conn. 1980) (recognizing that additur implicates fundamental fairness).

Therefore, the judgment of the trial court is reversed. The matter is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion. On remand, the trial court must conduct a proper analysis of the plaintiff's motion for additur, make specific findings of fact regarding the inadequacy of the verdict, and determine an appropriate additur amount, if warranted. If the court again grants an additur, it must then offer the defendants the statutory choice between accepting the additur or proceeding to a new trial on the issue of damages.

On remand, the trial court must ensure that all statutory procedures are followed.
