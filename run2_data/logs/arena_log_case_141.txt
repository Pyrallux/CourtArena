## DEFENSE APPELLANT'S OPENING BRIEF

---

### I. INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Appellant Robert Lalor respectfully submits this opening brief in support of his appeal from the judgment of the trial court committing him as a sexually violent person under Wisconsin Statute Chapter 980. The State has failed to meet its constitutional burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder that creates a substantial probability he will engage in future sexually violent acts. The trial court erred in admitting and relying upon unreliable actuarial instruments that measure nothing more than statistical probabilities derived from group data, rather than individualized assessments of Lalor's actual future dangerousness. This Court should reverse the commitment order and discharge Lalor.

---

### II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts as established in the record demonstrate the following:

On February 8, 1999, the State petitioned for Lalor's detention under Wisconsin's Sexually Violent Person Act, Wis. Stat. § 980.01 *et seq.*, alleging that Lalor was within ninety days of discharging his sentence for a 1992 conviction of first-degree sexual assault of a child. The State relied upon the examination report of Dr. Hugh Moore, a licensed psychologist who examined Lalor in September 1998.

Dr. Moore diagnosed Lalor with pedophilia and antisocial personality disorder. Critically, Dr. Moore's assessment was based entirely upon actuarial instruments—the Rapid Risk Assessment for Sexual Offense Recidivism (RRASOR)—which yielded a score suggesting a 49.8% probability of reoffending within five years and a 73.1% probability within ten years. These figures represent group statistics, not individualized predictions of Lalor's specific future conduct.

Following a probable cause hearing, the trial court ordered Lalor remain in custody for a court-ordered evaluation. Dr. Sheila Fields conducted this evaluation and reached conclusions similar to Dr. Moore's, relying upon multiple actuarial instruments including the RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG.

The trial court appointed Dr. Lynn Maskel as a defense expert. Dr. Maskel's testimony was limited to exposing the methodological limitations and scientific inadequacies of the actuarial tools upon which the State relied. Dr. Maskel did not offer an alternative diagnosis or conclusion; rather, she demonstrated that these instruments fail to reliably predict individual recidivism and suffer from significant base rate problems, false positive rates, and validation deficiencies.

The trial court found that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder and is dangerous because his mental disorder creates a substantial probability he will commit acts of sexual violence. The court committed Lalor to the custody of the Department of Social Services.

---

### III. STANDARD OF APPELLATE REVIEW

This Court reviews the trial court's commitment order de novo, applying constitutional standards to determine whether the State has proven beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant is a sexually violent person. The question of whether the evidence was sufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts is a question of law this Court reviews independently. *State v. Curiel*, 227 Wis. 2d 389, 597 N.W.2d 697 (1999).

While this Court normally defers to the trial court's credibility determinations, such deference does not extend to the legal question of whether the evidence, viewed most favorably to the State, is sufficient to satisfy the constitutional burden of proof. The ultimate question of whether the State has proven each element beyond a reasonable doubt is reviewed de novo. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307 (1979).

---

### IV. ARGUMENT

#### A. The State Failed to Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That Lalor Is a Sexually Violent Person

Under Wisconsin Statute § 980.01(7), a "sexually violent person" is defined as "a person who has been convicted of a sexually violent offense" and "is dangerous to others because of a mental disorder that creates a substantial probability that the person will commit a sexually violent act." The State bears the burden of proving this definition beyond a reasonable doubt. *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346 (1997).

The evidence presented by the State fails to establish this element. The State's experts, Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields, relied almost exclusively upon actuarial instruments—the RRASOR, MnSOST, MnSOST-R, and V-RAG—to establish Lalor's alleged "substantial probability" of reoffending. These instruments are fundamentally incapable of proving what the statute requires: that this particular individual, Lalor, poses a substantial probability of future dangerousness.

The RRASOR, the primary instrument relied upon by the State, is an actuarial tool that assigns points based on static factors—prior offenses, age, victim gender, and relationship to victim—and produces a statistical probability of recidivism derived from group data. As Dr. Maskel's testimony established, these instruments do not assess an individual's current mental state, treatment progress, or capacity for behavioral control. They measure group averages, not individual futures.

The United States Supreme Court in *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002), emphasized that civil commitment requires proof of "serious difficulty in controlling behavior" that "makes it dangerous" to release the person. The State's evidence did not establish that Lalor lacks control over his behavior. The actuarial instruments upon which the State relied do not measure volitional control; they measure statistical likelihood based on historical factors. Proof of dangerousness requiring civil commitment cannot rest upon group statistics applied to an individual without individualized assessment of that individual's current mental state and capacity for self-control. *In re Commitment of Nowacki*, 2001 WI 120, 248 Wis. 2d 244, 635 N.W.2d 789.

The State argues that the trial court was entitled to credit the experts' testimony. However, the question before this Court is not whether the trial court found the experts credible, but whether the evidence presented was legally sufficient to satisfy the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard. Even credited expert testimony regarding actuarial scores cannot substitute for proof of individualized dangerousness. The 73.1% "probability" cited by the State represents a statistical abstraction—a prediction about what percentage of men with similar backgrounds might reoffend—not proof that this particular man, Robert Lalor, will in fact reoffend.

This Court held in *State v. Klessig*, 2000 WI 130, 239 Wis. 2d 194, 619 N.W.2d 806, that disputes regarding the reliability of expert testimony go to the weight of the evidence. However, this principle does not excuse the State from meeting its constitutional burden. When the very foundation of the State's evidence is scientifically unreliable, no amount of credibility weighting can transform that evidence into proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

#### B. The Trial Court Erred in Admitting the Results of Unreliable Actuarial Instruments

The trial court erred in admitting and relying upon the results of the RRASOR and related actuarial instruments. Under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), and its Wisconsin counterpart, *State v. Dennis*, 1998 WI 85, 237 Wis. 2d 81, 597 N.W.2d 163, the trial court must ensure that expert testimony is based on reliable scientific methodology.

The actuarial instruments at issue here suffer from multiple reliability deficiencies:

First, these instruments were developed and validated on different populations than Lalor. The RRASOR and MnSOST were validated primarily on samples of convicted sexual offenders in Minnesota and other jurisdictions. There is no evidence that these instruments have been validated on the Wisconsin population or on individuals with Lalor's specific combination of diagnoses.

Second, these instruments produce high false positive rates when applied to individuals. A 73.1% probability of recidivism means that approximately 27% of individuals similarly classified will not reoffend. The State asks the trial court to commit Lalor based on a prediction that is wrong nearly three times out of ten—a standard that falls far short of beyond a reasonable doubt.

Third, these instruments measure static risk factors that cannot be modified through treatment. They do not account for post-offense treatment, behavioral changes, or other individual circumstances that may reduce risk. Dr. Maskel's testimony established that these instruments fail to capture relevant individualized data.

The trial court erred in admitting this testimony without adequately assessing its reliability. The admission of unreliable actuarial evidence violated Lalor's due process rights and requires reversal.

#### C. The State's Due Process Arguments Fail

The State invokes *Laxton v. State*, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 131, 646 N.W.2d 784, for the proposition that the trial court was not required to make an express finding that Lalor is "unable to control his behavior." However, *Laxton* does not stand for the proposition that no finding of lack of control is required. Rather, *Laxton* interpreted *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. 407 (2002), as requiring some lack-of-control finding, though not necessarily an express one.

The critical deficiency in this case is that the State presented no evidence whatsoever regarding Lalor's ability to control his behavior. The State's experts testified about actuarial probabilities and diagnoses but never addressed the constitutional requirement articulated in *Crane*: whether Lalor has "serious difficulty controlling behavior." The State argues this element was "inherently demonstrated" by evidence of high recidivism risk and mental disorder diagnosis. This argument conflates two distinct requirements.

A diagnosis of pedophilia and antisocial personality disorder, combined with a high actuarial score, does not establish serious difficulty in controlling behavior. Many individuals with these diagnoses successfully control their behavior through treatment, medication, or self-management strategies. The absence of any evidence regarding Lalor's actual volitional control is fatal to the commitment.

The *Crane* Court explicitly rejected the argument that proof of dangerousness alone is sufficient. Rather, the Constitution requires proof of "lack of control" that is "sufficient to distinguish between a dangerous sexual offender who can be civilly committed and a dangerous sexual offender who cannot be." *Kansas v. Crane*, 534 U.S. at 411-12. The State failed to present any evidence on this crucial element.

#### D. The State Failed to Prove Substantial Probability as Required by the Statute

Even accepting the State's evidence at face value, the evidence fails to establish "substantial probability" as required by Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). The term "substantial probability" requires more than a statistical likelihood based on group data. It requires individualized proof that this particular person is substantially likely to commit future acts.

The 73.1% probability cited by the State represents a group statistic. It means that, of 100 men with similar characteristics, approximately 73 might be expected to reoffend within ten years. This does not establish that Lalor specifically—given his particular circumstances, mental state, and potential for treatment—is substantially likely to reoffend.

Proof of "substantial probability" requires individualized assessment. The State presented no evidence regarding Lalor's treatment history, his response to treatment, his current mental state, or any factors that would distinguish him from the statistical average. The trial court's finding of substantial probability rests entirely upon group statistics applied mechanically to Lalor, without individualized determination of his own probability of recidivism.

This Court should hold that the State must prove, through evidence specific to the individual, that the person's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future dangerous acts. Group actuarial data, standing alone, is insufficient to meet this burden.

---

### V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant-Appellant Robert Lalor respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and discharge Lalor from commitment. The State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor is a sexually violent person. The evidence presented was constitutionally insufficient to establish that Lalor suffers from a mental disorder that creates a substantial probability he will commit future sexually violent acts. The trial court erred in admitting unreliable actuarial evidence and in failing to require proof of Lalor's lack of behavioral control as required by the Due Process Clause.

Alternatively, this Court should remand for a new trial at which the State is required to present individualized evidence of dangerousness, including evidence regarding Lalor's lack of control over his behavior, and at which the reliability of actuarial instruments is properly assessed under *Daubert* and *Dennis* standards.

**STATE OF WISCONSIN, Plaintiff-Respondent,**

**OPINION**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the court on appeal from a judgment of conviction and order of commitment pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 980.01(7). Defendant-Appellant Robert Lalor argues that the State failed to meet its burden of proving that he is a sexually violent person and that the trial court erred in admitting actuarial evidence. For the reasons discussed below, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The appellate court reviews the trial court's legal conclusions regarding the sufficiency of the evidence de novo. State v. Curiel, 227 Wis. 2d 389, 597 N.W.2d 697 (1999). The question of whether the evidence is sufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts is a question of law reviewed independently. Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979). However, this court defers to the trial court's credibility determinations and the weight given to expert testimony unless the witness is incredible as a matter of law. State v. Klessig, 2000 WI 130, 239 Wis. 2d 194, 619 N.W.2d 806.

**II. ADMISSION OF ACTUARIAL EVIDENCE**

Lalor argues that the trial court erred in admitting the results of the Rapid Risk Assessment for Sexual Offense Recidivism (RRASOR), Minnesota Sex Offender Screening Tool (MnSOST), and other actuarial instruments because they are unreliable and measure only group statistics rather than individualized dangerousness. He relies on Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), and its Wisconsin counterpart, State v. Dennis, 1998 WI 85, 237 Wis. 2d 81, 597 N.W.2d 163, to argue that the trial court must exclude expert testimony based on unreliable scientific methodology.

The State counters that disputes regarding the reliability of expert testimony go to the weight of the evidence, not its admissibility, citing State v. Klessig, 2000 WI 130, 239 Wis. 2d 194, 619 N.W.2d 806. The record shows that Dr. Hugh Moore and Dr. Sheila Fields testified regarding Lalor's diagnoses of pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder and utilized the actuarial tools to support their conclusions. The defense expert, Dr. Lynn Maskel, testified regarding the limitations of these tools but did not offer an alternative diagnosis or conclusion regarding Lalor's risk.

Under State v. Klessig, 2000 WI 130, 239 Wis. 2d 194, 619 N.W.2d 806, the trial court has the discretion to admit expert testimony, and challenges to the methodology generally affect the weight the trier of fact assigns to that testimony. Because Dr. Maskel's testimony exposed the limitations of the tools but did not render the State's experts incredible as a matter of law, the trial court did not err in admitting the evidence. The jury, or in this case the trial court sitting as the trier of fact, was free to resolve the disputes regarding the tools' reliability in favor of the State. The defense's argument that the evidence is scientifically insufficient is a request for this court to reweigh the evidence, which we cannot do.

**III. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE AND DUE PROCESS**

Lalor contends that the State failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he is a sexually violent person because the actuarial evidence does not establish the "substantial probability" required by statute and fails to demonstrate the "serious difficulty controlling behavior" required by the Due Process Clause.

**A. Substantial Probability**

Lalor argues that the State's evidence—consisting primarily of actuarial scores derived from group data—cannot establish the "substantial probability" that he, as an individual, will reoffend. He cites In re Commitment of Nowacki, 2001 WI 120, 248 Wis. 2d 244, 635 N.W.2d 789, for the proposition that dangerousness must be shown on an individual basis.

The State presented expert testimony from Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields that Lalor suffers from pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder. Dr. Moore's RRASOR score indicated a 73.1% likelihood of reoffending within ten years, and Dr. Fields utilized multiple actuarial tools which converged on a high risk assessment. While the defense correctly notes that these tools rely on static factors and group data, the experts applied these tools to Lalor's specific history and diagnoses. The trial court found this evidence sufficient to establish a substantial probability of reoffense. Under Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979), this court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the State. The State's evidence, if credited, supports the finding that Lalor's mental disorders create a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts.

**B. Lack of Control**

Lalor argues that the State failed to prove the "serious difficulty controlling behavior" element required by Kansas v. Crane, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). He asserts that a diagnosis of pedophilia and a high actuarial score do not, by themselves, prove a lack of volitional control.

The State responds that this element was "inherently demonstrated" by the evidence of Lalor's mental disorders and high recidivism risk. The State relies on Laxton v. State, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 131, 646 N.W.2d 784, which held that the trial court is not required to make an express finding that the respondent is "unable to control his behavior," provided the record supports such a finding.

The record establishes that Lalor was diagnosed with pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder. Dr. Moore's evaluation using the Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R) was consistent with the label of psychopath. These diagnoses, combined with the actuarial prediction of a high likelihood of reoffending, provide a sufficient basis for the trial court to infer that Lalor has serious difficulty controlling his behavior. As held in Laxton v. State, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 131, 646 N.W.2d 784, the Constitution does not require a separate finding on volitional impairment so long as the evidence of mental abnormality and dangerousness supports an inference of lack of control. The trial court's implicit finding on this issue is supported by the expert testimony regarding the nature of Lalor's disorders and his high risk of reoffense.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly admitted the actuarial evidence, and the State presented sufficient evidence to support the commitment order. The trial court's findings are not clearly erroneous, and the State met its burden of proving that Lalor is a sexually violent person. Therefore, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

However, *Jackson* establishes the standard for criminal convictions where the burden is "beyond a reasonable doubt." In civil commitment proceedings under Chapter 980, while the State bears the burden of proving the statutory elements beyond a reasonable doubt, the question remains whether the evidence presented is legally sufficient to satisfy constitutional requirements. *In re Commitment of Nowacki, 2001 WI 120, 248 Wis. 2d 244, 635 N.W.2d 789*.

In *Nowacki*, this Court explicitly held that "the State must prove the essential elements of a sexually violent person commitment by proof beyond a reasonable doubt," and that "the determination of whether the State has met its burden of proof is a question of law that this court reviews independently." The prosecution's reliance on *Jackson* for the proposition that this Court cannot reweigh evidence conflates the standard of review with the sufficiency of the evidence inquiry. ## II. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON LAXTON v. STATE IS MISPLACED

The Court in *Laxton* found sufficient evidence because the record contained testimony that the respondent "suffers from a mental abnormality that predisposes him to commit acts of sexual violence" and because "the nature of the mental abnormality is such that it can be said that the respondent has serious difficulty controlling his behavior."

The prosecution argues that *State v. Klessig, 2000 WI 130, 239 Wis. 2d 194, 619 N.W.2d 806* establishes that challenges to expert methodology go to weight, not admissibility. The Court explained that the factors to be considered include "whether the theory or technique can be (and has been) tested," "whether the theory or technique has been subjected to peer review and publication," "the known or potential rate of error," and "whether the theory or technique has gained general acceptance in the relevant scientific community."

This type of evidence is insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Lalor's mental disorder creates a substantial probability that he will engage in acts of sexual violence. The actuarial scores represent probabilities derived from group data, not individualized predictions. The prosecution's burden requires proof that Lalor, as an individual, is likely to reoffend—not that a group of offenders with similar characteristics is likely to reoffend.

The appellate court reviews the trial court's legal conclusions regarding the sufficiency of the evidence de novo. State v. Curiel, 227 Wis. 2d 389, 597 N.W.2d 697 (1999). The question of whether the evidence is sufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts is a question of law reviewed independently. **II. ADMISSION OF ACTUARIAL EVIDENCE**

The State counters that under State v. In Klessig, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that "disputes regarding the reliability of an expert's methodology go to the weight of the evidence, not its admissibility." Id., 239 Wis. 2d 194, ¶ 45. The defense expert, Dr. Lynn Maskel, testified regarding the limitations of the actuarial tools, specifically their reliance on static factors and group data. However, Dr. Maskel's testimony did not render the State's experts, Dr. Hugh Moore and Dr. Sheila Fields, incredible as a matter of law. The trial court was within its discretion to admit the testimony and allow the trier of fact to resolve any disputes regarding the tools' reliability. The defense's request to exclude the evidence entirely based on Dr. Maskel's critique is effectively a request to have this court reweigh the evidence, which we cannot do. State v. Curiel, 227 Wis. 2d 389, 597 N.W.2d 697 (1999).

**III. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

**A. Substantial Probability**

Lalor contends that the State failed to prove the "substantial probability" element because the actuarial evidence constitutes group statistics rather than an individualized assessment. He relies on In re Commitment of Nowacki, 2001 WI 120, 248 Wis. 2d 244, 635 N.W.2d 789, to argue that the State must prove the elements on an individual basis.

The defense argues that this application was merely mechanical and lacked individualized clinical judgment. However, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, as required by Jackson v. The defense's argument that the evidence is insufficient because it relies on actuarial tools is a challenge to the weight of the evidence, which the trial court was free to resolve in favor of the State.

**B. Serious Difficulty Controlling Behavior**

Lalor argues that the State failed to prove the "serious difficulty controlling behavior" element required by Kansas v. Crane, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). He asserts that a diagnosis of pedophilia and a high actuarial score do not, by themselves, prove a lack of volitional control, and that the record contains no evidence of behavioral manifestations or failed attempts to control behavior.

The State responds that this element was "inherently demonstrated" by the evidence of Lalor's mental disorders and high recidivism risk. The State relies on Laxton v. In Laxton, the court stated that "the record must contain sufficient evidence from which a trier of fact could infer that the respondent has serious difficulty controlling his behavior." The record establishes that Lalor was diagnosed with pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder. These diagnoses, combined with the actuarial prediction of a high likelihood of reoffending, provide a sufficient basis for the trial court to infer that Lalor has serious difficulty controlling his behavior. While the defense argues that there is no specific evidence of failed attempts to control behavior, Laxton does not require such specific evidence; it requires only that the nature of the mental abnormality and the dangerousness support an inference of lack of control. The evidence of pedophilia, psychopathy, and high recidivism risk meets this standard. As held in Laxton, the Constitution does not require a separate finding on volitional impairment so long as the evidence supports an inference of lack of control. The trial court's implicit finding on this issue is supported by the expert testimony.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The appellate court reviews the trial court's legal conclusions regarding the sufficiency of the evidence de novo. State v. Curiel, 227 Wis. 2d 389, 597 N.W.2d 697 (1999). The question of whether the evidence is sufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant's mental disorder creates a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts is a question of law reviewed independently. Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979). However, this court defers to the trial court's credibility determinations and the weight given to expert testimony unless the witness is incredible as a matter of law. State v. Klessig, 2000 WI 130, 239 Wis. 2d 194, 619 N.W.2d 806.

**II. ADMISSION OF ACTUARIAL EVIDENCE**

Lalor argues that the trial court erred in admitting the results of the Rapid Risk Assessment for Sexual Offense Recidivism (RRASOR), Minnesota Sex Offender Screening Tool (MnSOST), and other actuarial instruments. He contends the trial court failed to perform its gatekeeping function under Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), and State v. Dennis, 1998 WI 85, 237 Wis. 2d 81, 597 N.W.2d 163, because the instruments are unreliable and measure only group statistics. The defense asserts that the trial court should have excluded the evidence because Dr. Maskel exposed fundamental limitations in the testing instruments.

The State counters that under State v. Klessig, 2000 WI 130, 239 Wis. 2d 194, 619 N.W.2d 806, challenges to the reliability of expert testimony go to the weight of the evidence, not its admissibility. We agree. In Klessig, the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that "disputes regarding the reliability of an expert's methodology go to the weight of the evidence, not its admissibility." Id., 239 Wis. 2d 194, ¶ 45. The defense expert, Dr. Lynn Maskel, testified extensively regarding the limitations of the actuarial tools, specifically their reliance on static factors and group data. The trial court allowed this testimony, thereby satisfying the gatekeeping requirements of Daubert and Dennis by permitting the jury to hear the critiques of the methodology. The fact that Dr. Maskel's testimony exposed problems with the instruments does not render the State's experts, Dr. Hugh Moore and Dr. Sheila Fields, incredible as a matter of law. The trial court was within its discretion to admit the testimony and allow the trier of fact to resolve any disputes regarding the tools' reliability. The defense's request to exclude the evidence entirely based on Dr. Maskel's critique is effectively a request to have this court reweigh the evidence, which we cannot do. State v. Curiel, 227 Wis. 2d 389, 597 N.W.2d 697 (1999).

**III. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

**A. Substantial Probability**

Lalor contends that the State failed to prove the "substantial probability" element because the actuarial evidence constitutes group statistics rather than an individualized assessment. He relies on In re Commitment of Nowacki, 2001 WI 120, 248 Wis. 2d 244, 635 N.W.2d 789, to argue that the State must prove the elements on an individual basis. The defense argues that the experts' application of the actuarial tools was "mechanical" and lacked individualized clinical judgment.

The State presented expert testimony from Dr. Moore and Dr. Fields that Lalor suffers from pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder. Dr. Moore's RRASOR score indicated a 73.1% likelihood of reoffending within ten years, and Dr. Fields utilized multiple actuarial tools which converged on a high risk assessment. While the defense correctly notes that these tools rely on group data, the experts applied these tools to Lalor's specific history, diagnoses, and PCL-R score. The defense argues that this application was merely mechanical and lacked individualized clinical judgment. However, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, as required by Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307 (1979), the experts' testimony linking the actuarial results to Lalor's specific condition supports the finding that his mental disorders create a substantial probability of future sexually violent acts. The defense's argument that the evidence is insufficient because it relies on actuarial tools is a challenge to the weight of the evidence, which the trial court was free to resolve in favor of the State. As held in Nowacki, the State must prove the elements on an individual basis, but it may use actuarial tools to do so where, as here, the experts apply those tools to the respondent's specific characteristics.

**B. Serious Difficulty Controlling Behavior**

Lalor argues that the State failed to prove the "serious difficulty controlling behavior" element required by Kansas v. Crane, 534 U.S. 407 (2002). He asserts that a diagnosis of pedophilia and a high actuarial score do not, by themselves, prove a lack of volitional control, and that the record contains no evidence of behavioral manifestations or failed attempts to control behavior. The defense emphasizes that the record lacks evidence of impulsive acts, failed treatment, or specific instances where Lalor could not control his behavior.

The State responds that this element was "inherently demonstrated" by the evidence of Lalor's mental disorders and high recidivism risk. The State relies on Laxton v. State, 2002 WI 82, 254 Wis. 2d 131, 646 N.W.2d 784, which held that the trial court is not required to make an express finding that the respondent is "unable to control his behavior," provided the record supports such a finding. In Laxton, the court stated that "the record must contain sufficient evidence from which a trier of fact could infer that the respondent has serious difficulty controlling his behavior." Id., 254 Wis. 2d 131, ¶ 52.

The record establishes that Lalor was diagnosed with pedophilia and anti-social personality disorder. Dr. Moore's evaluation using the Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R) was consistent with the label of psychopath. These diagnoses, combined with the actuarial prediction of a high likelihood of reoffending, provide a sufficient basis for the trial court to infer that Lalor has serious difficulty controlling his behavior. While the defense argues that there is no specific evidence of failed attempts to control behavior, Laxton does not require such specific evidence; it requires only that the nature of the mental abnormality and the dangerousness support an inference of lack of control. The evidence of pedophilia, psychopathy, and high recidivism risk meets this standard. As held in Laxton, the Constitution does not require a separate finding on volitional impairment so long as the evidence supports an inference of lack of control. The trial court's implicit finding on this issue is supported by the expert testimony.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly admitted the actuarial evidence, and the State presented sufficient evidence to support the commitment order. The trial court's findings are not clearly erroneous, and the State met its burden of proving that Lalor is a sexually violent person. Therefore, the judgment of the trial court is affirmed.
