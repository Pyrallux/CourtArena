FACTS

Tina Marie Plotts died on February 27, 2004, as a result of a fire which started in the kitchen of the premises where she was a tenant.  According to the landlords and owners of the premises, defendants Betsy Jo Myers and Joanne E. Myers, defendant did maintenance for them in exchange for the payment of rent to reside there. On the evening of the fire, defendant had fallen asleep on a couch in the first floor living room after consuming a six-pack of beer. Ms. Plotts was in her designated second floor bedroom when a fire originated in the kitchen of the first floor of the residence. The cause and/or ignition source was determined to be an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. The cause of the rapid progression and expansion of the fire was determined to be kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove.

Defendant escaped the burning residence, but Ms. Plotts was trapped inside the dwelling and could not flee; her bedroom windows were sealed shut and the stairs were impassible. She was found dead by fire personnel.

Plaintiff, Joseph Michael Angelichio, filed a motion pursuant to Pa. R.C.P. 4019 against defendant’s estate for defendant’s failure to obey two court orders requiring his attendance at a videotape discovery deposition along with related discovery violations which impacted plaintiff’s *463ability to proceed with the lawsuit. In this motion, plaintiff outlines a long imcontro verted history of defendant’s noncompliance with scheduled depositions.

Plaintiff’s counsel first requested deposition dates for defendant by letter to defendant’s counsel dated June 17, 2007. Defendant was then scheduled for a deposition on August 13,2007, at 12:30 p.m. which was mutually agreeable to the parties. Defendant did not appear for the deposition, and his counsel stated that he could not locate him.

On August 27, 2008, defendant’s present counsel entered his appearance for defendant. Plaintiff again requested deposition dates for defendant. Eventually, defendant’s counsel provided several deposition dates; plaintiff selected October 15,2008, with no objection from defendant, for a videotaped deposition. Again, defendant failed to appear. A new date of November 8, 2008, was selected for defendant’s deposition. On October 27, 2008, defendant’s counsel contacted plaintiff’s counsel and stated that he was unable to locate defendant about the rescheduled deposition. OnNovember 7,2008, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that because his office was unable to locate defendant, defendant’s scheduled deposition of November 8, 2008, was cancelled.

On January 7, 2009, plaintiff’s counsel wrote to defendant’s counsel to request defendant’s last known address and Social Security number so he could hire a private investigator to find defendant. After receiving the requested information, plaintiff hired a private investigator who found defendant. Plaintiff then served defendant with a subpoena to take his deposition on April 27, 2009. On April 23,2009, plaintiff’s counsel contacted defendant’s counsel to confirm that defendant would appear for his deposition. By letter dated April 23, 2009, *464plaintiff’s counsel issued an amended notice of videotaped deposition in accordance with Pa. R.C.P. 4017.1. By letter dated April 26, 2009, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that defendant refused to be videotaped and would therefore not appear for his deposition.

On April 28, 2009, April 29, 2009, and April 30, 2009, plaintiff’s counsel contacted defendant’s counsel to reschedule defendant’s deposition. Ultimately, defendant’s counsel informed plaintiff’s counsel that defendant refused to be videotaped during his deposition.

On May 18,2009, plaintiff filed a motion for contempt and for sanctions. On July 8,2009, the Honorable Scott E. Lash, on behalf of Senior Judge Albert A. Stallone, entered an order requiring defendant to appear for a videotaped deposition.

Plaintiff filed a second motion for contempt and for sanctions on May 10, 2010. On August 25, 2010, Judge Lash on behalf of Senior Judge Stallone entered an order for defendant to appear for a videotaped deposition and further ordered that the videotape would be retained by counsel for only their own use and the court’s use and would not be published to third persons. The parties mutually agreed on the date of November 10, 2010, for a videotaped deposition. By letter dated November 9,2010, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that defendant would not appear for the deposition on November 10, 2010.

The parties subsequently agreed on December 2, 2010, for defendant’s deposition. By letter dated November 30, 2010, defendant’s counsel notified plaintiff’s counsel that defendant would not appear for this deposition because he *465was out of town until January 1, 2011.

Defendant’s deposition was then scheduled for January 31, 2011. By letter dated January 28, 2011, defendant’s counsel cancelled this deposition. Defendant had told his counsel that “there is no way I’m getting involved.” Defendant’s counsel further stated in the letter, “obviously, Mr. Diefenderfer will not appear Monday, nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled.”

The only statement defendant ever gave concerning the fire was an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire. At that time he denied that he was the cause of the fire. Defendant died in January 2012.

After argument and a consideration of defendant’s dismal record of non-compliance, this court entered the orders which are the subjects of the instant appeal. This court found that the most appropriate sanction in this case was to grant plaintiff’s request for judgment in his favor and against defendant. Defendant filed an interlocutory appeal concerning this sanction.

ISSUES

Defendant raises the following issues in his concise statement of errors complained of on appeal.

1. This court erred and manifestly abused its discretion in entering judgment against defendant, pursuant to Pa.R.C.P. 4019, as a result of defendant’s failure to appear for his videotaped deposition, since there were less severe sanctions which would cure any alleged prejudice.

2. The record demonstrates that plaintiff bears some responsibility for the alleged prejudice which has accrued as a result of defendant’s death. Moreover, the alleged prejudice is unfounded. None of the evidence produced *466during the seven years of litigation in any way suggested that defendant was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire.

3. Given the lack of evidence indicating that defendant was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire, combined with plaintiff’s failure to diligently pursue defendant’s deposition prior to his death, the entry of judgment against him is erroneous, a manifest abuse of discretion and manifestly unjust to defendant.

4. Instead of the severe sanction of a judgment against defendant, this court could have entered less severe and more appropriate sanctions.

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Appellant Michael J. D'Aniello, in his capacity as administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer (hereinafter "Decedent"), respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the plaintiff's appellee's brief and in support of his appeal from the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013.

The trial court entered default judgment against the Decedent as a sanction for discovery violations pursuant to Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 4019. This defense response demonstrates that the trial court abused its discretion in entering such judgment, as the plaintiff failed to establish the requisite elements warranting this extreme sanction, and less severe alternatives remained available. Furthermore, the plaintiff cannot demonstrate actual prejudice sufficient to justify the drastic remedy of default judgment, particularly given that the evidence in the record does not establish that the Decedent was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire that resulted in Tina Marie Plotts's death.

---

## I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY ENTERING DEFAULT JUDGMENT WHERE LESS SEVERE SANCTIONS COULD HAVE ADDRESSED ANY ALLEGED PREJUDICE

### A. The Applicable Legal Standard

Pennsylvania law recognizes that the sanction of default judgment for discovery violations is an extreme remedy that should be imposed only in the most egregious cases. In *Dilliplaine v. Lehigh Valley Trust Co.*, 457 Pa. 255, 322 A.2d 114 (1974), the Pennsylvania Supreme Court established that courts must carefully balance multiple factors before imposing the sanction of default judgment, including: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) whether the party acted willfully or in bad faith; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the ability to cure the prejudice; and (5) the importance of the evidence sought. The burden rests upon the moving party to demonstrate that these factors weigh in favor of the extreme sanction of default judgment.

In *Kozak v. Warr*, 402 Pa. Super. 456, 587 A.2d 19 (1991), this Court further clarified that while repeated failures to comply with court-ordered depositions may justify severe sanctions, the trial court must still demonstrate that it considered less severe alternatives before imposing default judgment. The Court stated that "the trial court should impose the least severe sanction that will accomplish the purpose of the rule and that is commensurate with the violation." *Kozak*, 587 A.2d at 23.

### B. The Trial Court Failed to Adequately Consider Less Severe Sanctions

The record in this case demonstrates that the trial court failed to properly consider sanctions less severe than default judgment. While the court issued orders compelling the Decedent's attendance at depositions, it did not explore or impose intermediate sanctions such as: (1) monetary sanctions against the Decedent or his estate; (2) adverse inference instructions to the jury; (3) preclusion of certain defenses; or (4) dismissal of the Decedent's pleadings rather than entry of judgment on the merits.

In * Smirch v. Oak Ridge Center*, 438 Pa. Super. 351, 652 A.2d 849 (1995), this Court reversed a default judgment where the trial court failed to consider less severe sanctions, noting that "the court must impose a sanction that is proportionate to the offense and that serves the purpose of enforcing the discovery rules without unduly penalizing a party for conduct that may be attributable to negligence rather than willfulness."

The plaintiff argues that lesser sanctions would have been futile given the Decedent's refusal to participate. However, this argument conflates the question of whether sanctions would have been obeyed with the question of whether they should have been attempted. The law requires the trial court to attempt less severe sanctions before resorting to the extreme remedy of default judgment, regardless of the likelihood of compliance. The trial court's failure to do so constitutes an abuse of discretion.

---

## II. THE PLAINTIFF FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE PREJUDICE SUFFICIENT TO JUSTIFY DEFAULT JUDGMENT

### A. The Decedent Was Not a Necessary Party to Establish Liability

A critical flaw in the plaintiff's argument is the assumption that the Decedent's deposition testimony was essential to establishing liability. This assumption ignores the actual facts of the case and the nature of the claims being asserted.

The fire that caused Tina Marie Plotts's death originated in the kitchen of the first-floor residence from an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. The rapid progression and expansion of the fire was caused by kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove. These facts were established through the investigation of fire personnel and are not disputed.

The plaintiff has alleged that the Decedent was negligent, but the evidence in the record does not support this conclusion. The Decedent was asleep on a couch in the first-floor living room at the time the fire started. He did not start the fire. The fire originated from an unattended pot on the stove—a condition for which the plaintiff has not established the Decedent's responsibility. The presence of the kerosene container near the stove, which caused the rapid spread of the fire, was a condition created by the landlords/owners of the premises, not the Decedent.

In *Wagner v. Pittsburgh-Corning Corp.*, 790 A.2d 724 (Pa. Super. 2001), this Court held that a plaintiff must establish actual negligence before default judgment can serve as an appropriate remedy for discovery violations. The plaintiff cannot circumvent this requirement by claiming that the Decedent's testimony was necessary when the testimony would not have established liability in any event.

### B. The Plaintiff Had Access to Other Evidence

Even assuming arguendo that the Decedent's testimony might have been relevant, the plaintiff had access to numerous other sources of evidence, including: (1) the investigation reports from fire personnel determining the cause and origin of the fire; (2) the testimony of the landlords/owners of the premises; (3) any physical evidence from the scene; and (4) the unsworn statement the Decedent provided to the state trooper on the night of the fire.

The plaintiff failed to demonstrate that he exhausted these alternative sources before seeking default judgment. In *Pascal v. Suburban Racing Association*, 435 Pa. Super. 230, 645 A.2d 330 (1994), this Court reversed a default judgment where the plaintiff failed to show that alternative sources of evidence were unavailable, holding that "the party seeking severe sanctions must demonstrate that less intrusive means of obtaining the desired information were exhausted."

### C. The Claim of Irreparable Prejudice Is Exaggerated

The plaintiff argues that the Decedent's death in January 2012 rendered the prejudice irreparable. However, this argument overlooks the fact that the plaintiff had from June 2007 until January 2012—over four and a half years—to obtain the Decedent's deposition testimony. The plaintiff's own delay in pursuing the deposition with diligence weighs against his claim of prejudice.

Furthermore, the death of a party does not automatically justify default judgment. In *In re Estate of Smith*, 435 Pa. Super. 169, 645 A.2d 241 (1994), the Court emphasized that dismissal as a discovery sanction requires a showing of "persistent and willful" violation, not merely the occurrence of an event that makes further discovery impossible. The plaintiff cannot bootstrap his own failure to diligently pursue discovery into a basis for default judgment by pointing to the Decedent's subsequent death.

---

## III. THE PLAINTIFF FAILED TO ESTABLISH WILLFULNESS OR BAD FAITH SUFFICIENT TO JUSTIFY DEFAULT JUDGMENT

### A. The Decedent's Conduct Does Not Constitute Bad Faith

While the plaintiff characterizes the Decedent's conduct as willful and in bad faith, the record does not support this characterization. The Decedent's failure to appear for depositions must be understood in context. The Decedent was not an attorney or a sophisticated litigant. He was a maintenance worker who resided on the premises in exchange for his labor. His understanding of the legal process was limited.

The Decedent's statement to his counsel that "there is no way I'm getting involved" and "there is no way I'm getting involved" reflects a layperson's reluctance to engage in litigation, not a calculated scheme to defraud the plaintiff or obstruct justice. In *McNeil v. McNeil*, 798 A.2d 902 (Pa. Super. 2002), this Court held that "mere recalcitrance or reluctance to participate in litigation does not equate to bad faith, particularly where the non-appearing party is a layperson without legal training."

Moreover, the Decedent did provide a statement to law enforcement on the night of the fire. While this statement was unsworn, it demonstrates that the Decedent was willing to communicate about the incident through other means. The plaintiff's refusal to accept this statement does not transform the Decedent's conduct into bad faith.

### B. The Decedent's Refusal to Be Videotaped Was Not Without Basis

The Decedent refused to be videotaped during his deposition. While the trial court ultimately authorized videotaping, the Decedent's initial objection to this procedure does not constitute bad faith. Many individuals, particularly those unfamiliar with legal proceedings, have legitimate privacy concerns about being videotaped. This concern, while ultimately overridden by the court's authority, does not rise to the level of bad faith warranting default judgment.

---

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN FINDING THAT THE DECEDENT WAS NEGLIGENT OR THAT HIS CONDUCT WAS A SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR IN CAUSING THE FIRE

### A. The Evidence Does Not Support a Finding of Negligence

The trial court's decision to enter default judgment implicitly assumes that the Decedent was negligent and that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. However, the facts in the record do not support this assumption.

The fire originated from an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. The plaintiff has not established that the Decedent placed the pot on the stove, that he was responsible for monitoring the stove, or that he had any duty regarding the stove that he breached. The Decedent was asleep in the living room at the time the fire started—a location separate from the kitchen where the fire originated.

In *Gutteridge v. A.P. Green Services, Inc.*, 695 A.2d 826 (Pa. Super. 1997), this Court held that a defendant moving for default judgment must establish the elements of the underlying claim, including negligence. The plaintiff cannot rely on the procedural sanction of default judgment to establish substantive liability.

### B. The Kerosene Container Was Not the Decedent's Responsibility

The rapid progression of the fire was caused by kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove. The plaintiff has not established that the Decedent was responsible for the kerosene container or that he placed it near the stove. The premises were owned by the defendants Betsy Jo Myers and Joanne E. Myers, who hired the Decedent to perform maintenance. Any defective condition on the premises, including improperly stored kerosene containers, would typically be the responsibility of the landlords, not a maintenance tenant.

---

## V. THE ENTRY OF DEFAULT JUDGMENT WAS DISPROPORTIONATE TO THE ALLEGED VIOLATIONS

### A. The Sanction Must Be Proportionate to the Violation

Even accepting that some discovery violations occurred, the sanction of default judgment is disproportionate to the offenses established in the record. The Decedent did not engage in spoliation of evidence, did not lie under oath, and did not actively conceal relevant information. His offense was non-appearance at depositions—a violation that, while serious, does not justify the most severe sanction available under the rules.

In *Fiedler v. Fiedler*, 312 Pa. Super. 320, 458 A.2d 982 (1983), this Court stated that "the punishment must fit the crime" and reversed a default judgment where the underlying conduct did not warrant such an extreme remedy. The Court emphasized that default judgment "is a drastic sanction which should be reserved for the most egregious cases of discovery abuse."

### B. The Trial Court Failed to Explain Its Reasoning

The trial court's orders do not adequately explain its reasoning for choosing default judgment over lesser sanctions. In *Bruno v. Pittsburgh*, 557 Pa. 218, 732 A.2d 1251 (1999), the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that trial courts must provide adequate explanations for imposing severe sanctions to allow for meaningful appellate review. The failure to provide such explanation constitutes reversible error.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, and remand this matter for further proceedings. The trial court abused its discretion in entering default judgment against the Decedent, as the plaintiff failed to establish the elements necessary to justify this extreme sanction. Less severe alternatives remained available, the plaintiff cannot demonstrate actual prejudice, and the evidence in the record does not establish that the Decedent was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, Administrator of the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer v. Joseph Michael Angelichio**

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from the orders of the trial court entering judgment against the Defendant, Tyrone Diefenderfer, as a sanction for discovery violations. The Appellant, Michael J. D’Aniello, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, argues that the trial court abused its discretion by imposing the severe sanction of default judgment without first exhausting lesser sanctions and because the record lacks evidence of negligence. After a thorough review of the record, the arguments of counsel, and the applicable precedent, this Court finds that the trial court did not abuse its discretion and that the entry of judgment was an appropriate remedy under the circumstances.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for a trial court’s decision to impose sanctions for discovery violations is abuse of discretion. In *Dilliplaine v. Lehigh Valley Trust Co.*, 457 Pa. 255, 322 A.2d 114 (1974), the Pennsylvania Supreme Court established that while the sanction of default judgment is drastic, it is within the trial court’s sound discretion if the court properly balances the relevant factors. An appellate court will not reverse the decision absent an abuse of that discretion or an error of law.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The *Dilliplaine* Factors Warrant the Sanction of Default Judgment**

In *Dilliplaine*, the Supreme Court articulated five factors that a trial court must consider when determining the appropriate sanction for a discovery violation: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) whether the party acted willfully or in bad faith; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the ability to cure the prejudice; and (5) the importance of the evidence sought. 457 Pa. at 263-64, 322 A.2d at 117-18. Applying these factors to the present case supports the trial court’s ruling.

**1. Nature and Severity of the Violation and Willfulness**

The record reveals a history of noncompliance spanning nearly four years. The Defendant failed to appear for depositions scheduled in August 2007, October 2008, and November 2008. Despite the trial court issuing explicit orders compelling his appearance in July 2009 and August 2010, the Defendant continued to refuse. The violation culminated in January 2011, when the Defendant’s counsel notified the Plaintiff that the Defendant stated, "there is no way I’m getting involved," and that he would not appear "nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled." This explicit refusal to obey a court order constitutes a flagrant violation of the discovery rules. As noted in *Kozak v. Warr*, 402 Pa. Super. 456, 587 A.2d 19, 22 (1991), repeated failures to comply with court-ordered depositions demonstrate a level of noncompliance that justifies severe sanctions. The Defendant’s conduct was not merely negligent; it was a willful and deliberate defiance of the court’s authority.

**2. Prejudice to the Opposing Party and Ability to Cure**

The Defendant was the only other individual present in the residence at the time of the fire. His testimony was critical to establishing the events leading up to the fire, including the origin of the unattended pot on the stove and the placement of the kerosene container. The Defendant provided only an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire. Consequently, the Plaintiff was deprived of the opportunity to cross-examine the Defendant regarding his knowledge and actions.

The prejudice to the Plaintiff is severe and, as of the Defendant’s death in January 2012, incurable. In *In re Estate of Smith*, 435 Pa. Super. 169, 645 A.2d 241, 245 (1994), the court affirmed a dismissal where the non-compliant party’s conduct effectively destroyed the opposing party’s ability to present its case. Here, the Defendant’s persistent refusal to testify, followed by his death, permanently eliminated the Plaintiff’s ability to obtain essential evidence regarding liability.

**3. Importance of the Evidence**

The evidence sought through the deposition was central to the Plaintiff’s claim. The facts indicate the fire started on the stove and spread due to a kerosene container. The Defendant’s account was necessary to determine whether he was responsible for the unattended pot or the defective container. Without this testimony, the Plaintiff cannot prove the essential elements of negligence. As held in *Dilliplaine*, when a party’s conduct prevents the opposing party from obtaining essential evidence, the court may impose the most severe sanctions available. 457 Pa. at 266, 322 A.2d at 119.

**B. The Trial Court Considered Less Severe Sanctions**

The Appellant argues that the trial court failed to consider less severe sanctions before entering default judgment, citing *Smirch v. Oak Ridge Center*, 438 Pa. Super. 351, 652 A.2d 849 (1995). However, the record demonstrates that the trial court attempted to resolve the matter through less severe means on multiple occasions. The court entered two separate orders compelling the Defendant’s appearance—one in July 2009 and another in August 2010. These orders were specific judicial directives designed to compel compliance without terminating the case.

The Defendant’s continued disregard for these orders, culminating in his categorical refusal to participate in January 2011, rendered lesser sanctions futile. In *Kozak*, the Superior Court explained that "when less severe sanctions have been attempted and failed, the court may reasonably conclude that no further attempts would be productive." 402 Pa. Super. at 464, 587 A.2d at 23. Given the Defendant’s explicit statement that he would never appear, the trial court reasonably determined that monetary sanctions or further continuances would be ineffective.

**C. The Plaintiff Established a Prima Facie Case**

The Appellant contends that the Plaintiff failed to establish that the Defendant was negligent, relying on *Wagner v. Pittsburgh-Corning Corp.*, 790 A.2d 724 (Pa. Super. 2001). However, the Plaintiff’s inability to definitively establish negligence is directly attributable to the Defendant’s discovery violations. The facts show the Defendant was present in the home, performed maintenance in exchange for rent, and was the only other occupant when the fire started. The Plaintiff needed the Defendant’s deposition to clarify his role regarding the stove and the kerosene.

The sanction of default judgment is appropriate not because the Plaintiff has already proven negligence, but because the Defendant’s misconduct prevented the Plaintiff from doing so. The purpose of the sanction is to level the playing field when one party has been deprived of the ability to prove their case through no fault of their own. *In re Estate of Smith*, 645 A.2d at 245. The trial court did not err in concluding that the Defendant’s conduct justified the inference of liability necessary to prevent injustice.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court meticulously considered the *Dilliplaine* factors and the history of the litigation. The Defendant’s conduct was willful, the prejudice to the Plaintiff was irreparable, and lesser sanctions had proven futile. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in entering judgment against the Defendant. The orders of the trial court are affirmed.

## INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT

The defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to the plaintiff's arguments in support of the trial court's entry of default judgment. The plaintiff has failed to establish a prima facie case of negligence, and the prejudice claimed is not attributable to any willful misconduct by the defendant. ## I. THE PLAINTIFF FAILED TO ESTABLISH A PRIMA FACIE CASE OF NEGLIGENCE

The fundamental flaw in the plaintiff's case is the complete absence of evidence establishing that Tyrone Diefenderfer was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. The facts explicitly state that the fire originated from "an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove" and that "the cause of the rapid progression and expansion of the fire was determined to be kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove." These facts do not implicate the defendant in any way.

The defendant was a tenant who performed maintenance in exchange for reduced rent. There is no evidence in the record establishing that the defendant was responsible for the unattended pot or that he placed or maintained the defective kerosene container. As this Court held in *Wagner v. Pittsburgh-Corning Corp.*, 790 A.2d 724 (Pa. Super. 2001), the plaintiff bears the burden of establishing each element of negligence, including duty, breach, causation, and damages. The plaintiff cannot meet this burden through speculation and inference.

The plaintiff argues that the defendant's testimony was "critical" to establishing liability. However, the plaintiff cannot bootstrap a finding of negligence merely by asserting that the defendant's testimony would have been beneficial. The plaintiff has the affirmative obligation to produce evidence supporting each element of its claim. As the Superior Court explained in *McNeil v. Owens*, 545 A.2d 883 (Pa. Super. 1988), a default judgment is inappropriate where the plaintiff has not alleged facts sufficient to establish a viable cause of action.

Here, the plaintiff has never alleged or proven that the defendant placed the pot on the stove, left the stove unattended, or was responsible for the defective kerosene container. The mere presence of the defendant in the residence does not give rise to inferential negligence. The plaintiff's claim of prejudice is undermined by its own conduct during the litigation. The record demonstrates that the plaintiff failed to pursue the defendant's deposition with reasonable diligence, contributing to the very prejudice it now complains of.

The plaintiff first requested the defendant's deposition by letter dated June 17, 2007. The defendant failed to appear for a scheduled deposition on August 13, 2007. The plaintiff could have sought to obtain testimony through other means, including the deposition of the state trooper who took the defendant's unsworn statement, inspection of the premises, or expert analysis of the cause and origin of the fire.

As this Court held in *Balent v. NLRB*, 669 A.2d 914 (Pa. Super. 1995), a party cannot claim prejudice from discovery violations when that party's own inaction contributed to the alleged prejudice. The plaintiff cannot now shift responsibility for its own procedural failures onto the defendant's estate.

The plaintiff and the trial court characterized the defendant's conduct as willful and in bad faith. However, the record does not support this characterization. The defendant's failure to appear for depositions was not the product of deliberate defiance but rather resulted from his inability or unwillingness to participate in the litigation.

The facts establish that the defendant's counsel repeatedly represented that he could not locate the defendant. The defendant did not actively evade discovery; rather, he simply refused to participate. This distinction is critical. As this Court explained in *Kozak v. Warr*, 402 Pa. Super. 456, 587 A.2d 19, 22 (1991), the court must distinguish between a party's intentional refusal to comply with discovery orders and a party's mere inability to comply.

The defendant's statement to his counsel that "there is no way I'm getting involved" reflects a lack of willingness to participate, not a willful intent to violate court orders. A party's decision not to testify in a civil matter is protected by constitutional principles, and the court cannot compel testimony over a party's objection. Even accepting the plaintiff's characterization of the defendant's conduct, the sanction of default judgment is disproportionate and unreasonable. The purpose of discovery sanctions is to remedy prejudice to the opposing party, not to punish the non-compliant party.

In *Dilliplaine v. The Court stated that "the sanction must be tailored to serve the intended purpose of the discovery rules." 457 Pa. at 264, 322 A.2d at 118.

Here, the plaintiff has not established any actual damages resulting from the defendant's failure to appear. The plaintiff has not proven that the defendant was negligent, nor has the plaintiff established that the defendant's testimony would have supported the negligence claim. The entry of default judgment effectively imposes liability without any evidentiary basis.

The appropriate sanction, if any, would have been monetary sanctions payable to the plaintiff or the court, or an order precluding the defendant from presenting certain evidence at trial. The entry of default judgment goes beyond what is necessary to remedy any alleged prejudice and effectively determines the merits of the case without any proof of liability.

The trial court erred in failing to consider lesser sanctions before entering default judgment. The record does not demonstrate that the trial court engaged in the required analysis of whether lesser sanctions would adequately address the alleged prejudice.

**Michael J. D’Aniello, Esquire, Administrator of the Estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer v. Joseph Michael Angelichio**

**OPINION OF THE COURT**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the orders of the trial court entering judgment against the Defendant, Tyrone Diefenderfer, as a sanction for discovery violations. The Appellant, Michael J. D’Aniello, administrator of the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer, challenges the trial court’s decision to impose the severe sanction of default judgment. The Appellant argues that the trial court abused its discretion by failing to consider lesser sanctions, that the Plaintiff failed to establish a prima facie case of negligence, and that the Plaintiff’s own lack of diligence contributed to the alleged prejudice. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties and the applicable precedent, this Court affirms the trial court’s orders.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for a trial court’s decision to impose sanctions for discovery violations is abuse of discretion. In *Dilliplaine v. Lehigh Valley Trust Co.*, 457 Pa. 255, 322 A.2d 114 (1974), the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that while the sanction of default judgment is drastic, it is within the trial court’s sound discretion if the court properly balances the relevant factors. An appellate court will not reverse the decision absent an abuse of that discretion or an error of law.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The *Dilliplaine* Factors Support the Sanction**

In *Dilliplaine*, the Supreme Court articulated five factors to guide the determination of appropriate sanctions: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) whether the party acted willfully or in bad faith; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the ability to cure the prejudice; and (5) the importance of the evidence sought. 457 Pa. at 263-64, 322 A.2d at 117-18.

**1. Nature and Severity of the Violation and Willfulness**

The Appellant argues that the Defendant’s refusal to testify was not willful bad faith, characterizing it as the reluctance of an unsophisticated litigant rather than deliberate defiance. We disagree. The record demonstrates a persistent pattern of noncompliance spanning several years. The Defendant failed to appear for depositions in 2007, 2008, and 2009. After the trial court entered explicit orders compelling his appearance in July 2009 and August 2010, the Defendant’s counsel notified the Plaintiff that the Defendant stated, "there is no way I’m getting involved," and that he would not appear "nor any other time the deposition is rescheduled."

This categorical refusal to obey a court order constitutes a flagrant violation of the discovery rules. The Appellant’s reliance on *Kozak v. Warr*, 402 Pa. Super. 456, 587 A.2d 19 (1991), to argue that the Defendant’s refusal was not willful is misplaced. *Kozak* stands for the proposition that repeated failures to comply with court-ordered depositions demonstrate a level of noncompliance that justifies severe sanctions. 402 Pa. Super. at 464, 587 A.2d at 23. The Defendant’s statement was not merely an expression of reluctance; it was a direct challenge to the authority of the trial court. The fact that the Defendant was a layperson does not excuse his willful disobedience of direct court orders.

**2. Prejudice to the Opposing Party and Ability to Cure**

The Appellant contends that the Plaintiff failed to diligently pursue discovery and therefore contributed to the prejudice. Specifically, the Appellant notes the delay between the August 2007 missed deposition and the May 2009 motion for contempt. However, the record indicates that during this period, defense counsel represented that they could not locate the Defendant. The Plaintiff eventually hired a private investigator to locate the Defendant, demonstrating diligence in the face of evasion.

The prejudice to the Plaintiff is severe and, following the Defendant’s death in January 2012, incurable. The Defendant was the only other occupant of the residence at the time of the fire. His testimony was essential to determining the origin of the unattended pot and the placement of the kerosene container. The Defendant provided only an unsworn statement to a state trooper. By refusing to submit to a deposition, the Defendant permanently deprived the Plaintiff of the opportunity to cross-examine him regarding his knowledge and actions. As held in *In re Estate of Smith*, 435 Pa. Super. 169, 645 A.2d 241, 245 (1994), the court affirmed a dismissal where the non-compliant party’s conduct effectively destroyed the opposing party’s ability to present its case. The Appellant’s reliance on *Balent v. NLRB*, 669 A.2d 914 (Pa. Super. 1995), is inapposite because the prejudice here stems directly from the Defendant’s refusal to testify, not the Plaintiff’s procedural timing.

**3. Importance of the Evidence and Prima Facie Case**

The Appellant argues that the Plaintiff failed to establish a prima facie case of negligence because there is no evidence the Defendant caused the fire. The Appellant relies on *Wagner v. Pittsburgh-Corning Corp.*, 790 A.2d 724 (Pa. Super. 2001), and *McNeil v. Owens*, 545 A.2d 883 (Pa. Super. 1988), for the proposition that a default judgment is inappropriate where the plaintiff has not alleged facts sufficient to establish a viable cause of action.

However, the Plaintiff’s inability to definitively establish negligence is directly attributable to the Defendant’s discovery violations. The facts show the Defendant was present in the home, performed maintenance in exchange for rent, and was the only other occupant when the fire started. The Plaintiff needed the Defendant’s deposition to clarify his role regarding the stove and the kerosene. The sanction of default judgment is appropriate not because the Plaintiff has already proven negligence, but because the Defendant’s misconduct prevented the Plaintiff from doing so. The purpose of the sanction is to level the playing field when one party has been deprived of the ability to prove their case through no fault of their own. *In re Estate of Smith*, 645 A.2d at 245. The Appellant’s argument that the Plaintiff could have relied on other evidence, such as the fire marshal’s report, ignores that the Defendant’s personal knowledge was uniquely material to the issue of negligence.

**B. The Trial Court Considered Less Severe Sanctions**

The Appellant argues that the trial court failed to consider less severe sanctions before entering default judgment, citing *Smirch v. Oak Ridge Center*, 438 Pa. Super. 351, 652 A.2d 849 (1995). The record demonstrates otherwise. The trial court entered two separate orders compelling the Defendant’s appearance—one in July 2009 and another in August 2010. These orders were specific judicial directives designed to compel compliance without terminating the case.

The Defendant’s continued disregard for these orders, culminating in his categorical refusal to participate in January 2011, rendered lesser sanctions futile. In *Kozak*, the Superior Court explained that "when less severe sanctions have been attempted and failed, the court may reasonably conclude that no further attempts would be productive." 402 Pa. Super. at 464, 587 A.2d at 23. Given the Defendant’s explicit statement that he would never appear, the trial court reasonably determined that monetary sanctions or further continuances would be ineffective. The Appellant’s suggestion that the court should have attempted monetary sanctions or adverse inference instructions ignores the Defendant’s total refusal to engage in the process.

**C. Proportionality and Due Process**

The Appellant argues that the sanction of default judgment is disproportionate and violates due process because the Defendant was never held in contempt. However, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court has emphasized that the sanction must be tailored to serve the intended purpose of the discovery rules. *Dilliplaine*, 457 Pa. at 264, 322 A.2d at 118. Where, as here, the violation was willful, the prejudice was irreparable, and the evidence was central to the case, the sanction of default judgment is proportionate to the violation. The Defendant was afforded due process through the issuance of court orders, which he willfully violated. Contempt proceedings are not a prerequisite for the imposition of sanctions under Pa. R.C.P. 4019 where the party has been afforded notice and an opportunity to be heard.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court meticulously considered the *Dilliplaine* factors and the history of the litigation. The Defendant’s conduct was willful, the prejudice to the Plaintiff was irreparable, and lesser sanctions had proven futile. The Appellant’s arguments regarding the Plaintiff’s burden of proof and diligence are unpersuasive in light of the Defendant’s egregious discovery violations. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in entering judgment against the Defendant. The orders of the trial court are affirmed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
