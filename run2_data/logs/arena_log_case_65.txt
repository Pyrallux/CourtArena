The officer's pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional under the Fourth Amendment because it was conducted without reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed and dangerous. The Fourth Amendment prohibits warrantless searches and seizures absent specific, articulable facts justifying the intrusion. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21–22 (1968). A pat-down search for weapons is permissible only when an officer has reasonable suspicion that the individual poses an immediate threat to officer safety. *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366, 373 (1993). Here, the officer possessed no objective facts supporting such a suspicion.  

The record shows the defendant was fully compliant, polite, and cooperative throughout the encounter. The officer testified he observed no signs of intoxication, smelled no alcohol or marijuana, and detected no indications of drug use. *Suppression Tr. 28:5–9, 31:5–11, 38:14–17*. He admitted seeing no furtive or suspicious movements and having "no fear for his safety" or "suspicion of any illegality from the initial contact based on defendant’s demeanor." *Suppression Tr. 33:3–19, 34:8–35:13*. Crucially, the officer explicitly acknowledged, "Aside from you not believing the story that he is lost, there’s no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity." *Suppression Tr. 33:16–19*.  

The officer’s subjective belief that the defendant’s "lost" explanation was suspicious—because he claimed to be unfamiliar with a route to Lansdale despite having been there before—does not objectively support a reasonable suspicion of danger. *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119, 124–25 (2000) (holding that "nervous, evasive behavior" alone is insufficient; reasonable suspicion requires "specific and articulable facts" that criminal activity is afoot). The officer’s testimony that defendant’s repeated inquiry about ID procedures was a "ruse" further fails to establish any threat to officer safety. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 271–72 (2000) (rejecting suspicion based solely on "unparticularized suspicion" or "hunches"). Reasonable suspicion must be grounded in objective facts indicating an immediate weapon threat—not mere disagreement with a suspect’s story or perceived deception.  

Moreover, the officer had already completed the traffic stop’s mission by issuing the citation for driving with a suspended license and informing the defendant he was "free to go." *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348, 356–57 (2015) (holding that a traffic stop may not be extended beyond the time reasonably required to handle the original violation without independent reasonable suspicion). At the moment of the pat-down, the officer had no basis to detain the defendant further. The pat-down was therefore an unlawful seizure conducted without the requisite reasonable suspicion of danger.  

The Fourth Amendment requires more than an officer’s "unparticularized suspicion" or "hunch" to justify a weapons frisk. *J.L.*, 529 U.S. at 272. Here, the officer’s actions violated that standard, rendering the pat-down unconstitutional. Any evidence derived from that unlawful search must be suppressed.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE APPELLATE COURT OF PENNSYLVANIA

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

The Commonwealth, through its prosecution, asks this Court to suppress the drug evidence obtained from the search of Defendant's vehicle, arguing that the officer's pat-down search was unconstitutional under the Fourth Amendment. The prosecution contends that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion that the Defendant was armed and dangerous. This defense respectfully submits that the prosecution's argument fails because: (1) the officer possessed reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down for weapons based on the totality of the circumstances; (2) even assuming arguendo that the initial pat-down was improper, the Defendant's voluntary consent to search his vehicle was independently valid and purged any taint from the alleged unlawful pat-down; and (3) the prosecution cannot meet its heavy burden of proving that the search falls within a recognized exception to the warrant requirement. Accordingly, this Court should deny the suppression motion and allow the evidence to be admitted.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts, as found by the trial court and supported by the record, establish the following relevant circumstances surrounding the encounter between Officer Robert M. Coles and the Defendant on March 17, 2012.

The encounter occurred at approximately 3:42 a.m. in Montgomery Township, a jurisdiction divided from neighboring Upper Gwynedd Township by Route 309. The officer, with approximately fifteen years of law enforcement experience, observed the Defendant's vehicle stopped at an intersection in the center lane, well past the marked stop line, "cocked to the right." The Defendant was traveling thirty-five miles per hour in a forty-five mile-per-hour zone. ## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Officer Possessed Reasonable Suspicion to Conduct a Pat-Down Search

The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 9 (1968). While a police officer may briefly stop a individual for investigative purposes if the officer has reasonable suspicion that criminal activity is afoot, the officer may also conduct a pat-down search for weapons if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous. Id. at 21-22; Minnesota v. Dickerson, 508 U.S. 366, 373 (1993). Reasonable suspicion is a "particularized and objective basis" for suspecting criminal activity, viewed through the lens of a reasonable officer. United States v. Cortez, 449 U.S. 411, 417-18 (1981).

This defense submits that the prosecution mischaracterizes the applicable legal standard and ignores the totality of circumstances that supported the officer's reasonable suspicion.

#### 1. The Totality of Circumstances Established Reasonable Suspicion

The prosecution erroneously focuses on isolated facts while ignoring the totality of circumstances confronting the officer. While individual factors might be susceptible to innocent explanation, the combination of facts known to the officer at the time created a reasonable suspicion that the Defendant might be armed and dangerous.

The encounter occurred at 3:42 a.m., a late-night hour when criminal activity is more likely. The Defendant was driving on a suspended license — a criminal offense in Pennsylvania. The Defendant claimed to be "lost" despite having been to Lansdale before, and his explanation for his route was vague. The officer testified that the Defendant's girlfriend was in Lansdale, the Defendant claimed to be coming from Philadelphia, yet he was found in Upper Gwynedd Township — a location that did not align logically with his stated destination. The officer testified that "based on — that his girlfriend lives in Lansdale, he is lost, does not know where he got to, why he was coming out of Upper Gwynedd, I felt that would be somewhat suspicious." This combination of factors — the late hour, the suspended license, the inconsistent travel narrative, and the geographical disorientation — created a reasonable suspicion of criminal activity beyond the traffic violation.

In United States v. Sokolow, 490 U.S. 1, 9-10 (1989), the Supreme Court held that reasonable suspicion can arise from the "collective knowledge" of facts that seem innocent when viewed in isolation but, taken together, warrant further investigation. Here, the officer's fifteen years of experience informed his assessment that the Defendant's story was inconsistent and potentially indicative of criminal behavior. The prosecution's argument that "being lost" is an innocent explanation ignores that the officer was not merely confronted with a lost motorist — he was confronted with a suspended driver who could not explain his presence in a particular location despite having been there before.

#### 2. The Defendant's Behavior During the Encounter Provided Additional Reasonable Suspicion

The prosecution emphasizes the Defendant's compliance and politeness. However, the prosecution conflates compliance with the absence of reasonable suspicion. An individual's demeanor alone does not negate reasonable suspicion when other articulable facts support the suspicion. Illinois v. Wardlow, 528 U.S. 119, 124 (2000).

Critically, after being told he was "free to go" and after his girlfriend was en route to pick him up, the Defendant re-engaged the officer and asked again about the license seizure form — a procedure the officer had just explained "thoroughly." The officer testified that he believed this was a "ruse" and that the Defendant "was being deceptive." While the trial court noted it did not necessarily accept the officer's reasoning, the officer's subjective perception — combined with the objective fact that the Defendant re-initiated contact after being told he was free to leave — contributed to the reasonable suspicion calculus.

The officer also testified that when he asked if there was anything that would concern him, the Defendant "automatically put his hands" on the vehicle and offered to be searched. The officer testified: "I find that unusual." The officer had seen this behavior in the past "when people are trying to be overly cooperative." This observation, while perhaps subject to interpretation, contributed to the officer's reasonable belief that the Defendant might be armed and attempting to conceal weapons.

#### 3. The Prosecution's Reliance on the "Free to Go" Language Is Misplaced

The prosecution argues that because the officer told the Defendant he was "free to go," the pat-down was an unlawful seizure. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the prosecution conflates the pat-down with the vehicle search. The officer asked if he could "ask a question" and the Defendant agreed. The Defendant then voluntarily offered to be searched. This was a consensual encounter, not a seizure. Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991) (holding that a consensual encounter is not a seizure under the Fourth Amendment).

Second, the "free to go" language came after the officer completed the traffic-related business — issuing the citation for driving while suspended. However, the officer's subsequent questions and the Defendant's voluntary response re-initiated a consensual encounter. The officer was not required to ignore evidence of potential criminal activity simply because he had completed the traffic stop. The encounter had not ended — the Defendant was still present, had not yet departed, and voluntarily chose to engage further with the officer.

Third, even if the initial pat-down were somehow improper, the evidence was obtained through the Defendant's subsequent voluntary consent to search his vehicle — a consent that was independently valid.

---

### B. Even If the Pat-Down Were Improper, the Voluntary Consent Purged Any Taint

Even assuming arguendo that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion for the initial pat-down, the Defendant's subsequent voluntary consent to search his vehicle was an independent source of evidence that purged any taint from the alleged unlawful search. This doctrine of attenuation applies where the connection between the unlawful conduct and the discovery of evidence is "so attenuated as to dissipate the taint." Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 487-88 (1963); see also Utah v. Strieff, 579 U.S. 232, 241 (2016) (holding that evidence obtained after an unlawful stop is admissible when there is "a sufficient causal connection" between the unlawful stop and the evidence).

Here, the Defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle. The consent was not the product of any unlawful coercion. The officer did not draw his weapon. The officer had no safety concerns. The Defendant was not under arrest. The Defendant was told he was "free to go." The officer asked if he could "ask a question" and the Defendant agreed. The Defendant then voluntarily offered to be searched, stating "I can search him."

In Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 227 (1973), the Supreme Court held that voluntary consent is a well-established exception to the warrant requirement. The prosecution bears the burden of proving that consent was voluntary, but the Defendant's conduct here — offering to be searched without prompting, after being told he was free to leave — overwhelmingly establishes voluntariness.

The prosecution cannot credibly argue that the Defendant was coerced into consenting when the officer had no weapon drawn, expressed no safety concerns, and the Defendant affirmatively offered to be searched. The Defendant's consent was an independent act of free will that broke any causal chain between any alleged unlawful pat-down and the discovery of evidence in the vehicle.

---

### C. The Prosecution Cannot Meet Its Burden of Proof

The prosecution bears the burden of proving that a search falls within a recognized exception to the warrant requirement. Commonwealth v. McCree, 924 A.2d 621, 627 (Pa. 2007). The prosecution has not met this burden.

The prosecution argues that the pat-down was improper because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion that the Defendant was armed and dangerous. However, as set forth above, the totality of circumstances supported such a suspicion. Alternatively, even if the pat-down were improper, the Defendant's voluntary consent to search his vehicle was an independent basis for the search.

The prosecution's argument rests on the false premise that the officer had absolutely no objective basis for concern. This ignores the late-night hour, the suspended license, the inconsistent travel narrative, the Defendant's re-engagement after being told he was free to go, and the Defendant's unusual behavior in offering to be searched. Viewed objectively through the lens of a reasonable officer with fifteen years of experience, these facts warranted further investigation and supported a reasonable suspicion of danger.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this defense respectfully requests that this Court deny the suppression motion and allow the drug evidence to be admitted. The officer possessed reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down search based on the totality of circumstances, including the late-night hour, the Defendant's suspended license, his inconsistent travel narrative, and his unusual behavior after being told he was free to go. Even if the pat-down were improper, the Defendant's voluntary consent to search his vehicle was an independent basis for the search that purged any taint from any alleged unlawful conduct.

The prosecution cannot meet its burden of proving that the search was unconstitutional. The search was lawful, and the evidence should not be suppressed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The matter before the Court is the Defendant's motion to suppress drug evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The prosecution argues that the search was unconstitutional because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to conduct a pat-down, rendering the subsequent seizure of evidence unlawful. The defense counters that the officer possessed reasonable suspicion based on the totality of the circumstances and, alternatively, that the Defendant’s voluntary consent to search the vehicle purged any taint from prior procedural irregularities. Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented, the motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Traffic Stop Concluded Prior to the Search**

The Fourth Amendment prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 9 (1968). A traffic stop constitutes a seizure, and it "becomes unlawful if it is prolonged beyond the time reasonably required to complete the mission of issuing a ticket for the violation." *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348, 354–55 (2015). Here, the officer’s mission was to address the Defendant’s suspended license. The officer issued the citation, completed the license forfeiture form, and explicitly informed the Defendant that he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 18:3–7, 19:8–10). At this juncture, the investigative detention related to the traffic violation had concluded. The prosecution’s argument that the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to extend the stop is well-taken; however, the record indicates the officer did not extend the stop, but rather, the Defendant re-engaged the officer after being told he was free to leave.

**B. The Encounter Transformed into a Consensual Interaction**

After being told he was free to go, the Defendant re-initiated contact by asking again about the license seizure form. The officer answered the question, and the Defendant began walking back to his car. The officer then asked if he could ask a question, and the Defendant agreed. (Suppression Tr. 21:15–22:25). Under *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991), a police encounter does not constitute a seizure under the Fourth Amendment if, "in view of all of the circumstances surrounding the incident, a reasonable person would have believed that he was free to leave." Here, the officer had already communicated that the Defendant was free to go. The subsequent interaction, initiated by the Defendant and continued with his permission, was consensual rather than a detention. Therefore, the officer was not required to possess reasonable suspicion to ask the Defendant if he had anything that would concern him.

**C. The Defendant Voluntarily Consented to the Search**

The defense correctly argues that the search of the vehicle was predicated on voluntary consent. The record shows that after the officer asked if the Defendant had anything that would concern him, the Defendant "automatically put his hands" on the vehicle and stated, "I can search him." (Suppression Tr. 23:18–24:2). The officer did not draw his weapon, testified he had no safety concerns, and did not employ coercion. (Suppression Tr. 24:3–5, 32:2–4).

Voluntary consent is a well-established exception to the warrant requirement. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 222 (1973). The prosecution bears the burden of proving that consent was involuntary, and it has failed to do so here. The Defendant’s affirmative offer to be searched, made after he was told he was free to leave and while the officer’s weapon was holstered, constitutes a clear act of free will.

**D. The Pat-Down Does Not Taint the Vehicle Search**

The prosecution focuses heavily on the constitutionality of the pat-down, arguing it was unsupported by reasonable suspicion. Even assuming arguendo that the pat-down of the Defendant’s person was improper, the evidence at issue was discovered in the vehicle, not on the Defendant’s person. The search of the vehicle was distinct from the pat-down and was authorized by the Defendant’s consent.

Under the attenuation doctrine, evidence obtained after an unlawful police conduct is admissible if the connection between the illegal conduct and the evidence is sufficiently attenuated. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 487–88 (1963). Here, any taint from an improper pat-down is purged by the intervening act of voluntary consent. The Defendant’s offer to be searched was not the product of the alleged illegality but rather a spontaneous decision made after the traffic stop had effectively ended. See *Utah v. Strieff*, 579 U.S. 232, 241 (2016) (discussing the attenuation of taint through intervening circumstances).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has not demonstrated that the search of the vehicle violated the Defendant’s Fourth Amendment rights. The officer completed the traffic stop and informed the Defendant he was free to go. The subsequent interaction was consensual, culminating in the Defendant’s voluntary offer to be searched. This consent was valid and independently purged any potential taint from prior actions. Therefore, the motion to suppress is denied.

The officer's pat-down of the defendant was unconstitutional under the Fourth Amendment because it lacked reasonable suspicion that the defendant was armed and dangerous. The Fourth Amendment prohibits warrantless searches and seizures absent specific, articulable facts supporting reasonable suspicion. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). A pat-down search for weapons requires "a particularized and objective basis for suspecting the particular person stopped is armed and dangerous." *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366, 373 (1993). This standard demands more than a "hunch" or generalized suspicion of criminal activity; it requires specific facts indicating the presence of a weapon. *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119, 123-24 (2000).

Here, the officer's testimony confirms the absence of any objective facts supporting reasonable suspicion for a weapons pat-down. The officer explicitly admitted that "aside from you not believing the story that he is lost, there's no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity" (Suppression Tr. 33:16-19). He further acknowledged "no signs of defendant’s being drunk, smelled no alcohol, and sensed no issue of intoxication" (Suppression Tr. 28:5-9), "no indicia of intoxication or being under the influence of drugs" (Suppression Tr. 31:5-11), "no burnt or fresh marijuana" (Suppression Tr. 38:14-17), and "no furtive or suspicious movements by defendant during the encounter" (Suppression Tr. 31:22-25, 33:16-19, 42:2-43:3). Critically, the officer testified he had "no fear for his safety or suspicion of any illegality from the initial contact based on defendant’s demeanor" (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19) and explicitly stated he had "no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4). 

The defendant’s suspended license and vague explanation of his route—while potentially indicative of a traffic violation—do not support reasonable suspicion of an armed and dangerous suspect. Suspended driving alone is a non-violent offense that "does not inherently suggest the presence of weapons." *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417-18 (1981). Similarly, a motorist’s claim of being "lost" in a familiar area—without additional corroborating facts—constitutes "an innocent explanation susceptible to non-criminal interpretation." *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 272 (2000) (rejecting anonymous tip as insufficient for frisk where "no basis for believing the tipster was credible or that the informant was describing events he actually observed"). The officer’s subjective belief that the defendant’s story was "suspicious" or a "ruse" (Suppression Tr. 50:17-52:20) is legally insufficient. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27 ("[T]he officer must be able to point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion."). 

The officer’s claim that the defendant’s behavior was "unusual" for "being overly cooperative" (Suppression Tr. 56:21-57:9) is irrelevant to the pat-down analysis because the officer did not articulate how this behavior suggested the presence of a weapon. *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85, 92-93 (1979) (holding that "a search of a person must be supported by probable cause to believe that the person searched is armed and dangerous," and mere presence in a location with criminal activity is insufficient). The defendant’s compliance, politeness, and lack of resistance further undermine any claim of reasonable suspicion. *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 10 (1989) (noting that "cooperative behavior is not evidence of criminal activity").

Because the pat-down was conducted without reasonable suspicion of an armed and dangerous suspect, it violated the Fourth Amendment. Under the exclusionary rule, evidence obtained as a direct result of unconstitutional conduct must be suppressed. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 487-88 (1963). The vehicle search occurred immediately after the unconstitutional pat-down and was part of the same continuous sequence of events initiated by the unlawful intrusion. The evidence discovered in the vehicle is thus "fruit of the poisonous tree" and inadmissible. *Id.* The prosecution bears the burden of proving the search was lawful, *Commonwealth v. McCree*, 924 A.2d 621, 627 (Pa. 2007), but has failed to demonstrate any constitutional basis for the pat-down or the subsequent vehicle search. The motion to suppress must be granted.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes the nature of the encounter and ignores critical facts that establish a consensual interaction independent of any alleged unlawful conduct. The defense respectfully submits that the motion to suppress should be denied because: (1) the search of the vehicle was based on the defendant's voluntary consent, not an unlawful pat-down; (2) the prosecution fails to demonstrate that a Terry pat-down even occurred; (3) even assuming arguendo that any prior illegality occurred, the attenuation doctrine purges any taint from the voluntary consent; and (4) the defendant-initiated interaction transforms the encounter into a consensual one under clearly established precedent.

## II. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S CLAIM THAT A PAT-DOWN OCCURRED WITHOUT REASONABLE SUSPICION

### A. The Record Does Not Establish That a Terry Pat-Down Occurred

The prosecution assumes without adequate foundation that an unconstitutional pat-down search of the defendant's person occurred. A careful review of the record reveals ambiguity regarding whether any physical search of the defendant's person actually took place prior to the vehicle search.

The officer testified that "he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle" and that "after he pats him down, put his hands on the side of the vehicle and states, I can search him." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The phrase "after he pats him down" is ambiguous—it could reflect the defendant's own action of patting himself down, not the officer's. Critically, the officer never testified that he personally conducted a pat-down search of the defendant. The prosecution bears the burden of proving that a search occurred, and the record does not establish that the officer conducted a warrantless pat-down of the defendant's person. *Commonwealth v. McCree*, 924 A.2d 621, 627 (Pa. 2007) (holding that the prosecution bears the burden of proving the search was lawful).

### B. Even If a Pat-Down Occurred, It Was Consensual, Not Compelled

To the extent any physical contact occurred, it resulted from the defendant's own affirmative conduct. The defendant "automatically put his hands" on the vehicle and stated "I can search him." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). This was not a Terry frisk compelled by police authority; it was the defendant's voluntary offer to be searched. A consensual search, even of the person, does not require reasonable suspicion. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991).

The prosecution's reliance on *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), and *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366 (1993), is misplaced because those cases address compelled searches, not consensual encounters. Here, the defendant initiated contact with the officer, asked questions, and voluntarily offered to be searched. The prosecution cannot convert a consensual interaction into a Terry stop simply by reframing the defendant's voluntary conduct as police-directed.

## III. THE ENCOUNTER WAS CONSENSUAL, NOT A SEIZURE

### A. The Defendant Was Free to Leave and Re-Engaged the Officer

The prosecution's entire argument rests on the assumption that the defendant remained seized throughout the encounter. This assumption is contrary to the facts found by the court.

The officer explicitly informed the defendant that he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12). The defendant's girlfriend was en route to pick him up, and he explicitly stated he was not intending to drive anywhere. (Suppression Tr. 19:12-18). Under *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991), a police encounter does not constitute a seizure if "a reasonable person would have believed that he was free to leave." Here, the officer explicitly communicated that the defendant was free to go, and a reasonable person in the defendant's position would have understood this.

The prosecution argues that the defendant's re-engagement with the officer after being told he was free to go somehow transforms the encounter into a continued seizure. This argument fails. The defendant asked a question about the license seizure form, the officer answered it, and the defendant began walking back to his car. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The officer then asked if he could ask a question, and the defendant agreed. (Suppression Tr. 22:1-25). This subsequent interaction was a new, consensual encounter initiated at the officer's request, to which the defendant consented.

### B. The Defendant's Voluntary Agreement to Answer Questions Establishes a Consensual Encounter

The prosecution cannot circumvent the consensual nature of the encounter by ignoring the defendant's affirmative responses. The officer asked if he could ask a question, and "he turned around and came back to me. And he said, yes, I can — he would answer my questions." (Suppression Tr. 22:1-10). The defendant voluntarily agreed to engage with the officer. This is the textbook definition of a consensual encounter. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. at 437.

The prosecution's reliance on *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119 (2000), is misplaced. In that case, the defendant fled from police, establishing reasonable suspicion. Here, the defendant voluntarily approached the officer, not the reverse. The defendant was fully compliant, polite, and respectful throughout the encounter. (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19). His decision to answer the officer's question does not transform a consensual encounter into a seizure.

## IV. THE VEHICLE SEARCH WAS BASED ON VOLUNTARY CONSENT

### A. The Defendant Voluntarily Consented to the Vehicle Search

The prosecution does not meaningfully contest that the defendant consented to the search of his vehicle. The officer asked if the defendant had anything that would concern him, and "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The defendant stated "I can search him." (Suppression Tr. 24:1-2). The officer did not draw his weapon, had no safety concerns, and did not employ coercion. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5, 32:2-4).

Voluntary consent is a well-established exception to the warrant requirement. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 222 (1973). The prosecution bears the burden of proving that consent was involuntary, and it has not met that burden. *Id.* at 222. The defendant's affirmative offer to be searched, made after he was told he was free to leave, while the officer's weapon remained holstered, constitutes a clear act of free will.

The prosecution argues that the defendant's consent was tainted by prior unlawful conduct. However, this argument fails because the defendant was not seized at the time he gave consent. He had been told he was free to go, his ride was en route, and he voluntarily agreed to answer the officer's questions. Under *Florida v. Jardines*, 569 U.S. 1, 6 (2013), a consensual search is valid even if preceded by an unlawful encounter, provided the consent is voluntary.

### B. The Prosecution's Characterization of the Search as "Immediate" Is Factually Incorrect

The prosecution claims that "the vehicle search occurred immediately after the unconstitutional pat-down." This is factually incorrect. Approximately twenty-five minutes elapsed from the initial stop till when the evidence was found. (Suppression Tr. 24-25). During this time, the officer completed the citation, explained the license forfeiture, and engaged in conversation with the defendant. The defendant asked questions, received answers, and voluntarily offered to be searched. This is not an "immediate" search following an alleged illegality; it is a separate, consensual interaction.

## V. THE ATTENUATION DOCTRINE APPLIES

### A. The Defendant's Voluntary Consent Breaks Any Causal Chain

Even assuming arguendo that some prior police conduct was unlawful, the attenuation doctrine applies to purge any taint. Under *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 487-88 (1963), evidence obtained after unlawful police conduct is admissible if the connection between the illegal conduct and the evidence is sufficiently attenuated.

The Supreme Court recently addressed this doctrine in *Utah v. Strieff*, 579 U.S. 232 (2016). In that case, the Court held that evidence obtained following an unlawful stop was admissible because the defendant voluntarily consented to the search. The Court identified three factors: (1) the proximity of the unlawful conduct to the evidence; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the police misconduct. *Id.* at 241.

All three factors favor the defense here. First, significant time elapsed between any alleged illegality and the search—approximately twenty-five minutes. Second, multiple intervening circumstances occurred: the defendant was told he was free to go; his girlfriend was en route; he voluntarily re-engaged the officer; he asked questions; and he voluntarily offered to be searched. Third, there was no flagrant misconduct. The officer conducted a lawful traffic investigation, completed his tasks, and informed the defendant he was free to go. The subsequent interaction was consensual.

### B. The Defendant's Voluntary Re-Engagement Is an Independent Intervening Factor

The prosecution ignores the significance of the defendant's decision to re-engage the officer after being told he was free to go. The defendant asked a question, received an answer, and then agreed to answer the officer's question. This volitional act constitutes an independent source that breaks any causal chain between any alleged prior illegality and the evidence. *Utah v. Strieff*, 579 U.S. at 241-42.

The prosecution's reliance on *Wong Sun* is misplaced. In that case, the Court found that the defendant's statement was fruit of the poisonous tree because it was obtained while the defendant was unlawfully detained. Here, the defendant was not detained when he consented to the search. He was free to leave, had a ride coming, and voluntarily agreed to answer questions and be searched.

## VI. PROSECUTION FAILS TO ESTABLISH THAT THE SEARCH WAS UNCONSTITUTIONAL

### A. The Prosecution Bears the Burden of Proof

The prosecution bears the burden of proving that the search was unlawful. *Commonwealth v. McCree*, 924 A.2d 621, 627 (Pa. 2007). The prosecution has failed to meet this burden because it has not established that: (1) a Terry pat-down of the defendant occurred; (2) the defendant was seized at the time of the search; or (3) the defendant's consent was involuntary.

### B. The Prosecution's Arguments Rely on Speculation

The prosecution's entire argument rests on the assumption that the pat-down was unconstitutional. However, the prosecution has not established that a pat-down occurred, let alone that it was unconstitutional. The officer's testimony establishes that the defendant offered to be searched, not that the officer conducted a compelled pat-down. The prosecution's argument is based on speculation, not evidence in the record.

## VII. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court deny the prosecution's motion to suppress. The evidence establishes that: (1) the defendant was told he was free to go; (2) the defendant voluntarily re-engaged the officer; (3) the defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his vehicle; and (4) any prior alleged illegality was attenuated by the defendant's voluntary consent. The prosecution has not met its burden of proving that the search was unconstitutional.

The motion to suppress should be denied.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on the Defendant's motion to suppress drug evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The Court has reviewed the rebuttal arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The prosecution argues that the evidence must be suppressed as "fruit of the poisonous tree" resulting from an unconstitutional pat-down. The defense counters that the search was conducted pursuant to voluntary consent obtained during a consensual encounter that was distinct from, and subsequent to, the conclusion of the traffic stop. Based on the facts provided and the applicable law, the motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Pat-Down Was Likely Unsupported by Reasonable Suspicion**

The Court agrees with the prosecution that the record indicates the officer conducted a pat-down of the Defendant without reasonable suspicion. The officer testified that he "patted [the Defendant] down" and found nothing. (Suppression Tr. 56:5-14). A *Terry* frisk requires "a particularized and objective basis for suspecting the particular person stopped is armed and dangerous." *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366, 373 (1993). Here, the officer explicitly admitted he had "no fear for his safety," observed "no furtive or suspicious movements," and saw no indicia of criminal activity other than the Defendant's claim of being lost. (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19, 31:22-25). A hunch based on a motorist being "lost" or "overly cooperative" is insufficient to justify a weapons search. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 272 (2000); *Sokolow*, 490 U.S. at 7. Therefore, the pat-down of the Defendant's person was likely unconstitutional under the Fourth Amendment.

**B. The Illegality of the Pat-Down Did Not Taint the Subsequent Vehicle Search**

While the prosecution correctly identifies the pat-down as unlawful, it fails to demonstrate that the evidence discovered in the vehicle is the fruit of that illegality. The critical flaw in the prosecution's argument is its characterization of the entire encounter as a single, continuous seizure. The record clearly establishes that the traffic investigation concluded, and the seizure ended, before the consent to search the vehicle was given.

**1. The Traffic Stop Concluded and the Defendant Was "Free to Go"**

The Fourth Amendment permits an officer to detain a motorist only "for the time necessary to complete the mission of the stop." *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348, 354 (2015). Here, the officer's mission was to address the Defendant's suspended license. The officer completed the necessary paperwork, issued the citation, and explained the license forfeiture procedures. Crucially, the officer testified that he told the Defendant he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12). At this moment, the investigative detention terminated. *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 40-41 (1996) (holding that the Fourth Amendment does not require police to advise a suspect that he is free to leave before a consensual conversation can occur, but here the officer did so explicitly).

**2. The Defendant Re-Engaged the Officer in a Consensual Encounter**

After being told he was free to go, the Defendant did not leave. Instead, he waited for his girlfriend and re-engaged the officer by asking a question about the license seizure form. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The officer answered, and the Defendant began walking back to his car. The officer then asked if he could ask a question, and the Defendant voluntarily agreed. A police encounter is consensual, and does not constitute a seizure, if "a reasonable person would have believed that he was free to leave." *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991). Given that the officer had just told the Defendant he was free to go, and the Defendant voluntarily initiated further contact, the subsequent interaction was consensual.

**3. The Defendant's Consent Was Voluntary and Attenuated from the Prior Illegality**

The search of the vehicle was conducted based on the Defendant's consent. After the officer asked if the Defendant had anything that would concern him, the Defendant "automatically put his hands" on the vehicle and stated, "I can search him." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The officer did not draw his weapon and testified he had no safety concerns. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5). These facts support a finding of voluntary consent. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 222 (1973).

Even assuming the pat-down was unconstitutional, the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine does not require suppression here. Under the attenuation doctrine, evidence obtained after unlawful police conduct is admissible if the connection between the illegal conduct and the evidence is sufficiently attenuated. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 487-88 (1963). The Supreme Court in *Utah v. Strieff*, 579 U.S. 232, 241 (2016), identified three factors relevant to this analysis: (1) the temporal proximity of the illegality to the evidence; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct.

All three factors favor the admission of the evidence. First, there was a significant break in time and events between the pat-down and the vehicle search. The officer completed the traffic stop, informed the Defendant he was free to go, and engaged in a separate conversation. Second, the "free to go" statement and the Defendant's decision to re-engage the officer were intervening circumstances that broke the causal chain. The Defendant was not detained when he consented; he was free to leave and chose to stay. Third, the officer's misconduct, if any, was not flagrant. The officer lawfully stopped the vehicle to investigate a suspended license and conducted a routine check. The pat-down, while unjustified, was not the product of harassment or systemic abuse.

Because the Defendant's consent was given during a consensual encounter that followed the termination of the seizure, it was an independent act of free will that purged any taint from the prior pat-down. *Strieff*, 579 U.S. at 240-41.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has failed to establish that the evidence discovered in the vehicle must be suppressed. While the officer may have lacked reasonable suspicion to pat-down the Defendant, that illegality did not taint the subsequent vehicle search. The officer completed the traffic stop, told the Defendant he was free to go, and obtained voluntary consent during a consensual encounter initiated by the Defendant. Under the attenuation doctrine, the connection between the unlawful pat-down and the evidence is too attenuated to warrant suppression. Accordingly, the motion to suppress is **DENIED**.

## I. THE PAT-DOWN WAS UNCONSTITUTIONAL

The prosecution convincingly established—and this Court acknowledged—that the officer conducted a pat-down of the Defendant without reasonable suspicion. This finding is amply supported by the record.

Under *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), and its progeny, a frisk for weapons requires "a particularized and objective basis for suspecting the particular person stopped is armed and dangerous." *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366, 373 (1993). The officer himself testified that he had "no fear for his safety" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4, 33:3-19), observed "no furtive or suspicious movements" (Suppression Tr. 31:22-25, 33:16-19, 42:2-43:3), and detected no indicia of intoxication or drug influence (Suppression Tr. 31:5-11, 44:21-45:15). The officer's sole articulated basis for the pat-down was the Defendant's claim of being "lost" and his "overly cooperative" demeanor. As *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 272 (2000), held, "a tip from an anonymous informant that a young black male standing at a particular bus stop and carrying a gun" is insufficient without corroboration. Here, the officer had even less—a motorist's statement that he was lost in an area he had visited before, which the officer himself acknowledged might not be suspicious in other contexts (Suppression Tr. 29:22-30:2). ## II. THE VEHICLE SEARCH WAS NOT SUFFICIENTLY ATTENUATED FROM THE UNCONSTITUTIONAL PAT-DOWN

The Court's ruling rests on the attenuation doctrine as articulated in *Utah v. Strieff*, 579 U.S. 232 (2016), and *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). The Defense submits that the Court erred in its application of this doctrine by failing to recognize that the "consensual encounter" was a continuation of the same unlawful seizure, not an intervening circumstance sufficient to break the causal chain.

### A. The Defendant Was Not Free to Leave

The Court's finding that the Defendant was "free to go" ignores the practical reality of the encounter. While the officer testified that he told the Defendant he was "free to go" (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12), this statement was hollow under the circumstances. The Defendant was stranded on the shoulder of a road at 3:42 in the morning, his driver's license had just been seized, his car was about to be towed, and he was waiting for his girlfriend to pick him up. He had no means of leaving the scene independently.

Moreover, two police vehicles with rotating lights were positioned behind him (Suppression Tr. 13, 20:7-24, 48:19-21). The dashboard camera was activated and videotaping the entire interaction (Suppression Tr. 48:22-49:8). A backup officer had arrived and was standing along the curb (Suppression Tr. 20:20-24). Under these circumstances, a reasonable person would not have believed he was truly free to leave. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991) (holding that a seizure occurs when, considering all the circumstances, a reasonable person would not feel free to leave).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the "free to go" analysis is objective, not subjective. In *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 40-41 (1996), the Court acknowledged that police need not explicitly tell a suspect he is free to leave before a consensual conversation. However, the Court in *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348, 354 (2015), made clear that an officer may not prolong a traffic stop for unrelated investigations. Here, the officer extended the encounter not for traffic purposes but to investigate his suspicion that the Defendant was transporting contraband from Philadelphia—a purpose wholly unrelated to the license check.

### B. The Officer Initiated the Subsequent Interaction

Even accepting that the traffic stop technically concluded, the subsequent interaction was not a truly consensual encounter initiated by the Defendant. After being told he was free to go, the Defendant asked a single question about the license seizure form (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The officer answered, and the Defendant began walking back to his car. It was the officer who then "asked [defendant] if I could ask him a question" (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The Defendant did not approach the officer seeking to engage in further conversation; the officer initiated the further questioning.

This distinction is critical. A consensual encounter requires that the citizen initiate the contact or voluntarily agree to further police questioning after being fully informed of his right to refuse. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. at 437. Here, the officer asked to ask a question, and the Defendant—being polite, cooperative, and stranded—agreed. This is not the stuff of truly voluntary consent.

### C. The Attenuation Factors Do Not Favor the Prosecution

The Court applied the *Strieff* factors and found they favored admission of the evidence. The Defense respectfully submits that this analysis was flawed.

First, regarding temporal proximity: Approximately twenty-five minutes elapsed from the initial stop till when the evidence was found (Suppression Tr. 24-25). However, the pat-down and the vehicle search were separated by only minutes, with the vehicle search occurring immediately after the officer's questioning. The *Strieff* Court emphasized that temporal proximity alone is insufficient; there must be meaningful intervening circumstances. 579 U.S. at 241.

Second, regarding intervening circumstances: The Court found that the "free to go" statement and the Defendant's decision to re-engage the officer were intervening circumstances. But as argued above, the Defendant was not truly free to leave. He was stranded, his car was about to be seized, and he was waiting for a ride. The presence of a second officer and the rotating lights further undermined any claim that the Defendant felt free to depart. These circumstances do not constitute meaningful intervention sufficient to break the causal chain.

Third, regarding the purpose and flagrancy of the misconduct: The Court characterized the officer's conduct as "not flagrant." However, the officer deliberately conducted a pat-down without reasonable suspicion based solely on a hunch that the Defendant was lost and "overly cooperative." He then used the information obtained from that unlawful pat-down to justify asking for consent to search the vehicle. This is precisely the type of police conduct the exclusionary rule was designed to deter—manufacturing suspicion through unlawful intrusions and then exploiting that manufactured suspicion to justify further searches.

### D. The Search Was the Fruit of the Unlawful Pat-Down

The causal connection between the pat-down and the vehicle search cannot be severed. The officer testified that after the pat-down, he asked the Defendant if he had "anything that would concern" him, and then proceeded to search the car (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25, 23:18-24:2). The sequence is not coincidental—it is causation.

The officer's own testimony establishes that his suspicion about the vehicle arose from the pat-down and the Defendant's response to his questions. When asked by the Court what the basis of his "reasonable suspicion" was, the officer answered:

> "Again, where he was — where he was at, where he was coming from. Again, after I completely explained what he needed to do with his license, he re-engages me with that. ## III. THE CONSENT WAS NOT VOLUNTARY

Even if the Court finds that the encounter was technically consensual, the consent was not voluntary. The Defendant was stranded on a roadside at 3:42 AM, surrounded by police lights and two officers, his license had just been seized, and he was awaiting a ride. Under *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 222 (1973), consent must be voluntarily given, and the prosecution bears the burden of proving voluntariness by a preponderance of the evidence. The coercive environment here undermines any claim of voluntary consent.

To allow this evidence would invite law enforcement to circumvent constitutional safeguards by conducting illegal searches first and then claiming "consent" afterward. That is precisely the abuse the exclusionary rule was designed to prevent.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on the Defendant's motion to suppress drug evidence discovered during a search of his vehicle. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by counsel. The arguments presented, particularly those addressing the coercive nature of the encounter and the application of the attenuation doctrine, necessitate a revision of the Court's preliminary findings. Based on the facts provided and the applicable law, the motion to suppress is **GRANTED**.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Pat-Down Was Unconstitutional**

The Court reaffirms its finding that the officer's pat-down of the Defendant violated the Fourth Amendment. A *Terry* frisk requires "a particularized and objective basis for suspecting the particular person stopped is armed and dangerous." *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). The officer testified that he had "no fear for his safety," observed "no furtive or suspicious movements," and detected no indicia of criminal activity. (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19, 31:22-25). The officer's subjective suspicion that the Defendant was "lost" or "overly cooperative" is insufficient to justify a weapons search. *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266, 272 (2000). Therefore, the pat-down was unlawful.

**B. The "Free to Go" Statement Did Not Terminate the Seizure**

The central issue is whether the vehicle search was purged of the taint from the unlawful pat-down. The Court previously held that the officer's statement that the Defendant was "free to go" terminated the seizure, rendering the subsequent interaction consensual. Upon further review, the Court finds this conclusion was in error.

While the officer testified that he told the Defendant he was "free to go," (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10, 21:5-12), the Fourth Amendment inquiry is objective: whether a reasonable person would have believed he was free to leave. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 437 (1991). Here, the Defendant was stranded on the shoulder of a road at 3:42 a.m. His driver's license had been seized, and he was waiting for his girlfriend to pick him up. He had no practical means to leave the scene. Furthermore, two police vehicles with rotating lights were positioned behind him, and a backup officer was present on the curb. (Suppression Tr. 13, 20:7-24, 48:19-21).

Under these circumstances, a reasonable person in the Defendant's position would not have felt free to terminate the encounter and walk away, despite the officer's verbal statement. The coercive environment—the late hour, the show of authority by two officers, and the Defendant's dependency on a ride—negated the possibility of a truly consensual encounter. *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 40-41 (1996) (recognizing that the "free to leave" inquiry is based on the totality of the circumstances). Consequently, the seizure continued beyond the completion of the traffic citation.

**C. The Search Was Not Sufficiently Attenuated from the Illegality**

Because the seizure continued, the evidence discovered in the vehicle must be suppressed unless it is sufficiently attenuated from the unlawful pat-down. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 487-88 (1963). The Court applies the three-factor test from *Utah v. Strieff*, 579 U.S. 232, 241 (2016): (1) temporal proximity; (2) intervening circumstances; and (3) purpose and flagrancy of the misconduct.

First, the temporal proximity between the unlawful pat-down and the vehicle search is close. The officer conducted the pat-down, found nothing, and immediately asked to search the vehicle. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25, 23:18-24:2). There was no significant break in the interaction to dissipate the taint of the initial illegality.

Second, there were no intervening circumstances sufficient to break the causal chain. The State relies on the "free to go" statement and the Defendant's re-engagement of the officer. However, as established above, the "free to go" statement was ineffective given the coercive context. Furthermore, it was the officer who re-initiated the questioning after the Defendant asked about the license seizure form. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). This re-initiation by the officer, while the Defendant was effectively detained, does not constitute an independent act of free will by the Defendant. Unlike the outstanding warrant in *Strieff*, which was a "neutral intervening circumstance" discovered by chance, 579 U.S. at 240-41, the circumstances here were manufactured by the officer's continued detention of the Defendant.

Third, the purpose and flagrancy of the misconduct weigh in favor of suppression. The officer prolonged the traffic stop to investigate a hunch that the Defendant was transporting drugs, despite having already completed the mission of the stop (issuing the citation for the suspended license). *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348, 354 (2015). The officer's decision to pat-down the Defendant without reasonable suspicion, and then to use that encounter to pivot to a drug search, was an exploitation of the unlawful detention.

**D. The Consent Was Not Voluntary**

Finally, the State argues that the search was valid because the Defendant consented. However, consent given during an unlawful detention is not voluntary unless it is the product of a "free and deliberate choice" rather than a mere acquiescence to a claim of lawful authority. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 222 (1973). Given that the Defendant was not free to leave and was subjected to the coercive presence of two officers, his consent to search the vehicle was not truly voluntary. The officer did not advise the Defendant that he could refuse the search, and the Defendant's "agreeable" demeanor (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18) cannot override the objective reality of the detention.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The evidence discovered in the Defendant's vehicle was the fruit of an unconstitutional pat-down and an unlawful seizure. The "free to go" statement did not terminate the encounter under the objective standard of *Florida v. Bostick*, and no intervening circumstances broke the causal chain between the illegality and the discovery of the drugs. The officer's conduct violated the Defendant's Fourth Amendment rights, and the exclusionary rule requires that the evidence be suppressed.
