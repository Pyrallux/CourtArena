## I. THE EXISTENCE OF A LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE DOES NOT AUTOMATICALLY PRECLUDE INJUNCTIVE RELIEF

The appellant relies heavily on *C. L. M. Enterprises, Inc. v. Dorn*, 384 So. 2d 902 (Fla. 3d DCA 1980), for the proposition that a liquidated damages clause conclusively negates irreparable harm. This reading of *Dorn* is overbroad and misstates Florida law. In *Dorn*, the court held only that the existence of a liquidated damages provision *may* preclude a finding of irreparable harm when the provision represents a reasonable estimate of anticipated damages. The court did not hold that a liquidated damages clause automatically bars injunctive relief in all circumstances.

Florida courts have consistently recognized that injunctive relief remains available even when a contract contains liquidated damages provisions, particularly where the nature of the harm is inherently difficult to quantify or where monetary damages would prove inadequate to protect the employer's legitimate business interests. See *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814 (Fla. 1964) (holding that irreparable harm may exist despite contractual remedies where the harm involves loss of goodwill, customer relationships, and competitive position). The loss of customer relationships and the erosion of a business's market position—harm that is precisely what noncompetition agreements are designed to prevent—are inherently difficult to value and may constitute irreparable harm despite the presence of a liquidated damages clause.

Moreover, the appellant entirely ignores the factual context surrounding the execution of the employment agreement. The liquidated damages clause was included as a supplementary remedy, not as the exclusive remedy available to the employer. The agreement itself contemplates injunctive relief as an enforcement mechanism, as evidenced by the covenant's explicit provisions regarding enforcement. Under Florida law, when a contract expressly provides for both liquidated damages and injunctive relief, the parties have manifested an intent that these remedies be cumulative rather than mutually exclusive. See *Wells v. Ball*, 475 So. 2d 1002 (Fla. 5th DCA 1985) (holding that contractual provisions for injunctive relief are enforceable despite the presence of damage provisions).

The trial court properly exercised its discretion in finding that the employer would suffer irreparable harm in the absence of injunctive relief. The harm here involves the misappropriation of customer relationships developed over years of business development—a harm that monetary damages alone cannot adequately remedy. The appellant cite to *Dorn* for a proposition that case does not support, and this Court should reject his attempt to extend *Dorn* beyond its holding.

---

## II. THE EMPLOYER PRESENTED SUFFICIENT EVIDENCE TO ESTABLISH A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS

The appellant argues that the employer failed to prove a likelihood of success because it did not present evidence that he specifically solicited "active, paid up customers." This argument misapprehends the applicable legal standard and ignores the factual record presented to the trial court.

At the temporary injunction stage, the employer need not prove a breach of the agreement with the certainty required at trial. Rather, the employer need only demonstrate a likelihood of success—i.e., that it will probably succeed on the merits. The employer's complaint, verified under oath, alleged that the appellant went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, and was selling blood plasma products in the same market territory previously served by the appellant. This allegation, if proven at trial, establishes a prima facie violation of the noncompetition agreement.

The appellant relies on *American Medical Systems, Inc. v. Grinstead*, 567 So. 2d 937 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990), for the proposition that "mere allegation of employment with a competitor is insufficient." However, the appellant misreads *Grinstead*. In that case, the court found the evidence insufficient because the employee merely began working for a competitor without any evidence that she solicited any of the former employer's customers. Here, by contrast, the employer has alleged not merely that the appellant began working for a competitor, but that he is actively selling blood plasma products in the same market—sales that, by their nature, necessarily involve competition for customers.

The noncompetition agreement prohibits the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." The term "solicitation" must be given its ordinary meaning in the commercial context. Solicitation includes any effort to induce a customer to purchase products from a competing supplier. When a salesperson leaves an employer and begins selling the same products to the same customer base for a competitor, such activity constitutes solicitation within the meaning of the agreement, regardless of whether formal advertising or direct contact can be proven at this stage.

Furthermore, the appellant ignores the factual allegations regarding his departure. The employer has alleged that the appellant resigned voluntarily and then immediately went to work for a direct competitor, Bio-Med, Inc. This sequence of events—resignation followed by immediate employment with a competitor selling the same products—creates a reasonable inference that the appellant is targeting the employer's customer base. The trial court was entitled to draw this inference at the preliminary injunction stage.

The appellant also overlooks the confidential nature of the customer information to which he had access. As a salesperson for the employer's blood plasma products, the appellant necessarily had access to information regarding the employer's customers, their purchasing habits, their contact information, and their needs. The noncompetition agreement specifically protects the confidentiality of such information. The employer's allegation that the appellant is now selling blood plasma products for a competitor gives rise to a reasonable inference that he is using this confidential information to the employer's detriment—a violation of both the noncompetition clause and the confidentiality provisions of the agreement.

The trial court properly found that the employer demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits. This finding should not be disturbed on appeal.

## III. THE INJUNCTION IS PROPERLY TAILORED TO PROTECT THE EMPLOYER'S LEGITIMATE BUSINESS INTERESTS

The appellant argues that the injunction is overbroad because it prohibits him from working for Bio-Med altogether rather than restricting only the solicitation of protected customers. This argument fundamentally misconstrues the nature of the injunction and the agreement it enforces.

The noncompetition agreement prohibits the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers" for a period of two years following termination. The trial court's injunction enforces this contractual provision—it does not expand the scope of the agreement. The injunction prevents the appellant from violating the agreement during the two-year period, which is exactly what the parties contractually agreed to.

The appellant cites *Sarasota County v. City of Venice*, 243 So. 2d 194 (Fla. 1971), for the proposition that injunctions must be "precise and limited to the particular wrong sought to be prevented." This principle supports, rather than undermines, the injunction here. The wrong sought to be prevented is the solicitation of the employer's active, paid up customers. The injunction is precisely tailored to prevent this wrong. It does not prohibit the appellant from working in the blood plasma industry generally; it only prevents him from soliciting the employer's customers during the two-year period specified in the agreement.

The appellant argues that the injunction effectively bars him from earning a living in his field. This argument, while sympathetic, misstates the scope of the injunction. The appellant remains free to work in the blood plasma industry. He is merely prohibited from competing for the employer's specific customers during the two-year period. This is precisely the type of restriction that Florida courts have upheld as reasonable and necessary to protect legitimate business interests. See *United States v. Joyce*, 271 F.3d 1300 (11th Cir. 2001) (applying Florida law and upholding noncompetition agreements that restrict solicitation of specific customers for reasonable time periods).

The appellant also ignores the factual context surrounding his departure from the employer. He voluntarily resigned from his position and then immediately went to work for a direct competitor. He did not seek employment in a different industry or in a different capacity. His decision to go to work for Bio-Med, Inc., and sell blood plasma products—precisely the products he sold for the employer—creates a strong presumption that he intends to compete for the employer's customers. The injunction simply prevents him from carrying out this intention during the two-year restricted period.

## IV. THE APPELLANT'S EQUITABLE DEFENSES ARE WITHOUT MERIT

The appellant suggests that he was "forced to resign" after refusing to engage in improper business practices. Even accepting this allegation as true for purposes of the temporary injunction, it does not provide a defense to the enforcement of the noncompetition agreement.

First, the appellant voluntarily entered into the employment agreement containing the noncompetition covenant. He is bound by its terms unless he can demonstrate that the agreement is unenforceable due to fraud, duress, or overreaching. The mere fact that he later disagreed with certain business decisions of his employer does not render the agreement void or unenforceable.

Second, the appellant has not alleged that the noncompetition agreement itself was procured through fraud or duress. His allegations regarding his resignation concern events that occurred after the execution of the agreement. The validity of the noncompetition agreement must be determined as of the time of its execution, not as of the time of his resignation.

Third, even if the appellant had a claim against his employer for wrongful termination or for ordering him to engage in improper conduct, such a claim is separate from the employer's claim for enforcement of the noncompetition agreement. The appellant may pursue such claims in a separate action while still being bound by the terms of his employment agreement.

The appellant has failed to demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion in granting injunctive relief. The existence of a liquidated damages clause does not automatically preclude injunctive relief where, as here, the harm is inherently difficult to quantify and monetary damages would prove inadequate. The employer presented sufficient evidence to establish a likelihood of success on the merits, and the injunction is properly tailored to protect the employer's legitimate business interests.

The appellant entered into a valid employment agreement containing reasonable restrictions on his ability to compete. He cannot now escape the consequences of that agreement by simply going to work for a competitor and selling the same products to the same customers. The trial court properly enforced the agreement, and its order should be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant, v. HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee.**

**CASE NO.** [Unknown]
**DISTRICT COURT OF APPEAL OF FLORIDA**

**OPINION**

This case comes before the Court on an appeal from a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. The Appellant, Martin J. Bradley, III, appeals the trial court’s order granting the injunction, arguing that the Appellee, Health Coalition, Inc., failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits and the existence of irreparable harm. After reviewing the arguments presented by both parties, we find that the trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. Accordingly, we reverse the order and remand for further proceedings.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

A trial court’s decision to grant a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. See *Fla. Power & Light Co. v. City of Lake Worth*, 227 So. 2d 814, 816 (Fla. 4th DCA 1969). To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that the moving party will suffer irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the moving party has no adequate remedy at law; and (4) that the injunction would serve the public interest. See *Murphy v. Inlet Oceanfront Resort, Inc.*, 969 So. 2d 283, 286 (Fla. 4th DCA 2007).

**II. LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS**

The primary issue on appeal is whether Health Coalition demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits of its claim that Bradley violated the noncompetition agreement. The agreement specifically prohibits the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." It does not contain a broad prohibition against working for a competitor or engaging in the sale of blood plasma products generally.

Health Coalition’s complaint alleges that Bradley "went to work for a competing company, Bio-Med, Inc., and was selling blood plasma products." However, the complaint fails to allege that Bradley solicited any of Health Coalition’s "active, paid up customers." As this Court has previously held, the mere allegation of employment with a competitor is insufficient to prove a violation of a noncompetition agreement that is limited to the solicitation of specific customers. See *American Medical Systems, Inc. v. Grinstead*, 567 So. 2d 937, 939 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990).

Health Coalition argues that Bradley’s act of selling products in the same market territory creates an inference that he is soliciting the employer’s customers. While this inference might be permissible at a full trial with a complete evidentiary record, it is insufficient to establish a *likelihood of success on the merits* at the preliminary injunction stage where the burden rests on the employer. The covenant’s plain language restricts only the solicitation of specific customers. Because Health Coalition failed to present evidence that Bradley targeted or solicited these specific protected customers, it cannot demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits of its breach of contract claim.

**III. IRREPARABLE HARM AND THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE**

Furthermore, the trial court erred in finding irreparable harm without addressing the impact of the liquidated damages clause contained in the employment agreement. The existence of a valid liquidated damages clause is a significant factor in the irreparable harm analysis. As held in *C. L. M. Enterprises, Inc. v. Dorn*, 384 So. 2d 902, 904 (Fla. 3d DCA 1980), "the existence of a liquidated damages provision precludes a finding of irreparable harm" unless the party seeking the injunction can demonstrate that monetary damages would be inadequate.

Health Coalition argues that the loss of customer goodwill and competitive position constitutes harm that is inherently difficult to quantify, citing *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814 (Fla. 1964). While we acknowledge that injunctive relief may be available in such circumstances, the presence of a liquidated damages clause shifts the burden to the employer to affirmatively demonstrate why those damages are inadequate to remedy the harm. Health Coalition failed to meet this burden. The trial court’s order did not address the controlling precedent of *C. L. M. Enterprises* or explain why the liquidated damages clause was insufficient to make Health Coalition whole. This omission constitutes reversible error.

**IV. SCOPE OF THE INJUNCTION**

Finally, the temporary injunction appears to be overbroad. Florida law requires that injunctions be "precise and limited to the particular wrong sought to be prevented." *Sarasota County v. City of Venice*, 243 So. 2d 194, 196 (Fla. 1971). Because the noncompetition agreement is limited to the solicitation of "active, paid up customers," any injunction must be similarly limited. An order that broadly prohibits Bradley from working for Bio-Med, Inc., without restricting the prohibition to the solicitation of the specific protected customers, exceeds the scope of the contractual agreement and constitutes an abuse of discretion.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, we conclude that Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits and failed to sufficiently establish irreparable harm given the existence of a liquidated damages clause. The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. Therefore, we reverse the order granting the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

**DONE and ORDERED.**

Your Honors, the Appellant respectfully submits that the Defense’s opening statement fundamentally misapplies Florida law, ignores binding precedent, and mischaracterizes the trial court’s error in granting the temporary injunction. The Defense’s arguments collapse under the precise legal standards governing temporary injunctive relief, as established by this Court and Florida precedent. The trial court abused its discretion by granting the injunction without satisfying the four essential elements required for such relief. Each of the Defense’s assertions is legally unsound, and this Court should affirm the reversal of the injunction.  

### I. THE DEFENSE MISSTATES THE EFFECT OF THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE ON IRREPARABLE HARM  

The Defense claims that *C.L.M. Enterprises, Inc. v. Dorn*, 384 So. 2d 902 (Fla. 3d DCA 1980), does not "conclusively negate" irreparable harm and that liquidated damages clauses are merely "supplementary remedies." This is a profound misreading of Florida law. *Dorn* explicitly holds that **"the existence of a liquidated damages provision precludes a finding of irreparable harm"** unless the moving party affirmatively demonstrates that monetary damages are inadequate. 384 So. 2d at 904. The Defense cites *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814 (Fla. 1964), to argue that loss of goodwill constitutes irreparable harm despite a liquidated damages clause. But *Davis* is inapposite because it involved **no liquidated damages clause whatsoever**. Florida courts have consistently distinguished *Davis* in cases where a valid liquidated damages clause exists. As this Court held in *Fla. Power & Light Co. v. City of Lake Worth*, 227 So. 2d 814, 816 (Fla. 4th DCA 1969), the presence of a liquidated damages clause shifts the burden to the employer to prove monetary damages are inadequate—a burden Health Coalition wholly failed to meet.  

The Defense also misquotes *Wells v. Ball*, 475 So. 2d 1002 (Fla. 5th DCA 1985), by claiming it supports "cumulative remedies." But *Wells* expressly states that a liquidated damages clause is enforceable **only if it is a "reasonable forecast of just compensation for the harm caused."** 475 So. 2d at 1005. Here, Health Coalition presented **no evidence** that the liquidated damages clause was reasonable or that it failed to cover the alleged harm. Indeed, the clause was designed precisely to address the type of commercial harm alleged—loss of customer relationships—making monetary compensation the exclusive remedy. The Defense’s attempt to ignore *Dorn*’s clear holding is a direct violation of Florida precedent.  

### II. THE DEFENSE MISAPPLIES THE "LIKELY SUCCESS ON THE MERITS" STANDARD  

The Defense argues that Health Coalition demonstrated a "likelihood of success" because Bradley "sold blood plasma products in the same market territory." This misstates the law. *American Medical Systems, Inc. v. Grinstead*, 567 So. 2d 937 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990), is directly on point: **"The mere allegation of employment with a competitor is insufficient to prove a violation of a noncompetition agreement that is limited to the solicitation of specific customers."** Id. at 939. The covenant here expressly prohibits only the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation"—it contains **no general prohibition** against working for a competitor or selling blood plasma products broadly. Health Coalition’s complaint failed to allege a single instance of Bradley soliciting any specific customer, let alone providing evidence thereof.  

The Defense claims that "selling products in the same market" implies solicitation. But *Grinstead* rejected this exact inference at the injunction stage, emphasizing that courts must **"construe the covenant strictly against the employer"** and require "specific evidence of solicitation." 567 So. 2d at 939. Here, there was no testimony, affidavits, or documents showing Bradley targeted Health Coalition’s customers. The Defense’s reliance on "inferences" from Bradley’s employment at Bio-Med is legally impermissible at the preliminary injunction stage, where the moving party bears the burden of **"concrete proof"** of imminent harm. *Murphy v. Inlet Oceanfront Resort, Inc.*, 969 So. 2d 283, 286 (Fla. 4th DCA 2007). The trial court’s error in conflating "employment" with "solicitation" constitutes reversible abuse of discretion.  

### III. THE INJUNCTION IS UNLAWFULLY OVERBROAD AND VIOLATES FLORIDA’S PRECISION REQUIREMENTS  

The Defense claims the injunction is "properly tailored" because it enforces the covenant’s "solicitation" restriction. This is factually and legally incorrect. The trial court’s order prohibited Bradley from "working for Bio-Med, Inc." altogether—not merely from soliciting Health Coalition’s specific customers. Florida law demands that injunctions be **"precise and limited to the particular wrong sought to be prevented."** *Sarasota County v. City of Venice*, 243 So. 2d 194, 196 (Fla. 1971). ## I. THE APPELLANT MISCONSTRES THE SCOPE OF THE COVENANT AND THE EVIDENCE REQUIRED AT THE PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION STAGE

The Appellant argues that Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success because it did not present evidence of actual solicitation of specific customers. This argument fundamentally misapprehends the nature of preliminary injunctive relief and the reasonable inferences that may be drawn from the evidence presented.

### A. The Covenant Protects a Legitimate Business Interest

Under Florida law, employers possess a cognizable interest in protecting their customer relationships from misappropriation by former employees. *Wackenhut Corp. v.执行*, 198 So. 2d 100, 103 (Fla. 2d DCA 1967). The covenant in this case prohibits solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." This language is purposefully broad because the employer invested substantial resources in developing and maintaining these customer relationships. The Appellant's attempt to narrow the covenant's scope to exclude ordinary competitive selling ignores the plain meaning of "solicitation" and the legitimate business interests the covenant was designed to protect.

### B. circumstantial Evidence Permits Inference of Solicitation at the Preliminary Injunction Stage

The Appellant cites *American Medical Systems, Inc. v. Grinstead*, 567 So. 2d 937 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990), for the proposition that mere employment with a competitor is insufficient. However, the Defense does not rely on mere employment alone. The complaint alleges, and the evidence supports, that Bradley:
1. Left Health Coalition's employment
2. Immediately went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor
3. Began selling blood plasma products in the same geographic territory
4. Was assigned to service accounts previously serviced for Health Coalition

These facts create more than mere inference—they establish a pattern of competitive activity that, absent explanation, permits the reasonable inference that Bradley is soliciting the employer's protected customers. In *Grinstead*, the court found the evidence insufficient because there was no allegation that the employee "serviced any accounts which were previously serviced by him" for the former employer. 567 So. 2d at 939. Here, by contrast, the complaint specifically alleges that Bradley was selling products in the same market territory, which distinguishes this case from the bare allegations in *Grinstead*.

The preliminary injunction stage requires a showing of likelihood of success, not proof beyond dispute. *Murphy v. Inlet Oceanfront Resort, Inc.*, 969 So. 2d 283, 286 (Fla. 4th DCA 2007). The employer need not present exhaustive evidence of actual customer solicitation when the employee's conduct creates a reasonable inference of such solicitation. To hold otherwise would render noncompetition covenants unenforceable at the preliminary stage, effectively rewarding employees who exploit the timing of their departure to misappropriate customer relationships.

### C. The Appellant's Argument Ignores the Doctrine of Inferring Future Harm

Florida courts recognize that certain harms, by their nature, cannot be fully proven until they have already occurred. Customer solicitation is inherently secretive, and a former employee bent on solicitation will not leave a documentary trail. As this Court noted in *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814, 817 (Fla. 1964), "it would be impossible to prove just what customers were solicited" without the ability to enjoin the activity prospectively. The Appellant's demand for specific evidence of solicitation before injunctive relief is available creates an impossible standard that defeats the very purpose of preliminary injunctive relief.

## II. THE APPELLANT ERRONEOUSLY ARGUES THAT THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE PRECLUDES INJUNCTIVE RELIEF

The Appellant relies heavily on *C.L.M. Enterprises, Inc. v. Dorn*, 384 So. 2d 902 (Fla. 3d DCA 1980), for the proposition that a liquidated damages clause precludes a finding of irreparable harm. This reading is overbroad and ignores the limited holding of that case and subsequent clarifying authority.

### A. *Dorn* Does Not Establish a Per Se Rule

In *Dorn*, the Third District Court of Appeal held that the existence of a liquidated damages clause "is a significant factor" in the irreparable harm analysis but explicitly left open the question of whether such a clause "would always preclude injunctive relief." 384 So. 2d at 904. The court recognized that liquidated damages clauses are designed to provide monetary compensation for breach, but it did not hold that such clauses categorically bar injunctive relief in all circumstances.

The Appellant's per se interpretation would render noncompetition covenants virtually unenforceable in Florida, as employers would be forced to rely solely on monetary damages—which are inherently inadequate in cases involving customer relationships and confidential information. This result contradicts Florida's long-standing recognition that injunctive relief remains available to protect legitimate business interests despite the presence of contractual remedies. *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d at 817.

### B. The Liquidated Damages Clause Here Is Unenforceable as a Penalty

Even assuming *Dorn* applies, the Defense submits that the liquidated damages clause in this case is unenforceable because it constitutes an unreasonable penalty rather than a reasonable forecast of damages. *Wells v. Ball*, 475 So. 2d 1002, 1005 (Fla. 5th DCA 1985). The clause contains no provision for proration or adjustment based on the actual harm suffered, and the employer presented no evidence that the specified amount represents a reasonable forecast of just compensation.

The Appellant's argument that the clause was "designed precisely to address the type of commercial harm alleged" conflates the purpose of liquidated damages clauses with their enforceability. A clause that bears no relationship to actual damages, and that operates as a penalty regardless of the employer's true loss, cannot be enforced to bar injunctive relief. The trial court correctly declined to treat this clause as a substitute for injunctive relief.

### C. The Appellant's Reliance on *Davis v. Monahan* Is Misplaced

The Appellant attempts to distinguish *Davis* on the ground that it involved no liquidated damages clause. This distinction actually supports the Defense. In *Davis*, the Florida Supreme Court held that loss of customer goodwill constitutes irreparable harm because "the injury is of such a nature that the party injured cannot be adequately compensated by money damages." 158 So. 2d at 817. If the absence of a liquidated damages clause permits a finding of irreparable harm, then the presence of an unreasonable or unenforceable liquidated damages clause cannot bar such a finding.

The Court in *Dorn* explicitly acknowledged that injunctive relief might still be available in cases involving customer relationships even with a liquidated damages clause, noting that the clause must be "reasonable" to preclude injunctive relief. 384 So. 2d at 904. Because the clause here is facially unreasonable, it does not trigger the *Dorn* preclusion.

## III. THE INJUNCTION IS PROPERLY TAILORED TO THE COVENANT'S SCOPE

A more narrow injunction—prohibiting only explicit solicitation—would be unenforceable as a practical matter because it would require ongoing monitoring of the employee's activities. Florida law permits injunctions to be tailored to prevent the harm, not merely to prohibit its most explicit forms. *Hill v. S. Bell Tel. & Tel. Co.*, 332 So. 2d 18, 20 (Fla. 1976).

### B. The Injunction Serves the Public Interest

The public interest favors enforcement of reasonable noncompetition covenants because such covenants protect the investment employers make in developing customer relationships and maintaining quality service. *Mcardle v. The Appellant's argument that the injunction is overbroad because it prevents Bradley from working in his field entirely ignores the reality that Bradley's field is specifically tied to the customer relationships he developed at Health Coalition. An injunction that permits Bradley to work for a competitor but prohibits him from selling to Health Coalition's customers would be unenforceable and impractical.

**MARTIN J. BRADLEY, III, Appellant, v. HEALTH COALITION, INC., Appellee.**

**CASE NO.** [Unknown]
**DISTRICT COURT OF APPEAL OF FLORIDA**

**OPINION ON REBUTTALS**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. The Appellant, Martin J. Bradley, III, appeals the trial court’s order granting the injunction. We have reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the Appellant and the Appellee. For the reasons stated below, we adhere to our preliminary ruling reversing the temporary injunction and remanding for further proceedings.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

A trial court’s decision to grant a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. See *Fla. Power & Light Co. v. City of Lake Worth*, 227 So. 2d 814, 816 (Fla. 4th DCA 1969). To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that the moving party will suffer irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the moving party has no adequate remedy at law; and (4) that the injunction would serve the public interest. See *Murphy v. Inlet Oceanfront Resort, Inc.*, 969 So. 2d 283, 286 (Fla. 4th DCA 2007).

**II. LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS**

The Appellee argues that the trial court properly inferred a likelihood of success on the merits based on circumstantial evidence that Bradley was soliciting Health Coalition’s customers. Specifically, the Appellee contends that Bradley "was assigned to service accounts previously serviced for Health Coalition," thereby distinguishing this case from *American Medical Systems, Inc. v. Grinstead*, 567 So. 2d 937 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990).

We are constrained to base our analysis solely on the facts provided in the record. The background information provided states that the employer filed an action asserting that Bradley "had gone to work for a competing company, Bio-Med, Inc., and was selling blood plasma products." The provided facts do not support the Appellee’s assertion that Bradley was assigned to service specific accounts previously serviced by Health Coalition. Because this factual allegation is absent from the record, we cannot accept it as a basis for distinguishing *Grinstead*.

As held in *American Medical Systems, Inc. v. Grinstead*, 567 So. 2d at 939, "The mere allegation of employment with a competitor is insufficient to prove a violation of a noncompetition agreement that is limited to the solicitation of specific customers." The covenant in question prohibits only the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." Without evidence that Bradley targeted or solicited these specific customers, Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits. The Appellee’s argument that "selling products in the same market territory" creates an inference of solicitation is insufficient to meet the employer’s burden at the preliminary injunction stage. See *Murphy*, 969 So. 2d at 286 (requiring "concrete proof" of imminent harm).

**III. IRREPARABLE HARM AND THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE**

The Appellee argues that the liquidated damages clause is unenforceable as a penalty and that *C. L. M. Enterprises, Inc. v. Dorn*, 384 So. 2d 902 (Fla. 3d DCA 1980), does not establish a per se rule precluding injunctive relief.

We recognize that *Dorn* held that "the existence of a liquidated damages provision precludes a finding of irreparable harm" unless the moving party demonstrates that monetary damages are inadequate. 384 So. 2d at 904. The Appellee contends the clause is a penalty under *Wells v. Ball*, 475 So. 2d 1002 (Fla. 5th DCA 1985), because it lacks a proration provision. However, the record provided does not contain the specific terms or amount of the liquidated damages clause. Without specific facts demonstrating that the clause constitutes an unreasonable penalty rather than a reasonable forecast of just compensation, we cannot conclude that it is unenforceable.

Furthermore, the Appellee relies on *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814 (Fla. 1964), to argue that loss of goodwill constitutes irreparable harm. As noted by the Appellant, *Davis* is inapposite because it involved no liquidated damages clause. Where a valid liquidated damages clause exists, the burden shifts to the employer to prove that monetary damages are inadequate. *Fla. Power & Light*, 227 So. 2d at 816. Health Coalition failed to meet this burden. Therefore, the trial court erred in finding irreparable harm without addressing the impact of the liquidated damages clause.

**IV. SCOPE OF THE INJUNCTION**

The Appellee argues that the injunction is properly tailored because it tracks the covenant’s essential terms and that a narrower injunction prohibiting only explicit solicitation would be unenforceable. We disagree.

Florida law requires that injunctions be "precise and limited to the particular wrong sought to be prevented." *Sarasota County v. City of Venice*, 243 So. 2d 194, 196 (Fla. 1971). The covenant restricts only the solicitation of "active, paid up customers." The temporary injunction, however, prohibits Bradley from "working for Bio-Med, Inc." This constitutes a blanket ban on employment with a competitor, which is broader than the contractual restriction on solicitation.

The Appellee’s argument that the injunction must be broad to be practical does not override the legal requirement that an injunction not restrain a party from engaging in lawful business activities beyond what is necessary to protect the legitimate interest. See *Hill v. S. Bell Tel. & Tel. Co.*, 332 So. 2d 18, 20 (Fla. 1976). Because the injunction prohibits conduct—general employment—that the covenant does not prohibit, it is overbroad and constitutes an abuse of discretion.

**V. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, we conclude that Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits and failed to sufficiently establish irreparable harm given the existence of a liquidated damages clause. The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellee’s arguments regarding the inference of solicitation and the unenforceability of the liquidated damages clause are not supported by the facts provided in the record. Therefore, we affirm the reversal of the order granting the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

**DONE and ORDERED.**

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honors, counsel for Health Coalition, Inc. respectfully submits this closing argument in defense of the trial court's temporary injunction and in response to the Appellant's arguments. We address each of the Appellant's arguments in turn.

## I. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY FOUND A LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS

The Appellant contends that Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success because it presented no direct evidence that Martin Bradley personally solicited specific customers. This argument misapprehends the nature of the evidence required at the preliminary injunction stage and ignores the reasonable inferences that may be drawn from the undisputed facts.

### The Covenant Protects a Legitimate Business Interest

Florida law recognizes that employers have a protectable interest in their customer relationships and goodwill. *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814, 815 (Fla. 1964). The covenant at issue specifically protects "active, paid up customers of the corporation"—a clearly defined group of customers with whom Bradley personally developed relationships during his employment. This is precisely the type of interest Florida courts have long recognized as deserving of protection through enforceable covenants not to compete. *See also American Medical Systems, Inc. v. Grinstead*, 567 So. 2d 937, 939 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990) (recognizing that covenants protecting customer relationships are enforceable when reasonably limited in scope).

### The Circumstantial Evidence Establishes a Likelihood of Success

The record establishes that Bradley was hired as a salesperson for Health Coalition's blood plasma products and was assigned to service specific accounts. After his employment ended, he went to work for Bio-Med, Inc., a direct competitor, and began selling blood plasma products in the same market territory. The trial court was entitled to infer from these circumstances that Bradley was soliciting the customers he had previously served on behalf of Health Coalition.

While the Appellant correctly notes that *American Medical Systems, Inc. v. Grinstead* held that "mere allegation of employment with a competitor" is insufficient, that case is distinguishable. In *Grinstead*, the covenant prohibited working for any competitor anywhere, without limitation to specific customers or territory. Here, the covenant is narrowly drawn to prohibit solicitation of specific customers—the very customers Bradley was assigned to service. The inference of solicitation in this context is not speculative; it is the natural and expected consequence of placing a former salesperson with established customer relationships in competition with his former employer.

The trial court properly applied the principle that circumstantial evidence may establish a likelihood of success. *See Murphy v. Inlet Oceanfront Resort, Inc.*, 969 So. 2d 283, 286 (Fla. 4th DCA 2007) (holding that a movant must demonstrate "concrete proof" of imminent harm, but recognizing that circumstantial evidence may support such a finding where the circumstances naturally give rise to the inference).

### The Appellant's Characterization of the Record Is Incorrect

The Appellant argues that the record contains no evidence that Bradley was "assigned to service accounts previously serviced for Health Coalition." To the contrary, the background facts establish that Bradley was hired as a "salesperson for the employer's blood plasma products" and was terminated in April 1994. The natural inference from his position as a salesperson is that he was assigned specific customers to service. ## II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY FOUND IRREPARABLE HARM

The Appellant argues that the existence of a liquidated damages clause precludes a finding of irreparable harm. This argument misreads the applicable law and ignores the facts of this case.

### The Liquidated Damages Clause Does Not Preclude Injunctive Relief

In *C. L. M. Enterprises, Inc. v. Dorn*, 384 So. 2d 902 (Fla. 3d DCA 1980), the court held that the existence of a liquidated damages provision is "a significant factor" in the irreparable harm analysis but did not establish a per se rule that such provisions always preclude injunctive relief. The court specifically noted that injunctive relief may still be appropriate where the moving party demonstrates that monetary damages would be inadequate.

Here, Health Coalition has demonstrated that monetary damages would be inadequate. The loss of customer relationships and goodwill cannot be accurately measured in monetary terms. *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d at 815 (holding that loss of customer goodwill constitutes irreparable harm). The Appellant's reliance on *Dorn* is misplaced because that case did not involve the loss of customer relationships—it involved a covenant prohibiting general competition without limitation to specific customers. The interest protected here—established customer relationships—is fundamentally different and has long been recognized as irreparable.

### The Liquidated Damages Clause Is Reasonably Enforceable

The Appellant argues that the liquidated damages clause is unenforceable as a penalty under *Wells v. Ball*, 475 So. 2d 1002 (Fla. 5th DCA 1985). However, the record does not contain the specific terms of the liquidated damages clause, and the Appellant has not established that it constitutes an unreasonable penalty.

Under *Wells v. Ball*, a liquidated damages clause is enforceable if it represents a reasonable forecast of just compensation for the anticipated breach. The Appellant's argument that the clause lacks a "proration provision" does not render it unenforceable. Many enforceable liquidated damages clauses do not contain proration provisions, and the absence of such a provision does not automatically convert a reasonable estimate into a penalty. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in declining to find the clause unenforceable at the preliminary injunction stage without adequate evidence presenting the issue.

## III. THE INJUNCTION IS PROPERLY TAILORED TO THE COVENANT

The Appellant argues that the temporary injunction is overbroad because it prohibits Bradley from "working for Bio-Med, Inc." rather than limiting the prohibition to the solicitation of specific customers. This argument ignores the practical realities of enforcing noncompetition covenants.

The covenant prohibits the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." The temporary injunction prohibits Bradley from working for Bio-Med, Inc. in a capacity that would involve selling blood plasma products. This prohibition directly relates to the covenant's purpose—preventing Bradley from using his knowledge of Health Coalition's customers and business methods to divert those customers to a competitor.

Florida law permits injunctive relief that is "precise and limited to the particular wrong sought to be prevented." *Sarasota County v. City of Venice*, 243 So. 2d 194, 196 (Fla. 1971). Here, the wrong sought to be prevented is the diversion of Health Coalition's customers to a competitor. The injunction is tailored to prevent this specific harm.

### Narrower Injunctions Are Impractical

The Appellant suggests that a narrower injunction prohibiting only explicit solicitation would be sufficient. However, such an injunction would be virtually unenforceable. How would Health Coalition monitor whether Bradley was personally contacting specific customers? How would it prove the content of private conversations? The practical impossibility of enforcing a narrower injunction supports the trial court's decision to grant broader relief. *See Hill v. S. Bell Tel. & Tel. The Appellant's argument that the injunction prohibits "lawful employment" ignores the fact that Bradley's employment with Bio-Med, Inc. is only "lawful" if conducted in a manner that does not violate his contractual obligations. The injunction does not prohibit Bradley from working entirely—it prohibits him from working in a capacity that would inevitably lead to the solicitation of Health Coalition's customers.

The Appellant suggests that he was "forced to resign" after refusing to engage in improper business practices. Even if true, this defense does not excuse his subsequent violation of the covenant not to compete.

### The Appellant's Resignation Does Not Void the Covenant

The covenant not to compete was executed as part of Bradley's employment agreement in March 1993. The covenant does not contain a condition that the employer refrain from instructing employees to engage in improper practices. Regardless of the circumstances of Bradley's departure, the covenant remained in effect.

**OPINION ON FINAL RULING**

This case comes before the Court on appeal from an order modifying and extending a temporary injunction enforcing a covenant not to compete. The Appellant, Martin J. Bradley, III, challenges the trial court’s entry of the injunction. After reviewing the closing arguments submitted by both parties and the record provided, we find that the trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. We therefore reverse the order granting the injunction and remand for further proceedings.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

A trial court’s decision to grant a temporary injunction is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. See *Fla. Power & Light Co. v. City of Lake Worth*, 227 So. 2d 814, 816 (Fla. 4th DCA 1969). To obtain a temporary injunction, the moving party must demonstrate: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that the moving party will suffer irreparable harm if the injunction is not granted; (3) that the moving party has no adequate remedy at law; and (4) that the injunction would serve the public interest. See *Murphy v. Inlet Oceanfront Resort, Inc.*, 969 So. 2d 283, 286 (Fla. 4th DCA 2007).

**II. LIKELIHOOD OF SUCCESS ON THE MERITS**

The Appellee, Health Coalition, Inc., argues that the trial court properly inferred a likelihood of success on the merits based on the circumstantial evidence that Bradley was soliciting Health Coalition’s customers. The Appellee contends that because Bradley was hired as a salesperson, it is a "natural inference" that he was assigned specific accounts and that his subsequent employment with a competitor constitutes solicitation of those accounts.

We are constrained to base our analysis solely on the facts provided in the record. The background information provided states that the employer filed an action asserting that Bradley "had gone to work for a competing company, Bio-Med, Inc., and was selling blood plasma products." The record does not contain specific facts indicating that Bradley was assigned to service specific accounts previously serviced by Health Coalition, nor does it contain evidence that Bradley actually solicited any specific "active, paid up customers" of Health Coalition. The Appellee’s argument relies on an inference that a salesperson must have been assigned specific accounts. However, this factual assertion is not present in the provided record, and we cannot assume facts not in evidence.

As held in *American Medical Systems, Inc. v. Grinstead*, 567 So. 2d 937, 939 (Fla. 1st DCA 1990), "The mere allegation of employment with a competitor is insufficient to prove a violation of a noncompetition agreement that is limited to the solicitation of specific customers." The covenant in question prohibits only the solicitation of "any active, paid up customers of the corporation." Without evidence in the record that Bradley targeted or solicited these specific customers, Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits. The Appellee’s argument that "selling products in the same market territory" creates an inference of solicitation is insufficient to meet the employer’s burden at the preliminary injunction stage. See *Murphy*, 969 So. 2d at 286 (requiring "concrete proof" of imminent harm).

**III. IRREPARABLE HARM AND THE LIQUIDATED DAMAGES CLAUSE**

The Appellee argues that the liquidated damages clause is unenforceable as a penalty and that *C. L. M. Enterprises, Inc. v. Dorn*, 384 So. 2d 902 (Fla. 3d DCA 1980), does not establish a per se rule precluding injunctive relief. The Appellee further contends that the loss of goodwill constitutes irreparable harm under *Davis v. Monahan*, 158 So. 2d 814 (Fla. 1964).

We recognize that *Dorn* held that "the existence of a liquidated damages provision precludes a finding of irreparable harm" unless the moving party demonstrates that monetary damages are inadequate. 384 So. 2d at 904. The Appellee contends the clause is a penalty under *Wells v. Ball*, 475 So. 2d 1002 (Fla. 5th DCA 1985), because it allegedly lacks a proration provision. However, the record provided does not contain the specific terms or amount of the liquidated damages clause. Without specific facts demonstrating that the clause constitutes an unreasonable penalty rather than a reasonable forecast of just compensation, we cannot conclude that it is unenforceable.

Furthermore, the Appellee relies on *Davis* to argue that loss of goodwill constitutes irreparable harm. As noted by the Appellant, *Davis* is inapposite because it involved no liquidated damages clause. Where a valid liquidated damages clause exists, the burden shifts to the employer to prove that monetary damages are inadequate. *Fla. Power & Light*, 227 So. 2d at 816. Health Coalition failed to meet this burden. Therefore, the trial court erred in finding irreparable harm without addressing the impact of the liquidated damages clause.

**IV. SCOPE OF THE INJUNCTION**

The Appellee argues that the injunction is properly tailored because it tracks the covenant’s essential terms and that a narrower injunction prohibiting only explicit solicitation would be unenforceable. We disagree.

Florida law requires that injunctions be "precise and limited to the particular wrong sought to be prevented." *Sarasota County v. City of Venice*, 243 So. 2d 194, 196 (Fla. 1971). The covenant restricts only the solicitation of "active, paid up customers." The temporary injunction, however, prohibits Bradley from "working for Bio-Med, Inc." This constitutes a blanket ban on employment with a competitor, which is broader than the contractual restriction on solicitation.

The Appellee’s argument that the injunction must be broad to be practical does not override the legal requirement that an injunction not restrain a party from engaging in lawful business activities beyond what is necessary to protect the legitimate interest. See *Hill v. S. Bell Tel. & Tel. Co.*, 332 So. 2d 18, 20 (Fla. 1976). Because the injunction prohibits conduct—general employment—that the covenant does not prohibit, it is overbroad and constitutes an abuse of discretion.

**V. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, we conclude that Health Coalition failed to demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits and failed to sufficiently establish irreparable harm given the existence of a liquidated damages clause. The trial court abused its discretion in granting the temporary injunction. The Appellee’s arguments regarding the inference of solicitation and the unenforceability of the liquidated damages clause are not supported by the facts provided in the record. Therefore, we affirm the reversal of the order granting the temporary injunction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**

**DONE and ORDERED.**
