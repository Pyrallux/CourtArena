FACTS:
ORDER DENYING MOTIONS FOR CHANGE OF VENUE
We have consolidated the motions of the three defendants because they each seek the same relief: a change of venue. Each defendant was arrested and charged with various counts of Promoting Prostitution in the First Degree: Wei Ki Fang, one count; Siituu Sanerivi, six counts; and Wei Li Fang, nine counts plus one count of assault in the second degree. The defendants argue that the pre-trial publicity has been massive, pervasive, and prejudicial. Also, because both Wei Ki and Wei Li Fang are Chinese nationals — though Sanerivi was bom in Western Samoa— counsel for Wei Ki Fang submits that (he publicity is the product of bigotry from a “culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not bom here.” On these grounds they all seek a change of venue.
Discussion
I. Pre-trial Publicity
“A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process.” Irvin v. Dowd, 366 U.S. 717, 722 (1961) (quoting In re Murchison, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955)); see generally Km.. SAMOA Rev. CONST., art. I, § 2; U.S. CONST., amend. V. When the trial is conducted in front of a jury, due process assures an accused of, inter alia, certain minimal protections: 1) “a charge fairly made and fairly tried in a public tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power,” Sheppard v. Maxwell, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966) (quoting Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 236-37 (1940)); 2) “a panel of impartial, ‘indifferent’ jurors,” Irvin, 366 U.S. at 722; and 3) “the requirement that the jury’s verdict be based on evidence received in open court, not from outside sources,” Sheppard, 384 U.S. at 351. See Murphy v. Florida, 421 U.S. 794 (1975); Rideau v. Louisiana, 373 U.S. 723 (1963).
*107Due process is violated when inflammatory and pervasive publicity taints a jury pool, resulting in preconceived ideas by often misinformed jurors. The Supreme Court has established two ways in which to' show such a taint — presumed (or inherent) and actual prejudice. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 728 (actual prejudice); Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723 (presumed (or inherent) prejudice); Nevers v. Killinger, 169 F.3d 352, 362-63 (6th. Cir. 1999); United States v. Washington, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272-73 (D. Vt. 1993). Actual prejudice requires a showing “that it is reasonably likely that a fair and impartial jury cannot be secured.” Bell v. Lynbaugh, 663 F. Supp. 405, 417 (E.D. Tex. 1987). It is “discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire.” Nevers, 169 F.3d at 362 (citing Irvin, 366 U.S. at 725-28) (emphasis in original); see also Am. Samoa Gov’t v. Snow, 26 A.S.R.2d 78, 80 (Trial Div. 1994). At this point, such an inquiry is premature, as there has not yet been jury voir dire.
The defendants are left then with the highly difficult task of showing that the publicity up until now has created an atmosphere of presumed prejudice. A showing of presumed prejudice “is rare, and is reserved for exceptional cases where the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom.” Washington, 813 F. Supp at 272; United States v. Moreno, 815 F.2d 725, 731-39 (1st Cir. 1987); Coleman v. Kemp, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985). It can be proven without the benefit of juror interviews because the atmosphere created belies any claim of impartiality. See Rideau, 373 U.S. at 727; Moreno, 815 F.2d at 753 (Torruella, J., dissenting); Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1543; Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 272-73. But the defendants carry a heavy burden of showing oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1490; Commonwealth v. Drumheller, 808 A.2d 893, 902 (Pa. 2002).1
Applying these standards, and based on the paltry evidence before us, defendants have not presented a compelling case of prejudice. To begin with, they cite only newspaper articles and editorials. There is no *108evidence whatsoever of prejudiced radio or television coverage or public sentiment generally. But see Rideau, 373 U.S. at 723; Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1487. Furthermore, the newspaper articles, while prone to hyperbole, are not inflammatory. Granted, some of the headlines describe the defendants of a “prostitution ring” or “sex ring.” But, the articles make an effort to point out that the defendants have not been found guilty; rather, they are alleged to have committed the crimes. In fact, most of the articles contain an attempt to reproduce testimony and arguments from court, heard in a public forum open to all. That is, they contain information that an average citizen would be privy to were he to go to court himself. Finally, the defendants reproduced only six articles, a far cry from the type of overwhelming saturation which can lead to a presumption of prejudice. See Coleman, 778 F.2d at 1491-1537.

One might question why a newspaper would choose to publish such letters in the first place. Perhaps they believe that such a tabloid styled format is profitable. Or, perhaps they have a policy of publishing all letters, regardless of content.2 In either event, the airing of narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions is protected in our society precisely because it exposes the absurdity and weaknesses of the speaker’s views. But by protecting this type of speech, we need not sweepingly attribute it to all. We need only recognize it represents a very limited section of society.
*109Finally, we note that if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors. See, e.g., Washington, 813 F. Supp. at 273; Drumheller, 808 A.2d at 903. Only then can we meaningfully gauge the public’s sentiment.
n. Remedies
The defendants have asked that if we find no prejudice at this juncture, that we revisit the motion once again after voir dire. Thus, while we need not now decide the proper remedy were we to find prejudice, the possibility remains. We note then that were a showing of prejudice to be made, the remedy would not be a change of venue.
A change of venue is a common remedy stateside, but is unsuitable here in American Samoa. See Moreno, 815 F.2d 725; cf. In re San Juan Star Co., 662 F.2d 108, 117 (1981) (finding that change of venue unsuitable in Puerto Rico and alternative remedies appropriate). A change of venue stateside is sought in the hopes that moving the trial out of the jurisdiction where the prejudice has tainted the jury pool will result in a neutral jury elsewhere. See Irvin, 366 U.S. at 719-21. This is but a pipe dream here in American Samoa.
It is no secret that our island community is small. We have but one jury roll. All the registered voters on the island are part of that roll. Transferring a case to another part of the islands would only result in a physical change of venue; but, inherently, the proceedings would be the same. The jury would be selected from the same pool. Furthermore, because of the compact social structure of this island community, chances are that if the media has created a prejudicial environment it will have infected the farthest comers of the Territory. If anything, moving a criminal case would likely cause a great inconvenience to all the parties, including the Court.
Defendants cite A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 to support the notion that a transfer is possible. This enactment reads:
In any case where the interest of justice or the convenience of parties, witnesses or the court requires, the Chief Justice or the Associate Justice may order that a session of any division of the High Court adjourn from the courthouse to sit at any appropriate place in American Samoa.
This statute, however, was enacted in 1969 (P.L. 11-54) long before criminal jury trials in American Samoa were 'found to be neither “impractical nor anomalous,” and, therefore, constitutionally mandated. See King v. Andrus, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977). Rather, the statute is geared towards physically holding court in a more convenient *110location in the Territory; for example, this power might be invoiced in a case involving a dispute in Manu'a, where the majority of witnesses would be unable to attend a court session in Fagatogo. But the situation would have to be extreme. Indeed, we cannot recall any instance where this section has been used in recent times. The statute simply does not contemplate a change of venue of the type sought by the defendants. Compare Fed. R. Crim. P. 21(a) with A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 and Fed. R. Crim. P; 21(b).
Therefore, if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives. See Moreno, 815 F.2d at 731. A change of venue is not constitutionally required; instead, we need only assure a fair trial.3
Order
We accordingly deny the motion for change of venue. We will, however, allow the defendants to submit a voir dire questionnaire ahead of time for the court’s review not later than two weeks before trial. The questionnaire should provide the questions in both English and Samoan. It may include any inquiries about media exposure. The questions should be amenable to a yes or no response. More importantly, the questions shall not touch on 1) any anticipated instructions; 2) the verdict to be returned when those questions are based upon hypothetical facts or situations; 3) substantive arguments of the case; and 4) data available from jury information sheets. Then, based upon the answers, during voir dire, we will take appropriate steps in establishing whether any of the jurors has been prejudiced including individual voir dire if necessary.
It is so ordered.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the High Court of American Samoa

---

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the prosecution's argument and in support of our motion for change of venue. We acknowledge the thorough analysis provided in the trial court's denial order, but we contend that certain legal standards were applied too rigidly at this preliminary stage, and that the unique circumstances of American Samoa warrant a more cautious approach to preserving the defendants' constitutional right to a fair trial.

---

## I. The Defense Does Not Bear the Burden of Proving Prejudice at This Stage—Only of Raising Sufficient Doubt

The prosecution incorrectly frames this motion as one requiring the defense to prove actual or presumed prejudice. This misstates the applicable standard. The defense need not prove prejudice at this juncture; rather, we must raise a reasonable doubt that an impartial jury can be seated, at which point the burden shifts to the prosecution to demonstrate juror impartiality. The trial court acknowledged that actual prejudice "is discerned only by reviewing both the extent and nature of the [pre-trial] publicity and the responses of the prospective jurors in voir dire," *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999) (emphasis in original). However, this does not mean the defense must wait until after voir dire to raise the venue issue. The very purpose of a change of venue motion is to prevent the tainting of the jury pool before it occurs.

In *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717 (1961), the Supreme Court recognized that "the right to a change of venue is not dependent upon a showing that the jurors have actually been biased," but rather upon circumstances creating "a presumption of prejudice." *Id.* at 728 (Frankfurter, J., concurring). The defense has raised substantial concerns about the nature and extent of pre-trial publicity—concerns that the trial court acknowledged warranted further inquiry through voir dire. The prosecution's argument that the defense must prove prejudice before any voir dire effectively nullifies the venue remedy, as prejudice, once proven through juror responses, has already infected the jury selection process.

---

## II. The Trial Court Applied an Incorrect Legal Standard by Requiring "Oversaturation" Before Presumed Prejudice Could Attach

The prosecution celebrates the trial court's finding that the defendants presented a "paltry" case of prejudice. However, the trial court applied an incorrectly high standard by requiring "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage" before presumed prejudice could be found. This standard, derived from cases like *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487 (11th Cir. 1985), and *Commonwealth v. Drumheller*, 808 A.2d 893 (Pa. 2002), describes the quantum of publicity sufficient to establish presumed prejudice in some cases—but it does not represent the threshold that defendants must meet to obtain a change of venue.

The Supreme Court has held that presumed prejudice may arise when "the influence of the news media negatively pervades the proceedings, either in the community or courtroom." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993). This standard is qualitative, not merely quantitative. The trial court acknowledged that "if we were to make any finding of prejudice, it is more likely to follow the opportunity to listen to potential jurors." This acknowledgment suggests the court recognized that the current evidence may be insufficient but that juror responses might change its analysis. This is precisely the problem: by requiring the defense to prove presumed prejudice before voir dire, the trial court has required us to demonstrate a harm that can only be measured through the very process we seek to avoid.

The defense contends that the nature of the charges—promoting prostitution and assault—combined with the media's characterization of the defendants as operating a "prostitution ring" or "sex ring," creates a particularly prejudicial atmosphere in any community, but especially in a small island territory where such crimes are rare and highly sensationalized. The trial court noted that "some of the headlines describe the defendants of a 'prostitution ring' or 'sex ring.'" This characterization goes beyond merely reporting allegations; it implies criminal organization and moral depravity that simple charges of promoting prostitution do not necessarily convey.

---

## III. The Unique Circumstances of American Samoa Require Special Consideration

The prosecution and the trial court both correctly note that a change of venue is impractical in American Samoa. However, this practical reality should not immunize the Territory from constitutional scrutiny—it should instead prompt the court to consider whether alternative safeguards are sufficient to assure a fair trial. The trial court stated that "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives" and that "a change of venue is not constitutionally required; instead, we need only assure a fair trial." *See* *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987).

The defense agrees that alternative remedies may be appropriate, but we submit that the trial court erred in denying the motion outright rather than reserving judgment pending voir dire. The trial court offered to allow defendants to submit a voir dire questionnaire and to conduct individual voir dire if necessary. However, this remedy is insufficient because:

First, once voir dire begins, the jury pool has already been exposed to the publicity. Prospective jurors may not consciously recall the articles, but the publicity may nonetheless influence their unconscious biases. As the Supreme Court noted in *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 350 (1966), "a charge fairly made and fairly tried in a public tribunal free of prejudice, passion, excitement, and tyrannical power" is essential to due process.

Second, in a community as small as American Samoa, potential jurors may be reluctant to admit prejudice during voir dire due to social pressure. The compact social structure that makes a change of venue impractical also makes candid disclosure of bias unlikely. Jurors may know the defendants, the victims, or the witnesses, and may be reluctant to acknowledge this knowledge or the resulting prejudice.

Third, the trial court's proposed voir dire questionnaire, while helpful, cannot capture the full extent of pre-trial publicity exposure. The court proposed yes-or-no questions about media exposure, but this format cannot probe the nature or impact of that exposure. A juror who read an article about a "prostitution ring" may honestly answer "yes" to a question about media exposure without appreciating how that coverage affected their impressions of the defendants.

---

## IV. The Cultural Bias Argument Was Improperly Dismissed

The prosecution characterizes the defense's cultural bias argument as "pure speculation with no factual basis in the record." This mischaracterizes the argument. Counsel for Wei Ki Fang did not speculate about community bias; rather, counsel pointed to specific evidence in the record—letters to the editor and editorial content—that demonstrated prejudice against non-native residents. The trial court acknowledged that "the airing of narrow-minded, prejudiced opinions is protected in our society precisely because it exposes the absurdity and weaknesses of the speaker's views" and that "we need only recognize it represents a very limited section of society."

The defense does not claim that these letters reflect widespread community sentiment. Rather, we submit that in a small community where media outlets are "virtual monopolies," even limited expression of prejudice can influence the broader community's attitudes. The trial court itself noted that "the press has ethical obligations to be objective and truthful—even more so when covering the happenings of a small island community and even more so when the island's media outlets are virtual monopolies." This observation cuts both ways: if the press has enhanced power to influence, then prejudice expressed through the press has enhanced potential to harm.

Furthermore, the defense's cultural bias argument is not limited to the letters. The fact that two of the three defendants are Chinese nationals, combined with coverage emphasizing their foreign status, creates a risk that jurors will conflate national origin with criminal propensity. The prosecution's dismissal of this argument as "conclusory" ignores the record evidence of prejudicial coverage.

---

## V. The Remedy Must Be Proportionate to the Harm

Even if the defense has not established presumed prejudice to the degree found in cases like *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723 (1963), the Constitution requires more than a post-hoc remedy after prejudice has already infected the jury. The trial court stated that "if prejudice were ever proven at the preliminary stages of a criminal prosecution, we would have to explore other alternatives." The defense submits that the preliminary stages are now, and the alternatives must include meaningful protections.

The trial court suggested that a bench trial might "allay any lingering defense suspicions." This suggestion is inadequate because it imposes the burden of waiving a constitutional right—the right to a jury trial—on the defense, rather than on the prosecution to demonstrate that a jury can be impaneled without prejudice. Moreover, a bench trial does not address the underlying problem: the community's exposure to prejudicial publicity.

---

## CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of the motion for change of venue, or in the alternative, order that the motion be renewed following voir dire with the benefit of prospective juror responses. The defense does not seek to prevent the trial from proceeding; we seek only to assure that the defendants receive the fair trial to which every accused is entitled under the Due Process Clause.

The prosecution is correct that the defense bears a heavy burden in establishing presumed prejudice. But the trial court applied that burden too rigidly, requiring proof of the very prejudice that a change of venue is designed to prevent. In a small community like American Samoa, where the jury pool is limited, media influence is concentrated, and social pressures inhibit candid disclosure, the Constitution requires heightened vigilance against prejudice—not a relaxation of standards that effectively denies defendants any remedy.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court grant the motion for change of venue, or in the alternative, remand with instructions to implement robust voir dire procedures, including individual sequestered questioning of prospective jurors, and to reserve judgment on the venue motion pending the outcome of voir dire.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Appellate Court on the review of the Trial Court’s Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants, Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, appeal the denial, arguing that the Trial Court applied an unduly rigid standard for presumed prejudice and failed to account for the unique vulnerabilities of the jury pool in American Samoa. After reviewing the arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, as well as the record provided, the Court affirms the denial of the motion for change of venue.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The denial of a motion for change of venue is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. The burden rests heavily on the defendant to demonstrate that pre-trial publicity prevents the assembly of a fair and impartial jury. See *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 727 (1961). To warrant a change of venue prior to voir dire, the defendant must show presumed prejudice, a standard reserved for "extreme situations." *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794, 799 (1975).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Defendants Failed to Establish Presumed Prejudice**

The defendants argue that the Trial Court erred by requiring a showing of "oversaturation" to establish presumed prejudice, contending instead that a qualitative assessment of the publicity's inflammatory nature should suffice. However, the law is clear that presumed prejudice arises only in "exceptional cases" where the publicity is "so pervasive and prejudicial" that it renders the trial a "hollow formality." *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993) (citing *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963)).

The defense relies on *United States v. Washington* to argue for a qualitative standard, yet the record in this case is factually distinct from the "saturation" described in precedent. In *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 726 (1963), the defendant’s televised confession was broadcast to the entire community three times. Here, the defendants have submitted only six newspaper articles. While the defense correctly notes that the headlines used terms such as "prostitution ring" and "sex ring," this characterization alone does not meet the high threshold of inherent prejudice required to overcome the presumption of juror impartiality. See *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985) (holding that presumed prejudice requires "oversaturation of highly sensationalized news coverage").

The Trial Court found that the articles were not inflammatory, as they consistently noted the defendants were alleged to have committed the crimes and largely reproduced public court testimony. The defense’s assertion that these articles imply "moral depravity" is insufficient to demonstrate that the community atmosphere is so corrupted as to justify a preemptive venue change. The quantity and nature of the coverage here fall far short of the "massive, pervasive, and prejudicial" publicity required to presume prejudice. See *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 353 (1966).

**B. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Denying the Motion Prior to Voir Dire**

The defense argues that the Trial Court should have reserved judgment on the motion rather than denying it outright, asserting that the defense need only raise a "reasonable doubt" regarding juror impartiality at this stage. While the defense correctly cites *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 362 (6th Cir. 1999), for the proposition that actual prejudice is discerned through voir dire, this does not alter the burden required to grant a change of venue *before* voir dire occurs.

Because the defendants failed to meet the heavy burden of showing presumed prejudice, the Trial Court acted within its discretion in denying the motion at this juncture. The Court is not required to grant a change of venue based on speculation; rather, it may rely on the voir dire process to uncover actual bias. The Trial Court’s order explicitly provided for this by allowing the submission of a detailed voir dire questionnaire and individual questioning if necessary. These remedies are the standard procedural safeguards for addressing potential bias when presumed prejudice has not been established. See *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987) (noting that if prejudice is found, alternative remedies short of venue change may be appropriate).

**C. The Unique Circumstances of American Samoa Do Not Warrant a Change of Venue**

The defense raises compelling concerns regarding the "compact social structure" of American Samoa and the potential for unconscious bias or social pressure during voir dire. However, these same characteristics render a change of venue impractical and legally ineffective. As noted in the Trial Court’s order and supported by *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977), American Samoa maintains a single jury roll of all registered voters. Physically moving the trial to another location within the Territory would not change the composition of the jury pool.

The defense argues that the Trial Court’s reliance on A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 was misplaced, but the statute’s text—allowing the court to adjourn to an "appropriate place in American Samoa" for convenience—does not empower the court to import a neutral jury from outside the Territory. Consequently, a change of venue is a "pipe dream" in this context, as it would not solve the problem of a potentially tainted jury pool. The Trial Court correctly identified that the only viable path to ensuring fairness is through rigorous voir dire and, if necessary, a bench trial, rather than a physical relocation of the proceedings.

**D. The Cultural Bias Argument Is Insufficient for Presumed Prejudice**

Finally, the defense argues that the Trial Court dismissed the cultural bias argument too readily, pointing to letters to the editor and the "media monopoly" in the Territory. While the Court acknowledges that bias against foreign nationals is a serious concern, the record evidence of such bias remains limited to isolated letters and editorial content. Precedent requires that the publicity be pervasive throughout the community to support a finding of presumed prejudice. See *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 729 (1961). The existence of some prejudiced viewpoints, without evidence that they have permeated the jury pool to the extent of precluding a fair trial, does not justify overturning the Trial Court’s discretion.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Trial Court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motions for change of venue. The defendants failed to demonstrate the "exceptional" circumstances necessary for presumed prejudice, and the practical realities of the American Samoa judicial system preclude a physical venue change as a remedy. The Trial Court’s proposed remedies of a comprehensive voir dire questionnaire and individual questioning are sufficient to protect the defendants’ rights to a fair trial.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Appellate Court for a final determination on the review of the Trial Court’s Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants, Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, appeal the denial, arguing that the Trial Court applied an unduly rigid standard for presumed prejudice and failed to account for the unique vulnerabilities of the jury pool in American Samoa. Having reviewed the rebuttals of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms the denial of the motion for change of venue.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The denial of a motion for change of venue is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. The burden rests heavily on the defendant to demonstrate that pre-trial publicity prevents the assembly of a fair and impartial jury. See *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 727 (1961). To warrant a change of venue prior to voir dire, the defendant must show presumed prejudice, a standard reserved for "extreme situations" where the publicity is so pervasive and prejudicial that it renders the trial a "hollow formality." *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794, 799 (1975); *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Defendants Failed to Establish Presumed Prejudice**

The defense argues that the Trial Court erred by relying on a quantitative "oversaturation" standard, asserting instead that a qualitative assessment of the publicity's inflammatory nature should suffice. While the defense correctly notes that the qualitative nature of publicity is relevant, the legal threshold for presumed prejudice remains exceptionally high.

The defense relies on *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993), and *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985), to argue that the Trial Court was too rigid. However, *Coleman* itself involved a review of 1,537 articles over 18 months, and the court still found no presumed prejudice. Here, the defendants have submitted only six newspaper articles. While the defense argues that the "media monopoly" in American Samoa amplifies the impact of these six articles, the record lacks evidence demonstrating that this coverage reached the level of community saturation seen in *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 726 (1963), where a defendant’s televised confession was broadcast to the entire community three times.

The defense contends that the headlines—using terms like "prostitution ring" and "sex ring"—are inherently pejorative and that the use of the word "alleged" does not neutralize this prejudice. The Court acknowledges that these terms are serious. However, the articles themselves largely reproduced testimony and arguments from open court, which serves to inform rather than inflame. See *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 363 (1966) (noting that press coverage of judicial proceedings is not inherently prejudicial). The qualitative impact of these articles, while potentially damaging to the defendants' reputation, does not rise to the level of "inherent prejudice" required to presume that a jury cannot be fair. See *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 363 (6th Cir. 1999).

**B. The Cultural Bias Argument Is Insufficient for Presumed Prejudice**

The defense argues that the Trial Court improperly dismissed evidence of cultural bias, specifically letters to the editor expressing prejudice against foreign nationals. The defense asserts that in a "culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not born here," these letters reflect a broader community sentiment.

While the Court shares the Trial Court’s condemnation of narrow-minded and prejudiced opinions, the existence of isolated letters to the editor does not equate to the pervasive community prejudice required for presumed prejudice. As the Supreme Court held in *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 729 (1961), presumed prejudice must be shown to "pervade the community." The record here contains no evidence of widespread public sentiment or demonstrations of bias beyond these limited expressions. The defense’s argument that the "media monopoly" amplifies these letters is speculative without evidence of their actual impact on the jury pool. Therefore, the Trial Court did not abuse its discretion in determining that this evidence was insufficient to establish presumed prejudice.

**C. The Unique Circumstances of American Samoa Do Not Warrant a Change of Venue**

The defense reiterates its argument that the compact social structure of American Samoa makes a fair trial unlikely and that the Trial Court’s categorical rejection of venue change was error. The defense cites *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977), to argue that the practicality of jury trials does not negate the need for a fair trial.

The Court agrees with the prosecution that *King* supports the proposition that jury trials are practical in small island communities, but it does not address the remedy for prejudice. The more pertinent authority is *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987), which acknowledges that a change of venue is unsuitable in territories with a single jury roll. As the Trial Court correctly noted, A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 only allows for a physical relocation of the court for convenience; it does not allow for the importation of a neutral jury pool. Because all registered voters in American Samoa are on the single jury roll, moving the trial to a different physical location would not change the composition of the jury. Consequently, a change of venue would be a futile remedy that would not address the defendants' concerns about bias.

**D. The Proposed Remedies Are Constitutionally Adequate**

The defense argues that the Trial Court’s proposed voir dire questionnaire is inadequate because it restricts inquiries into anticipated instructions, hypothetical verdicts, and substantive arguments. The defense contends these restrictions prevent the court from probing subtle biases.

The Court finds that these restrictions are standard and necessary to avoid prejudicing the jury before the trial begins. The Trial Court’s order allows for a questionnaire in both English and Samoan and permits individual voir dire if necessary. These are the precise "alternative remedies" endorsed by *Moreno*, 815 F.2d at 731, when a change of venue is impractical. The defense’s assertion that voir dire cannot cure prejudice in a small community is a policy argument that, while noted, does not override the legal requirement that presumed prejudice must be proven before a trial can be halted or moved. See *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 273 (noting that voir dire is the primary mechanism for gauging public sentiment).

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Trial Court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motions for change of venue. The defendants failed to demonstrate the "exceptional" circumstances necessary for presumed prejudice, and the practical realities of the American Samoa judicial system preclude a physical venue change as an effective remedy. The Trial Court’s proposed remedies of a comprehensive voir dire questionnaire and individual questioning are sufficient to protect the defendants’ rights to a fair trial.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the Trial Court’s Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is affirmed. The parties are directed to proceed with the preparation for trial in accordance with the Trial Court’s instructions regarding voir dire.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Appellate Court on the review of the Trial Court’s Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue. The defendants, Wei Ki Fang, Siituu Sanerivi, and Wei Li Fang, appeal the denial, arguing that the Trial Court applied an unduly rigid standard for presumed prejudice and failed to account for the unique vulnerabilities of the jury pool in American Samoa. Having thoroughly reviewed the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms the denial of the motion for change of venue.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The denial of a motion for change of venue is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. The burden rests heavily on the defendant to demonstrate that pre-trial publicity prevents the assembly of a fair and impartial jury. See *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 727 (1961). To warrant a change of venue prior to voir dire, the defendant must show presumed prejudice, a standard reserved for "exceptional situations" where the publicity is so pervasive and prejudicial that it renders the trial a "hollow formality." *Murphy v. Florida*, 421 U.S. 794, 799 (1975); *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 727 (1963).

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Defendants Failed to Establish Presumed Prejudice**

The defense argues that the Trial Court erred by relying on a quantitative "oversaturation" standard, asserting instead that a qualitative assessment of the publicity's inflammatory nature should suffice. While the defense correctly notes that the qualitative nature of publicity is relevant, the legal threshold for presumed prejudice remains exceptionally high.

The defense relies on *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. 269, 272 (D. Vt. 1993), and *Coleman v. Kemp*, 778 F.2d 1487, 1490 (11th Cir. 1985), to argue that the Trial Court was too rigid. However, *Coleman* itself involved a review of 1,537 articles over 18 months, and the court still found no presumed prejudice. Here, the defendants have submitted only six newspaper articles. While the defense argues that the "media monopoly" in American Samoa amplifies the impact of these six articles, the record lacks evidence demonstrating that this coverage reached the level of community saturation seen in *Rideau v. Louisiana*, 373 U.S. 723, 726 (1963), where a defendant’s televised confession was broadcast to the entire community three times.

The defense contends that the headlines—using terms like "prostitution ring" and "sex ring"—are inherently pejorative and that the use of the word "alleged" does not neutralize this prejudice. The Court acknowledges that these terms are serious. However, the articles themselves largely reproduced testimony and arguments from open court, which serves to inform rather than inflame. See *Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333, 363 (1966) (noting that press coverage of judicial proceedings is not inherently prejudicial). The qualitative impact of these articles, while potentially damaging to the defendants' reputation, does not rise to the level of "inherent prejudice" required to presume that a jury cannot be fair. See *Nevers v. Killinger*, 169 F.3d 352, 363 (6th Cir. 1999).

**B. The Cultural Bias Argument Is Insufficient for Presumed Prejudice**

The defense argues that the Trial Court improperly dismissed evidence of cultural bias, specifically letters to the editor expressing prejudice against foreign nationals. The defense asserts that in a "culture which admits but never really accepts people who are not born here," these letters reflect a broader community sentiment.

While the Court shares the Trial Court’s condemnation of narrow-minded and prejudiced opinions, the existence of isolated letters to the editor does not equate to the pervasive community prejudice required for presumed prejudice. As the Supreme Court held in *Irvin v. Dowd*, 366 U.S. 717, 729 (1961), presumed prejudice must be shown to "pervade the community." The record here contains no evidence of widespread public sentiment or demonstrations of bias beyond these limited expressions. The defense’s argument that the "media monopoly" amplifies these letters is speculative without evidence of their actual impact on the jury pool. Therefore, the Trial Court did not abuse its discretion in determining that this evidence was insufficient to establish presumed prejudice.

**C. The Unique Circumstances of American Samoa Do Not Warrant a Change of Venue**

The defense reiterates its argument that the compact social structure of American Samoa makes a fair trial unlikely and that the Trial Court’s categorical rejection of venue change was error. The defense cites *King v. Andrus*, 452 F. Supp. 11, 17 (D.D.C. 1977), to argue that the practicality of jury trials does not negate the need for a fair trial.

The Court agrees with the prosecution that *King* supports the proposition that jury trials are practical in small island communities, but it does not address the remedy for prejudice. The more pertinent authority is *United States v. Moreno*, 815 F.2d 725, 731 (1st Cir. 1987), which acknowledges that a change of venue is unsuitable in territories with a single jury roll. As the Trial Court correctly noted, A.S.C.A. § 46.0601 only allows for a physical relocation of the court for convenience; it does not allow for the importation of a neutral jury pool. Because all registered voters in American Samoa are on the single jury roll, moving the trial to a different physical location would not change the composition of the jury. Consequently, a change of venue would be a futile remedy that would not address the defendants' concerns about bias.

**D. The Proposed Remedies Are Constitutionally Adequate**

The defense argues that the Trial Court’s proposed voir dire questionnaire is inadequate because it restricts inquiries into anticipated instructions, hypothetical verdicts, and substantive arguments. The defense contends these restrictions prevent the court from probing subtle biases.

The Court finds that these restrictions are standard and necessary to avoid prejudicing the jury before the trial begins. The Trial Court’s order allows for a questionnaire in both English and Samoan and permits individual voir dire if necessary. These are the precise "alternative remedies" endorsed by *Moreno*, 815 F.2d at 731, when a change of venue is impractical. The defense’s assertion that voir dire cannot cure prejudice in a small community is a policy argument that, while noted, does not override the legal requirement that presumed prejudice must be proven before a trial can be halted or moved. See *United States v. Washington*, 813 F. Supp. at 273 (noting that voir dire is the primary mechanism for gauging public sentiment).

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Trial Court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motions for change of venue. The defendants failed to demonstrate the "exceptional" circumstances necessary for presumed prejudice, and the practical realities of the American Samoa judicial system preclude a physical venue change as an effective remedy. The Trial Court’s proposed remedies of a comprehensive voir dire questionnaire and individual questioning are sufficient to protect the defendants’ rights to a fair trial.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the Trial Court’s Order Denying Motions for Change of Venue is affirmed. The parties are directed to proceed with the preparation for trial in accordance with the Trial Court’s instructions regarding voir dire.
