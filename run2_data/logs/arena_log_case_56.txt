### A. The Dispositive Agreement Did Not Waive the Right to Disclosure of the Confidential Informant for Purposes of the Probable Cause Determination

The prosecution's central contention that the "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress operated as a blanket waiver of all issues is legally unsound. The prosecution's reliance on United States v. Meza is misplaced. In Meza, the Ninth Circuit addressed waiver in the context of a defendant who explicitly agreed to forego certain defenses as part of a plea bargain. Here, however, the appellant never agreed to waive his challenge to the probable cause determination; to the contrary, his motion to suppress was premised on challenging precisely that probable cause determination. The confidentiality of the informant goes to the very heart of whether probable cause existed.

### B. The Informant's Identity Was Directly Relevant to the Motion to Suppress

The defense counsel's arguments establish that the request for disclosure was not limited to issues of "guilt or innocence" as the prosecution suggests, but was directly relevant to the probable cause determination underlying the suppression motion. The appellant argued that the confidential informant "set him up," that the informant was with him on the day in question, and that the informant's belongings were found in his car - all evidence bearing on the informant's reliability and credibility.

In Roviaro v. United States, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), the Supreme Court held that the government's privilege to withhold informant identity must yield when the informant's testimony may be relevant and helpful to the defense or essential to a fair determination of guilt or innocence. While Roviaro was decided in the trial context, its reasoning applies with equal force to suppression hearings where the informant's reliability is central to the probable cause inquiry.

The trial court in this case acknowledged it had heard testimony regarding the informant's reliability based on past tips. However, the defense was entitled to test that reliability through disclosure, particularly where "new information" regarding the informant's alleged involvement in the offense had just been received. The court of appeals erred in concluding that the dispositive agreement precluded this inquiry.

### C. The Appellant Preserved This Issue for Appeal

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The court has reviewed the opening arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in refusing to order the disclosure of a confidential informant’s identity after the defense re-urged its motion based on new evidence, given the existence of a "dispositive" plea agreement regarding the motion to suppress.

**I. Analysis of the Dispositive Plea Agreement and Waiver**

The prosecution correctly argues that the parties entered into a binding agreement where the motion to suppress would be dispositive. The record reflects that defense counsel explicitly confirmed this understanding, stating, "It was. It is clear. I am not trying to... [use] trickery... It was a dispositive Motion to Suppress." Under the principles outlined in *United States v. Meza*, 850 F.3d 1062, 1068 (9th Cir. 2017), a defendant who enters a guilty plea under a dispositive motion agreement waives all non-jurisdictional defenses not directly tied to the motion’s resolution. Furthermore, *United States v. Bownes*, 405 F.3d 634, 639 (7th Cir. 2005), establishes that a guilty plea constitutes a voluntary relinquishment of all defenses not preserved by the plea’s terms.

The prosecution contends that because the defense sought disclosure to prove "entrapment" or challenge "guilt or innocence," these issues were waived by the dispositive nature of the agreement. The court agrees with the prosecution that a dispositive motion agreement generally precludes the relitigation of guilt or innocence. If the defense sought the informant's identity solely to argue entrapment at a trial that will not occur, the waiver argument would be dispositive.

**II. Relevance of the Informant’s Identity to the Suppression Motion**

However, the defense persuasively argues that the informant’s identity and reliability were not merely collateral issues of guilt or innocence, but were central to the probable cause determination that was the subject of the suppression hearing. The record indicates that the defense received "new information" regarding the confidential informant, specifically that the informant’s belongings were found in the appellant’s car and that the informant was with him on the day in question. Defense counsel argued, "If she set him up, it goes to her reliability."

The legal standard for disclosing a confidential informant’s identity was established in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957). In *Roviaro*, the Supreme Court held that "the government's privilege to withhold the disclosure of an informant's identity must give way to the defendant's right to a fair trial when the informant's testimony may be relevant and helpful to the defense or essential to a fair determination of guilt or innocence." While *Roviaro* is often cited in the context of a trial, its underlying principle applies to suppression hearings where the informant’s credibility is the sole basis for probable cause.

Probable cause to search or arrest cannot be based solely on information from an unreliable source. If the defense possesses evidence suggesting the informant fabricated the tip or was personally involved in the crime ("set him up"), this evidence directly attacks the reliability of the tip and, consequently, the probable cause finding. The trial judge acknowledged the relevance of reliability, stating, "I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips." By denying the defense the opportunity to challenge this reliability with newly discovered evidence suggesting bias or fabrication, the trial court may have prevented a complete evaluation of the probable cause issue.

**III. Scope of the Waiver vs. Evidentiary Hearing**

The distinction drawn by the prosecution, relying on *United States v. Meza*, 850 F.3d 1062 (9th Cir. 2017), is that the dispositive agreement waived all issues outside the suppression motion. The court finds this interpretation to be overly broad when applied to the evidentiary foundation of the motion itself. The defense did not seek to litigate the case anew; rather, they sought to introduce specific evidence regarding the informant's credibility to the judge deciding the dispositive motion.

The defense’s argument that the informant’s reliability is "important even on a Motion to Suppress" is legally sound. A waiver of the right to a trial does not inherently waive the right to fully litigate the specific motion that determines the case's outcome. The "new information" regarding the informant's belongings and presence with the appellant goes to the heart of whether the officers had probable cause. Therefore, the issue falls within the scope of the suppression motion rather than outside it.

**IV. Conclusion**

The prosecution’s argument regarding the finality of the plea agreement is well-taken regarding general defenses of guilt or innocence. However, the defense has demonstrated that the informant’s identity is material to the probable cause determination, which was the sole subject of the dispositive motion. The application of *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), requires the court to balance the public interest in protecting the informant against the defendant’s right to prepare a defense. Here, the defense has shown a specific need for disclosure to challenge the informant's credibility, which is directly pertinent to the suppression hearing.

Accordingly, the defense’s argument is more persuasive. The dispositive nature of the plea agreement does not bar the disclosure of the informant when such disclosure is necessary to challenge the very probable cause that formed the basis of the dispositive motion.

**RULING**

The preliminary ruling is in favor of the Appellant. The court of appeals erred in rejecting the request to disclose the confidential informant’s identity. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion, specifically to allow the trial court to determine whether the informant’s identity must be disclosed to allow the defense to adequately challenge the reliability of the informant and the existence of probable cause.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the prosecution's closing statement. ## I. THE DISPOSITIVE AGREEMENT DID NOT WAIVE THE RIGHT TO CHALLENGE PROBABLE CAUSE

As the trial judge acknowledged, "I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." The defense sought to rebut this finding of reliability with new evidence that the informant was with the defendant on the day in question and that her belongings were found in his car—evidence suggesting she may have "set him up."

This is not a guilt-or-innocence defense. This is a direct challenge to the *legality of the search*. If the informant fabricated the tip or was personally involved in the crime, the tip lacks indicia of reliability, and any probable cause derived from that tip is undermined. The defense's argument goes to the very heart of the suppression motion, not to some collateral guilt-or-innocence issue.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Meza*, 850 F.3d 1062 (9th Cir. 2017), is misplaced. In *Meza*, the defendant agreed to a dispositive motion framework and then attempted to raise an entrapment defense—a classic guilt-or-ininnocence issue wholly unrelated to the suppression analysis. Here, by contrast, the defense does not seek to raise entrapment as an affirmative defense to the charge. ## II. ROVIARO v. UNITED STATES APPLIES TO SUPPRESSION HEARINGS

The prosecution contends that *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), applies only to trials, not to suppression hearings. This argument contradicts the Supreme Court's own language and ignores the purpose of the privilege itself.

In *Roviaro*, the Court held that the government's privilege to withhold an informant's identity "must give way to the defendant's right to a fair trial when the informant's testimony may be relevant and helpful to the defense or essential to a fair determination of guilt or innocence." 353 U.S. at 60-61. The prosecution focuses on the phrase "fair determination of guilt or innocence" and argues that because there was no trial, Roviaro is inapplicable.

However, this reading ignores the first part of the holding: the informant's testimony may be "relevant and helpful to the defense." The privilege to withhold an informant's identity is not absolute; it yields when disclosure is relevant to any material issue in the case. Here, the informant's reliability was the central issue in the suppression hearing. The defense could not effectively challenge that reliability without knowing the informant's identity.

The prosecution's argument proves too much. Under its logic, the government could withhold an informant's identity in any case where the defendant agrees to forgo trial—even if the informant's tip was the sole basis for the search. This would eviscerate the protections afforded by *Roviaro* and render the privilege to withhold identity absolute in precisely the cases where it matters most.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument conflicts with the fundamental principle that suppression hearings are adjudicative proceedings where the defendant has the right to challenge the legality of the search. As the Supreme Court held in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), probable cause requires a "fair probability" of criminal activity based on the totality of circumstances. When the totality of circumstances rests entirely on an informant's tip, the reliability of that informant is the critical factor. The defense cannot be precluded from challenging that reliability simply because it agreed to forgo a trial on the merits.

Under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. at 238, probable cause analysis requires the court to assess the informant's reliability, including "the basis of the informant's knowledge" and the "veracity or reliability of the informant." The prosecution is wrong to suggest that "whether the informant 'set him up' is irrelevant to whether officers had probable cause to search." To the contrary, if the informant fabricated the tip or was personally involved in the crime, the tip lacks the indicia of reliability required for probable cause.

The prosecution argues that "probable cause exists when officers reasonably believe a crime occurred, regardless of whether the tip was fabricated." This is a misstatement of the law. Probable cause requires not merely a belief that a crime occurred, but a reasonable belief based on reliable information. An informant's tip that is fabricated or motivated by bias does not provide the reliable information required for probable cause. As the Court stated in *Gates*, "the informant's veracity is relevant but not dispositive." The defense seeks to challenge that veracity—not to relitigate guilt or innocence, but to show that the tip was unreliable and therefore insufficient for probable cause.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Meza* for the proposition that entrapment is a "classic defense of guilt or innocence" is unavailing. The defense here does not seek to raise entrapment as an affirmative defense. Rather, it seeks to show that the informant's tip was unreliable, thereby negating probable cause. This is a threshold challenge to the search's legality, not a defense on the merits.

The prosecution's argument that "the law does not permit a party to unilaterally modify a binding agreement based on later-discovered information" mischaracterizes the defense's position. **FINAL RULING**

**I. Introduction**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in refusing to order the disclosure of a confidential informant’s identity after the defense re-urged its motion based on new evidence, specifically in the context of a "dispositive" plea agreement regarding the motion to suppress. The prosecution argues that the plea agreement constituted a binding waiver of all rights to disclosure because the defense sought to litigate issues of "guilt or innocence" rather than the specific suppression issue. The defense counters that the informant's reliability is the foundation of the probable cause determination and that new evidence challenging this reliability is directly tied to the resolution of the suppression motion. After careful consideration, the Court finds that the defense’s arguments are more persuasive and that the trial court’s ruling must be reversed.

**II. The Scope of the Waiver Under the Dispositive Agreement**

The prosecution relies heavily on the principle that plea agreements are binding contracts that must be upheld. Citing *United States v. Meza*, 850 F.3d 1062, 1068 (9th Cir. 2017), the prosecution argues that a defendant who agrees to a dispositive motion framework waives all non-jurisdictional defenses not directly tied to the motion’s resolution. Similarly, the prosecution cites *United States v. Bownes*, 405 F.3d 634, 639 (7th Cir. 2005), for the proposition that a guilty plea constitutes a voluntary relinquishment of all defenses not preserved by the plea’s terms.

While the Court agrees that plea agreements must be honored, the prosecution’s interpretation of the waiver in this case is overly broad. The defense did not seek to relitigate the case or introduce general defenses of guilt or innocence, such as entrapment, to exculpate the client from the crime itself. Rather, the defense sought to introduce evidence specifically regarding the informant's credibility to challenge the probable cause finding. The record indicates that the defense received "new information"—that the informant’s belongings were found in the appellant’s car and that the informant was with him on the day in question—after the agreement was made but before the ruling.

The waiver of the right to a trial does not inherently waive the right to fully litigate the specific motion that determines the case's outcome. The agreement was that the *outcome* of the suppression ruling would be dispositive, not that the defense was restricted from presenting relevant evidence on the very issue being litigated. The defense’s challenge to the informant’s reliability is not a collateral issue; it is an attack on the *foundation* of the State’s probable cause argument. Therefore, the waiver recognized in *United States v. Meza*, 850 F.3d 1062 (9th Cir. 2017), does not extend to waiving the right to challenge the evidentiary basis of the dispositive motion itself.

**III. The Relevance of the "New Information" to Probable Cause**

The prosecution characterizes the "new information" as "quintessential guilt/innocence evidence" and argues that it is irrelevant to probable cause. The prosecution contends that under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), probable cause requires only a "fair probability" of criminal activity based on the totality of the circumstances, and that this standard is met even if the tip was fabricated.

This interpretation of *Illinois v. Gates* is incorrect. While *Gates* established a flexible "totality of the circumstances" test, it did not eliminate the requirement that the information provided to the police be reliable. If an informant fabricates a tip or is personally involved in setting up the defendant to frame them, the tip lacks the indicia of reliability necessary to establish probable cause. The defense’s new evidence—that the informant’s belongings were found in the car and she was with the defendant—goes directly to the informant’s veracity and basis of knowledge. If the informant "set him up," the tip is unreliable, and any probable cause derived from that tip is undermined. This is not merely a defense to the charge of possession; it is a direct challenge to the legality of the search. The trial judge’s reliance on past tips to establish reliability, without allowing the defense to rebut that finding with new evidence of potential fabrication or bias, prevented a complete evaluation of the probable cause issue.

**IV. Application of *Roviaro* to Suppression Hearings**

The prosecution argues that *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), is inapplicable because the plea agreement negated any "fair determination of guilt or innocence" and no trial was held. This argument misunderstands the scope of *Roviaro*. The Supreme Court held that the government's privilege to withhold an informant's identity must give way when the informant's testimony is "relevant and helpful to the defense or essential to a fair determination of guilt or innocence." *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 60-61.

This principle applies with equal force to a suppression hearing where the informant's credibility is the sole basis for probable cause. Because there was no trial, the only issue before the court was whether the officers had probable cause to search and arrest. The informant's reliability was therefore not a collateral issue—it was the central issue. The defense cannot be precluded from challenging the very foundation of the State's probable cause argument simply because they agreed to forgo a trial on guilt. The prosecution’s logic would allow the State to rely on an unverified, potentially fabricated tip to secure a conviction via a plea agreement, effectively insulating the tip from any judicial scrutiny. This would eviscerate the protections afforded by *Roviaro*.

**V. Conclusion**

The prosecution’s argument regarding the finality of the plea agreement is well-taken regarding general defenses of guilt or innocence. However, the defense has demonstrated that the informant’s identity is material to the probable cause determination, which was the sole subject of the dispositive motion. The application of *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), requires the court to balance the public interest in protecting the informant against the defendant’s right to prepare a defense. Here, the defense has shown a specific need for disclosure to challenge the informant's credibility, which is directly pertinent to the suppression hearing. The waiver argument advanced in *United States v. Meza*, 850 F.3d 1062 (9th Cir. 2017), does not extend to waiving the right to challenge the evidentiary basis of the dispositive motion itself.

Accordingly, the defense’s argument is more persuasive. The dispositive nature of the plea agreement does not bar the disclosure of the informant when such disclosure is necessary to challenge the very probable cause that formed the basis of the dispositive motion.

**RULING**

The judgment of the court of appeals is reversed. The trial court erred in rejecting the request to disclose the confidential informant’s identity. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion, specifically to allow the trial court to determine whether the informant’s identity must be disclosed to allow the defense to adequately challenge the reliability of the informant and the existence of probable cause.
