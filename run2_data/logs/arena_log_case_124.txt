**PER CURIAM.**

This case comes before the Court on the State’s petition for review of a decision by the Court of Appeals reversing three convictions of second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals held, sua sponte, that the trial court committed “plain error” because there was no evidence of malice on the part of the defendant. After careful consideration of the record and the arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense, we conclude that the Court of Appeals erred in its determination that the evidence was insufficient to establish malice. Accordingly, we reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Murder in the second degree is defined as the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983). While an intent to kill is not a necessary element, the crime requires "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from circumstances demonstrating a reckless disregard for human life. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978).

**II. SUFFICIENCY OF EVIDENCE OF MALICE**

The central issue is whether the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to withstand a motion to dismiss or to support a finding of plain error due to a lack of evidence. The Court of Appeals concluded that there was no evidence of malice. We disagree.

The State presented evidence that the defendant spent the afternoon drinking, was described as loud and rowdy, and was refused service at Smokey’s Lounge. Following an altercation, the defendant entered his vehicle and drove onto Highway 311. Witness Walden Ball testified that he observed the defendant driving erratically, squealing tires, and passing his vehicle in a “no passing” zone. The defendant subsequently struck a motorcycle from behind, forcing it from the road, and continued driving. Hospital records indicated the defendant had a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent.

This evidence, when viewed in the light most favorable to the State, is sufficient to support an inference of malice. Under State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978), malice may be inferred where an act is performed in reckless disregard of the safety of others. The defendant’s decision to operate a motor vehicle while severely intoxicated, coupled with his aggressive driving maneuvers—passing illegally and striking another vehicle—constitutes an intentional act evincing a wanton disregard for human life. As held in State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983), such acts are sufficient to show malice if they proximately cause death.

The defendant argues that the evidence was insufficient because he lacked the requisite intent due to unconsciousness. The defense relies on the defendant’s lack of memory and a head injury sustained during the tavern altercation to suggest he was unconscious at the time of the accident. However, the trial court refused to admit evidence regarding the defense of unconsciousness. While the exclusion of this evidence may constitute reversible error—a matter we address below—it does not negate the sufficiency of the State’s evidence. The State’s evidence, standing alone, was legally sufficient to permit the jury to infer malice. The Court of Appeals erred in concluding that no evidence of malice existed in the record.

**III. THE DEFENSE OF UNCONSCIOUSNESS**

The defendant contends that the trial court committed reversible error by refusing to allow evidence of unconsciousness and by refusing to instruct the jury on that defense. The defense cites State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E. 2d 348 (1975), for the proposition that evidence of unconsciousness must be submitted to the jury when supported by the record, and State v. Hammonds, 241 N.C. 226, 84 S.E. 2d 793 (1954), for the requirement that jury instructions cover every theory supported by the evidence.

The record indicates that the defendant was struck in the head, fell, and subsequently had no memory of the events leading to the accident. This evidence, if believed, could support a defense of unconsciousness. The trial court’s refusal to permit this evidence or to instruct the jury on it may have deprived the defendant of a fair opportunity to present his theory of the case. However, this evidentiary ruling is distinct from the question of whether the State presented sufficient evidence of malice to survive a motion to dismiss. The Court of Appeals’ reliance on a perceived lack of evidence of malice to reverse the convictions was misplaced. The proper course would have been to evaluate the trial court’s exclusion of the unconsciousness evidence as a preserved error or plain error affecting the defendant’s substantial rights, rather than dismissing the State’s case as legally insufficient.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Court of Appeals erred in finding plain error based on a lack of evidence of malice. The State’s evidence regarding the defendant’s intoxication and reckless driving was sufficient to support the jury’s finding of malice under the principles established in State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983), and State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). The defendant’s arguments regarding the exclusion of evidence concerning unconsciousness present valid grounds for appellate review, but they do not support the conclusion that the State’s evidence was legally insufficient.

**PER CURIAM.**

This case is before us on the State’s petition for discretionary review of a decision by the Court of Appeals reversing three convictions of second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals held, sua sponte, that the trial court committed “plain error” because there was no evidence of malice on the part of the defendant. After reviewing the rebuttal arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, we reaffirm our conclusion that the Court of Appeals erred. The State presented sufficient evidence of malice to withstand a motion to dismiss, and the Court of Appeals improperly substituted its judgment for that of the jury. We therefore reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**I. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The central issue on appeal is whether the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to support a conviction for second-degree murder. Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983). While an intent to kill is not a necessary element, the crime requires "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from circumstances demonstrating a reckless disregard for human life. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978).

The defense argues in its rebuttal that the evidence was insufficient because the defendant’s conduct constituted mere negligence rather than an "intentional act" sufficient to show malice. The defense contends that State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983), requires a purposeful act, and that driving while intoxicated, without more, is negligence. We disagree.

Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, the record shows that the defendant had a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent, was observed driving erratically with squealing tires, passed another vehicle in a “no passing” zone, and struck a motorcycle from behind, forcing it from the road. This evidence supports an inference that the defendant was engaged in a course of conduct demonstrating a reckless disregard for human life. As held in State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978), "malice may be inferred from the circumstances of the act" when a defendant engages in conduct showing "wanton disregard for human life." The defendant’s decision to operate a vehicle while severely impaired and to aggressively pass in a prohibited zone constitutes an intentional act of recklessness sufficient to imply malice under State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983).

The defense attempts to distinguish State v. Wilkerson and State v. Robbins by noting that those cases involved the use of deadly weapons (firearms and knives) rather than an automobile. However, the principle that malice may be inferred from reckless disregard for safety is not limited to specific instrumentalities. A motor vehicle, operated in the manner described by the evidence, can be just as deadly as a firearm. The evidence here was sufficient to permit the jury to find that the defendant acted with malice.

**II. THE DEFENSE OF UNCONSCIOUSNESS**

The defense argues vigorously that the trial court committed reversible error by excluding evidence of the defendant’s unconsciousness and refusing to instruct the jury on that defense. The defense relies on State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E. 2d 348 (1975), and State v. Hammonds, 241 N.C. 226, 84 S.E. 2d 793 (1954), for the proposition that the trial court erred by preventing the jury from considering whether the defendant was unconscious at the time of the accident due to a head injury sustained during the tavern altercation.

We acknowledge the force of the defense’s argument regarding the exclusion of evidence. Under State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E. 2d 348 (1975), if there is substantial evidence of unconsciousness, the trial court must instruct the jury on that defense. The record indicates the defendant was struck in the head, fell, and had no memory of subsequent events. This evidence, if believed, could support a defense of unconsciousness. The trial court’s refusal to admit this evidence or to instruct the jury may have deprived the defendant of a fair trial.

However, the error in excluding this evidence is distinct from the question of whether the State presented sufficient evidence of malice to survive a motion to dismiss. The defense contends that the exclusion of the unconsciousness evidence rendered the State’s proof legally insufficient. We do not agree. The State’s evidence, standing alone, was legally sufficient to permit the jury to infer malice. The existence of a potential defense does not negate the sufficiency of the State’s prima facie case. As the prosecution correctly notes, the proper analysis for the exclusion of evidence is whether the error was harmless or reversible under State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 600, 299 S.E. 2d 867 (1983), not whether the State’s evidence was nonexistent.

The Court of Appeals erred by reversing the convictions on the ground that there was "no evidence of malice." This was not the correct procedural mechanism to address the trial court’s ruling on the unconsciousness defense. The Court of Appeals should have evaluated the trial court’s evidentiary ruling as a preserved error or as plain error affecting the defendant’s substantial rights, rather than dismissing the State’s case as legally insufficient.

**III. PLAIN ERROR REVIEW**

The defense argues that the Court of Appeals properly identified "plain error" under State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 600, 299 S.E. 2d 867 (1983), because the absence of malice was a fundamental defect. Plain error review is appropriate only when the error is "fundamental" and "obvious." Here, the error identified by the Court of Appeals—the lack of evidence of malice—was not an error at all. Because the State presented sufficient evidence of malice, the Court of Appeals’ application of the plain error doctrine was misplaced.

The Court of Appeals erred in reversing the convictions based on a finding that there was no evidence of malice. The State’s evidence regarding the defendant’s intoxication and reckless driving was sufficient to support the jury’s finding of malice under the principles established in State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983), and State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). The defendant’s arguments regarding the exclusion of evidence concerning unconsciousness present valid grounds for appellate review, but they do not support the conclusion that the State’s evidence was legally insufficient. We reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case to that court for consideration of the defendant’s remaining assignments of error regarding the exclusion of the unconsciousness evidence.

The "wanton disregard" standard applies to deliberate acts of aggression, not to the operation of a vehicle while impaired. v.
**DEFENDANT**

**ON APPEAL FROM THE COURT OF APPEALS**

**NO. 84A85**

**Filed: [Current Date]**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case is before us on the State’s petition for discretionary review of a decision by the Court of Appeals reversing three convictions of second-degree murder. The Court of Appeals held, sua sponte, that the trial court committed “plain error” because there was no evidence of malice on the part of the defendant. After reviewing the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, we reaffirm our conclusion that the Court of Appeals erred. The State presented sufficient evidence of malice to withstand a motion to dismiss, and the Court of Appeals improperly substituted its judgment for that of the jury. We therefore reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for further consideration consistent with this opinion.

**I. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The central issue on appeal is whether the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to support a conviction for second-degree murder. Murder in the second degree is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice but without premeditation and deliberation. State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983). While an intent to kill is not a necessary element, the crime requires "some intentional act sufficient to show malice and which proximately causes death." State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983). Malice may be implied from circumstances demonstrating a reckless disregard for human life. State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978).

The defense argues in its closing that the evidence was insufficient because the defendant’s conduct constituted mere negligence rather than an "intentional act" sufficient to show malice. The defense contends that State v. Lang, 309 N.C. 512, 308 S.E. 2d 317 (1983), requires a purposeful act, and that driving while intoxicated, without more, is negligence. We disagree.

Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the State, the record shows that the defendant had a blood alcohol level between .24 and .32 percent, was observed driving erratically with squealing tires, passed another vehicle in a “no passing” zone, and struck a motorcycle from behind, forcing it from the road. This evidence supports an inference that the defendant was engaged in a course of conduct demonstrating a reckless disregard for human life. As held in State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978), "malice may be inferred from the circumstances of the act" when a defendant engages in conduct showing "wanton disregard for human life." The defendant’s decision to operate a vehicle while severely impaired and to aggressively pass in a prohibited zone constitutes an intentional act of recklessness sufficient to imply malice under State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983).

The defense attempts to distinguish State v. Wilkerson and State v. Robbins by noting that those cases involved the use of deadly weapons (firearms and knives) rather than an automobile. However, the principle that malice may be inferred from reckless disregard for safety is not limited to specific instrumentalities. A motor vehicle, operated in the manner described by the evidence, can be just as deadly as a firearm. The evidence here was sufficient to permit the jury to find that the defendant acted with malice.

**II. THE DEFENSE OF UNCONSCIOUSNESS**

The defense argues vigorously that the trial court committed reversible error by excluding evidence of the defendant’s unconsciousness and refusing to instruct the jury on that defense. The defense relies on State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E. 2d 348 (1975), and State v. Hammonds, 241 N.C. 226, 84 S.E. 2d 793 (1954), for the proposition that the trial court erred by preventing the jury from considering whether the defendant was unconscious at the time of the accident due to a head injury sustained during the tavern altercation.

We acknowledge the force of the defense’s argument regarding the exclusion of evidence. Under State v. Caddell, 287 N.C. 266, 215 S.E. 2d 348 (1975), if there is substantial evidence of unconsciousness, the trial court must instruct the jury on that defense. The record indicates the defendant was struck in the head, fell, and had no memory of subsequent events. This evidence, if believed, could support a defense of unconsciousness. The trial court’s refusal to admit this evidence or to instruct the jury may have deprived the defendant of a fair trial.

However, the error in excluding this evidence is distinct from the question of whether the State presented sufficient evidence of malice to survive a motion to dismiss. The defense contends that the exclusion of the unconsciousness evidence rendered the State’s proof legally insufficient. We do not agree. The State’s evidence, standing alone, was legally sufficient to permit the jury to infer malice. The existence of a potential defense does not negate the sufficiency of the State’s prima facie case. As the prosecution correctly notes, the proper analysis for the exclusion of evidence is whether the error was harmless or reversible under State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 600, 299 S.E. 2d 867 (1983), not whether the State’s evidence was nonexistent.

The Court of Appeals erred by reversing the convictions on the ground that there was "no evidence of malice." This was not the correct procedural mechanism to address the trial court’s ruling on the unconsciousness defense. The Court of Appeals should have evaluated the trial court’s evidentiary ruling as a preserved error or as plain error affecting the defendant’s substantial rights, rather than dismissing the State’s case as legally insufficient.

**III. PLAIN ERROR REVIEW**

The defense argues that the Court of Appeals properly identified "plain error" under State v. Odom, 307 N.C. 600, 299 S.E. 2d 867 (1983), because the absence of malice was a fundamental defect. Plain error review is appropriate only when the error is "fundamental" and "obvious." Here, the error identified by the Court of Appeals—the lack of evidence of malice—was not an error at all. Because the State presented sufficient evidence of malice, the Court of Appeals’ application of the plain error doctrine was misplaced.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The Court of Appeals erred in reversing the convictions based on a finding that there was no evidence of malice. The State’s evidence regarding the defendant’s intoxication and reckless driving was sufficient to support the jury’s finding of malice under the principles established in State v. Robbins, 309 N.C. 771, 309 S.E. 2d 188 (1983), and State v. Wilkerson, 295 N.C. 559, 247 S.E. 2d 905 (1978). The defendant’s arguments regarding the exclusion of evidence concerning unconsciousness present valid grounds for appellate review, but they do not support the conclusion that the State’s evidence was legally insufficient. We reverse the decision of the Court of Appeals and remand the case to that court for consideration of the defendant’s remaining assignments of error regarding the exclusion of the unconsciousness evidence.

**REVERSED AND REMANDED.**
