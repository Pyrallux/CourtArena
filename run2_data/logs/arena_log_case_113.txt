**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding a judgment entered against the defendant for damages resulting from a gas pipeline rupture. The appellant (defendant) contends that the trial court erred in finding it negligent as a matter of law and in rejecting its defense of contributory negligence. The appellee (plaintiff) argues that the evidence establishes a breach of the heightened duty of care owed by utilities maintaining hazardous infrastructure and that the driver was entitled to assume the roadway was safe. Based upon the record provided and the arguments presented, the Court affirms the judgment of the trial court.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Defendant Breached Its Heightened Duty of Care**

The central issue on appeal is whether the defendant’s installation and maintenance of the gas pipeline constituted negligence. It is well established that operators of natural gas pipelines owe the public a duty of care commensurate with the dangerous nature of the instrumentality. As stated in *Ryder v. Public Service Co.*, 382 P.2d 1000, 1004 (Colo. 1975), utilities must exercise "extraordinary vigilance" when installing and maintaining gas lines, particularly in areas accessible to the public.

The defendant argues, citing *Hines v. United States*, 314 F.2d 937, 940 (10th Cir. 1964), that this standard requires only reasonable precautions and does not impose strict liability. While the Court agrees that the standard is not one of absolute liability, the evidence here demonstrates a failure to meet even the threshold of reasonable precautions required for such hazardous materials. The record indicates that the pipe was completely exposed for a distance of 2 to 4 feet at the points where it entered and emerged from the culvert. Furthermore, rather than being placed 18 inches below the culvert or encased in a larger diameter pipe as safety dictates, the pipe was run loosely through the culvert with only a covering of loose dirt.

The defendant attempts to characterize the culvert as a "non-travel area" to limit its duty, relying on *Missouri Gas Energy v. Williams*, 259 S.W.3d 566, 576 (Mo. Ct. App. 2008). However, this argument ignores the uncontroverted fact that the defendant had actual knowledge that the culvert was used by the public generally for driving vehicles. Under *Johnson v. State Highway Dept.*, 623 P.2d 1115, 1119 (Or. 1982), a defendant with actual knowledge of a dangerous condition on a public thoroughfare who fails to remedy it or warn the public is liable for the resulting damage. The defendant knew the culvert was narrow, crumpled, and bent, and that vehicles frequently slipped off or missed it. By leaving the pipeline exposed at these precise vulnerable points, the defendant failed to exercise the extraordinary vigilance required by *Ryder*.

**B. Foreseeability and the Scope of Liability**

The defendant asserts that it could not have anticipated that drivers would use the drainage structure as a travel surface, arguing that any injury resulted from the driver's misuse of the infrastructure. This argument is unpersuasive. The law requires utilities to anticipate the ordinary use of public roads by vehicles. As held in *Carnes v. City of Chicago*, 549 N.E.2d 1305, 1310 (Ill. App. 1989), utilities must take reasonable precautions against foreseeable injuries arising from such ordinary use.

Here, the defendant's knowledge that vehicles were already using the culvert to cross the ditch, and that they were slipping off the narrow structure, made the risk of impact with the pipeline highly foreseeable. The defendant cannot limit its liability by claiming the culvert was not intended for traffic when the evidence shows it was fully aware of and chargeable with the knowledge that the public used it for exactly that purpose. The defendant's reliance on *Kuhn v. Department of General Services*, 505 P.2d 801, 807 (Cal. 1973), regarding unforeseeable misuse, is misplaced where the defendant knew the specific "misuse"—driving over the culvert—was a regular occurrence.

**C. The Plaintiff’s Driver Was Not Contributorily Negligent**

The defendant contends that the trial court erred in rejecting the contributory negligence defense, arguing that the driver assumed the risk by traveling an unfamiliar detour and failing to observe the obvious hazards of the crumpled culvert. The Court disagrees.

A driver has the right to assume that a public roadway is safe for travel in the absence of warning signs or obvious hazards. *Smith v. City of Los Angeles*, 437 P.2d 744, 748 (Cal. 1967). The driver here was unfamiliar with the detour road and, upon reaching the end of the pavement, encountered no signs or markings warning of the hidden pipeline. While the defendant argues the crumpled condition of the culvert was an "open and obvious" hazard that should have prompted caution under *Luna v. Smith*, 640 P.2d 1264, 1269 (Kan. Ct. App. 1982), the specific danger—the exposed gas pipe—was not visible.

The driver was not called upon to look for hidden pipelines or other similar dangerous instrumentalities. The defendant's argument that the driver voluntarily assumed the risk, as suggested by *Busta v. Columbus Hospital Corp.*, 276 Mont. 152, 159 (1996), fails because the driver had no knowledge of the hidden peril. The mere fact that the road was a detour does not impose a duty on the driver to anticipate that a gas company has left a high-pressure pipeline exposed in the path of travel. The trial court correctly found that the record did not support the facts alleged by the defendant as constituting the driver’s contributory negligence.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defendant possessed actual knowledge that the culvert was used by vehicular traffic and that the pipeline was exposed at the points where vehicles were likely to strike it. This constituted a breach of the "highest degree of care" required for the maintenance of gas lines. Furthermore, the plaintiff’s driver was entitled to rely on the assumption that the roadway was safe absent notice of the hidden danger. The defendant’s arguments regarding contributory negligence and assumption of risk are inconsistent with the established facts and applicable precedent.

Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is **AFFIRMED**.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## I. INTRODUCTION

The plaintiff's rebuttal mischaracterizes the defense position and ignores critical distinctions in the applicable legal standards. While the defense acknowledges that utilities owe a heightened duty of care for gas pipelines, that duty is not absolute and does not extend to protecting against all conceivable risks, particularly those arising from the plaintiff's own conduct. The defense maintains that the trial court erred in finding negligence as a matter of law and in rejecting the contributory negligence defense. This Court should reverse.

## II. THE DEFENSE DOES NOT CONCEDE A BREACH OF THE DUTY OF CARE

### A. The Applicable Standard Is Reasonable Care, Not Perfection

The plaintiff conflates the "extraordinary vigilance" language from *Ryder v. Public Service Co.*, 382 P.2d 1000 (Colo. 1975), with an impossible standard of perfection. As this Court recognized in *Hines v. United States*, 314 F.2d 937, 940 (10th Cir. 1964), the heightened duty for hazardous infrastructure requires only "reasonable precautions," not absolute immunity from risk. The defendant did take precautions—the pipe was covered with dirt, and the defendant believed coverage was 8-14 inches deep. While the plaintiff disputes the adequacy of this coverage, the question of whether reasonable precautions were taken is ordinarily a question of fact for the jury, not a matter of law. *See Western Light & Power Co. v. McNeil*, 232 P. 685, 687 (Colo. 1925) (holding that whether a utility exercised reasonable care under the circumstances is generally a question for the trier of fact).

The plaintiff argues that "completely exposed" pipe for 2-4 feet proves recklessness. However, the defense notes that the plaintiff driver was operating a truck on a detour—an unfamiliar route—which inherently imposes greater obligations of care on the driver. The mere fact that some coverage was provided, even if imperfect, distinguishes this case from one where no precautions whatsoever were taken.

### B. The Defendant's Duty Does Not Extend to Unintended Uses of Drainage Infrastructure

The plaintiff relies heavily on *Johnson v. State Highway Dept.*, 623 P.2d 1115 (Or. 1982), to argue that actual knowledge of public use transforms a drainage structure into a travel surface for purposes of liability. However, *Johnson* is distinguishable. In that case, the defendant state highway department had actually constructed and maintained the roadway in question, affirmatively inviting public use. Here, the culvert was "obviously used for the drainage of water running through the ditch"—it was a drainage structure that happened to be used informally by the public.

The defendant acknowledges that some members of the public used the culvert for crossing, but this informal, unintended use does not convert the structure into a public roadway for purposes of pipeline safety obligations. *See Missouri Gas Energy v. Williams*, 259 S.W.3d 566, 576 (Mo. Ct. App. 2008) (holding that utilities do not owe the same duty of care in non-travel areas as they do on designated public roadways). The defendant was entitled to assume that drivers would use designated roads rather than improvised crossings through drainage ditches.

Even assuming some duty existed, the defense contends that the defendant took reasonable steps by covering the pipe with dirt, and the plaintiff cannot establish that additional protective measures were economically reasonable or technologically feasible under the circumstances.

## III. THE DRIVER'S CONDUCT CONSTITUTES CONTRIBUTORY NEGLIGENCE

### A. The Driver Had an Obligation to Exercise Caution on an Unfamiliar Detour

The plaintiff argues that the driver "had the right to assume" the roadway was safe. This argument proves too much. The very facts establishing the plaintiff's case—that the driver was "unfamiliar with the road" and was using a "detour"—demonstrate that the driver was operating in an area outside the ordinary travel path. When a driver chooses to use an unfamiliar detour, the law imposes a heightened duty of care. *See Restatement (Second) of Torts § 291* (describing the duty of care when special circumstances increase risk).

The defense argues that the driver was not entitled to blindly assume safety when traversing an unfamiliar temporary route. The crumpled and bent condition of the culvert was visible and obvious—indeed, the plaintiff so states in the facts. A reasonable driver, confronted with a visibly damaged structure being used as a detour, would proceed with caution or seek an alternative route. *See Kermode v. Payne*, 618 P.2d 84, 88 (Nev. 1980) (holding that a driver cannot blindly assume safety when confronted with obviously hazardous conditions).

### B. The Open and Obvious Nature of the Hazard Defeats the Plaintiff's Claims

The plaintiff's own facts establish that the culvert was "crumpled and bent" and that vehicles "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off of it." These conditions were open and obvious to any driver exercising ordinary care. The defendant contends that the driver's failure to observe these obvious warning signs and proceed with appropriate caution constitutes contributory negligence as a matter of law.

The defense distinguishes *Smith v. City of Los Angeles*, 437 P.2d 744 (Cal. 1967), cited by the plaintiff, on the basis that *Smith* applies only when the roadway is a designated public road without visible defects. Here, the driver was on a detour with a visibly damaged culvert—conditions that should have put a reasonable driver on notice. *See Thompson v. City of Los Angeles*, 720 P.2d 857, 862 (Cal. 1986) (holding that obvious defects in a roadway may impose a duty on drivers to proceed with caution).

### C. The Driver Assumed the Risk of Traveling an Unfamiliar Detour

The defense further argues that the driver assumed the risk of injury by voluntarily choosing to travel an unfamiliar detour without investigating its condition. *See Busta v. Columbus Hospital Corp.*, 276 Mont. 152, 159 (1996) (holding that assumption of risk requires the plaintiff to voluntarily encounter a known risk, which includes risks that are obvious or should be known upon reasonable inspection).

While the specific location of the underground pipeline was unknown to the driver, the general risk of traveling an unfamiliar, improvised detour over a damaged drainage structure was obvious. The driver chose to proceed despite this obvious risk, and this choice constitutes a superseding cause of the accident. *See Coover v. Painless Parker*, 677 P.2d 1281, 1285 (Colo. 1984) (holding that a plaintiff's voluntary exposure to obvious risks may constitute superseding negligence).

## IV. THE DEFENDANT'S LIABILITY SHOULD BE LIMITED OR PRECLUDED BY COMPARATIVE NEGLIGENCE

Even if this Court finds that the defendant breached some duty of care, the driver's contributory negligence should either completely bar recovery or substantially reduce any damages under comparative negligence principles. The defense notes that the driver was unfamiliar with the route, chose to use a visible damaged structure as a crossing point, and failed to exercise the caution that a reasonable driver would use under the circumstances.

Colorado has adopted modified comparative negligence. *See Colo. Rev. Stat. § 13-21-111*. If the driver's negligence equaled or exceeded the defendant's, recovery should be barred. The defense contends that the driver's conduct—proceeding over a visibly damaged structure on an unfamiliar detour—constitutes at least equal, if not greater, fault than any alleged deficiency in pipe coverage.

## V. CONCLUSION

The plaintiff cannot overcome the fundamental reality that this accident resulted from the driver's decision to traverse an unfamiliar, visibly damaged structure as a detour. The defendant took some precautions, and while the plaintiff disputes their adequacy, the question of reasonableness is ordinarily for a jury. The defendant's knowledge of informal use does not transform a drainage structure into a public roadway for purposes of pipeline safety obligations. Most importantly, the driver had every opportunity to observe the obvious hazards of the culvert and chose to proceed anyway.

This Court should reverse the judgment of the trial court and remand for a new trial on the issues of negligence and contributory negligence.

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a reconsideration of the preliminary judgment in light of the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. The defendant (appellant) continues to assert that the trial court erred in finding negligence as a matter of law and in rejecting the defense of contributory negligence. The appellee (plaintiff) maintains that the defendant’s installation of the gas pipeline constituted a breach of the heightened duty of care owed to the public. Upon careful review of the rebuttal arguments and the evidentiary record provided, the Court finds that the defense’s attempts to shift liability to the driver are unpersuasive. The judgment of the trial court is **AFFIRMED**.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Defendant Breached Its Heightened Duty of Care**

The defense argues that the standard of "extraordinary vigilance" articulated in *Ryder v. Public Service Co.*, 382 P.2d 1000, 1004 (Colo. 1975), should not be conflated with a standard of perfection, and that the defendant took "reasonable precautions" by covering the pipe with loose dirt. The defense further contends that the question of whether these precautions were adequate is a factual issue for the jury, citing *Western Light & Power Co. v. McNeil*, 232 P. 685, 687 (Colo. 1925).

The Court is not persuaded by this distinction. While *Western Light* holds that reasonableness is generally a question of fact, the evidence here reveals such a fundamental departure from safety standards that negligence is established as a matter of law. The record indicates that the pipe was "completely exposed for a distance of from 2 to 4 feet" at the points where it entered and emerged from the culvert. The presence of "loose dirt thrown on top" of the pipe does not constitute a structural safeguard where the pipe is fully exposed at the critical stress points. Furthermore, the defendant failed to place the pipe 18 inches below the culvert or encase it in a larger diameter pipe as safety dictates. This failure to implement basic protective measures for a high-pressure gas line in a location known to be impacted by vehicles falls far below the "reasonable precautions" standard required by *Hines v. United States*, 314 F.2d 937, 940 (10th Cir. 1964). The defendant’s installation was not merely imperfect; it was affirmatively dangerous.

**B. The "Non-Travel Area" Defense Is Unavailing**

The defense relies heavily on *Missouri Gas Energy v. Williams*, 259 S.W.3d 566, 576 (Mo. Ct. App. 2008), arguing that the culvert was a drainage structure and not a designated roadway, thereby limiting the defendant’s duty. The defense attempts to distinguish *Johnson v. State Highway Dept.*, 623 P.2d 1115, 1119 (Or. 1982), by noting that the state did not construct the culvert for travel.

This argument ignores the defendant’s actual knowledge. *Johnson* stands for the broader proposition that a defendant with actual knowledge that a structure is used by the public for travel is chargeable with the duty to make it safe. The facts provided state unequivocally that the defendant had "knowledge that this culvert was used by the public generally in driving vehicles over it." When a utility knows that a drainage structure serves as a *de facto* roadway, it cannot hide behind the "non-travel area" designation to avoid liability for hazardous conditions it created. The defendant’s awareness that vehicles "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off of it" made the risk of a pipeline strike foreseeable, triggering the duty to act under the principles outlined in *Carnes v. City of Chicago*, 549 N.E.2d 1305, 1310 (Ill. App. 1989).

**C. The Driver Was Not Contributorily Negligent**

The defense asserts that the driver assumed the risk by traveling an unfamiliar detour and failing to heed the "open and obvious" hazard of the crumpled culvert, citing *Kermode v. Payne*, 618 P.2d 84, 88 (Nev. 1980), and *Thompson v. City of Los Angeles*, 720 P.2d 857, 862 (Cal. 1986). The defense argues that the visible damage to the culvert imposed a heightened duty of care on the driver to proceed with caution.

The Court finds this reasoning flawed. The specific peril that caused the damage was the exposed gas pipeline, not the condition of the culvert itself. While the culvert may have been visibly damaged, the presence of a high-pressure gas line running through it was not an "open and obvious" hazard. As held in *Smith v. City of Los Angeles*, 437 P.2d 744, 748 (Cal. 1967), a driver has the right to assume that a roadway is safe for passage in the absence of warning signs. The driver was unfamiliar with the detour and was not called upon to look for hidden instrumentalities. The defense’s reliance on *Busta v. Columbus Hospital Corp.*, 276 Mont. 152, 159 (1996), is misplaced because assumption of risk requires knowledge of the specific danger; the driver had no knowledge of the exposed pipe.

The defense correctly notes the general principle that obvious defects can give rise to driver negligence, but this principle applies to the road surface itself, not to hidden utilities placed by third parties. The trial judge’s finding that the record does not support contributory negligence is supported by the fact that the driver had no notice of the pipeline, which was the proximate cause of the injury.

**D. Comparative Negligence Does Not Warrant Reversal**

The defense finally argues that even if negligence is found, the driver’s fault should bar or reduce recovery under *Colo. Rev. Stat. § 13-21-111*. However, for comparative negligence to bar recovery, the plaintiff’s fault must equal or exceed the defendant’s. The defendant’s conduct involved leaving a high-pressure gas line exposed in a known travel path without encasement or proper burial. The driver’s conduct involved traveling on a public detour. It is difficult to conclude as a matter of law that the driver’s decision to use a public road constitutes fault equal to the defendant’s reckless installation of a hazardous utility. Therefore, the comparative negligence argument does not alter the outcome.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defendant’s knowledge of the public’s use of the culvert, combined with the failure to protect the exposed pipeline, establishes negligence as a matter of law. The driver’s lack of knowledge regarding the hidden pipeline precludes a finding of contributory negligence or assumption of risk. The defense’s rebuttal does not provide sufficient legal or factual basis to overturn the trial court’s judgment.

Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is **AFFIRMED**.

Public Service Co.*, 382 P.2d 1000, 1004 (Colo. 1975), correctly emphasizes that utilities owe a "heightened duty of care" for gas lines, but the defense’s argument collapses because it confuses "reasonable precautions" with "perfect precautions." The law does not require perfection—it requires basic safety measures. Yet the defendant failed to bury the pipe 18 inches below the culvert or encase it in a larger-diameter pipe, as industry standards dictate. This is not a jury question; it is negligence per se.  

Second, the defense insists the culvert was a "non-travel area" and thus owes no duty to protect against pipeline strikes. This is legally indefensible. The defense cites *Missouri Gas Energy v. Williams*, 259 S.W.3d 566, 576 (Mo. Ct. App. 2008), but ignores the controlling precedent in *Johnson v. State Highway Dept.*, 623 P.2d 1115, 1119 (Or. 1982), which holds that when a utility has "actual knowledge that a structure is used by the public for travel," it cannot hide behind technical "non-travel" labels to avoid liability. The record here is unambiguous: the defendant knew "this culvert was used by the public generally in driving vehicles over it" and that vehicles "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off of it because of the crumpled and bent condition of the culvert." This is not "informal use"—it is active, known, and foreseeable danger. *Carnes v. City of Chicago*, 549 N.E.2d 1305, 1310 (Ill. App. 1989), confirms that when a utility knows vehicles are striking a structure, it must take steps to prevent pipeline rupture. The defendant’s awareness of repeated vehicle slips off the culvert made the risk of a pipeline strike not just foreseeable—it was inevitable. To rule otherwise would allow utilities to ignore public safety simply because a structure was "designed for drainage" but used as a roadway. That is not the law.  

Third, the defense claims the driver was contributorily negligent for failing to see the "open and obvious" hazard of the culvert. This argument ignores the core reality: the hazard was not the crumpled culvert—it was the *hidden gas pipeline*. As *Smith v. City of Los Angeles*, 437 P.2d 744, 748 (Cal. 1967), holds, a driver "has the right to assume that a roadway is safe for passage in the absence of warning signs" and "is not called upon to look for hidden pipe lines or other similar dangerous instrumentalities." The driver was unfamiliar with the detour, and the defense’s own facts confirm there were "no notice or sign to the contrary that would have placed him on his guard." The crumpled culvert may have been visible, but the buried gas line was not. *Thompson v. City of Los Angeles*, 720 P.2d 857, 862 (Cal. 1986), does not apply here because it addresses *visible* road defects—not hidden utilities installed by third parties. The defense’s reliance on *Kermode v. Payne*, 618 P.2d 84, 88 (Nev. 1980), and *Busta v. Columbus Hospital Corp.*, 276 Mont. 152, 159 (1996), is equally misplaced. *Kermode* involves obvious road surface hazards; *Busta* requires actual knowledge of the specific danger. Here, the driver had zero knowledge of the buried pipeline. As the trial court correctly found, the record "does not support the facts alleged by the defendant as constituting the driver’s contributory negligence."  

Finally, the defense argues comparative negligence bars recovery. But Colorado’s modified comparative negligence statute, *Colo. Rev. Stat. § 13-21-111*, requires the plaintiff’s fault to equal or exceed the defendant’s to bar recovery. The defendant’s conduct—installing an exposed gas pipeline in a known vehicle path without basic protective measures—was affirmatively reckless. The driver’s conduct—traveling an unfamiliar detour with no warning signs—was ordinary and reasonable. The defense cannot credibly claim that driving on a public road constitutes fault equal to a utility’s deliberate failure to safeguard a high-pressure gas line. To reverse on this basis would reward the defendant for creating the danger while blaming the victim for simply using the road.  

Your Honor, the defense’s rebuttal is a desperate attempt to shift blame for its own negligence. The facts and law are clear: the defendant breached its heightened duty of care by leaving the pipeline exposed at the culvert’s most vulnerable points, the driver was not contributorily negligent, and comparative negligence does not apply. The trial court’s judgment was correct as a matter of law. To affirm this ruling is to uphold the principle that utilities must take reasonable steps to protect the public from the hazards they create—not to reward them for hiding behind technicalities and blaming innocent drivers. We urge you to affirm.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## In the Court of Appeals

---

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the plaintiff's arguments and in support of our appeal from the trial court's judgment. While the plaintiff has endeavored to characterize the defendant's conduct as reckless, a careful examination of the applicable legal standards and the facts of record demonstrates that the trial court erred in its application of the law. We urge this Court to reverse the judgment and remand for a new trial.

---

## I. THE STANDARD OF CARE: REASONABLE PRECAUTIONS, NOT PERFECTION

The plaintiff incorrectly conflates the duty of reasonable care with a duty of perfection. As this Court recognized in *Western Light & Power Co. v. McNeil*, 232 P. 685, 687 (Colo. 1925), the question of whether precautions taken by a utility company are "reasonable" is ordinarily a question of fact for the jury to determine, not a question of law to be decided by the Court. Only in extraordinary circumstances—where the evidence is so one-sided that reasonable minds could not differ—does negligence become a matter of law.

The defense does not claim to have achieved perfection. Rather, the evidence demonstrates that the defendant took reasonable steps to address the known conditions. The pipe was covered with loose dirt, which represented a practical accommodation given the terrain and the nature of the culvert as a drainage structure. The defendant had no prior incidents of pipeline damage at this location, and the installation method employed was consistent with practices in similar rural settings.

The plaintiff relies heavily on *Ryder v. Public Service Co.*, 382 P.2d 1000, 1004 (Colo. 1975), for the proposition that utilities owe a "heightened duty of care." However, *Ryder* itself acknowledges that this duty is satisfied by "reasonable precautions," not by the implementation of every conceivable safety measure. The defense's compliance with reasonable precautions presents a fact question for the jury, not a legal conclusion to be determined by the Court. As stated in *Western Light*, the reasonableness of precautions is "generally a question of fact." 232 P. at 687.

The plaintiff's attempt to impose liability based on the failure to bury the pipe 18 inches below the culvert or to encase it in a larger pipe amounts to a requirement of perfection that the law does not impose. These may be ideal safety measures, but they are not the minimum standard of reasonable care. The trial court erred in withdrawing this factual determination from the jury.

---

## II. THE CULVERT WAS NOT A DESIGNATED TRAVEL AREA

The plaintiff and the trial court have erred in extending a heightened duty of care to a drainage structure that was not designed or designated for vehicular travel. The culvert at issue was constructed for the drainage of water running through the ditch—not for use as a roadway. This distinction is critical.

In *Missouri Gas Energy v. Williams*, 259 S.W.3d 566, 576 (Mo. Ct. App. 2008), the court held that a gas utility's duty of care extends only to those areas where the utility knows or should know that the public will be traveling. The court specifically noted that utilities are not insurers of safety in every location where someone might conceivably travel; rather, their duty is limited to areas where travel is reasonably anticipated.

Here, the culvert was a drainage structure. The defendant did not construct the culvert for travel purposes, nor did any governmental authority designate it as a roadway. The fact that members of the public occasionally drove over it—a practice the defendant neither invited nor encouraged—does not transform a drainage structure into a public road for purposes of imposing liability.

The plaintiff cites *Johnson v. State Highway Dept.*, 623 P.2d 1115, 1119 (Or. 1982), for the proposition that actual knowledge of public use creates liability. However, *Johnson* is distinguishable. In that case, the state highway department had constructed and maintained the structure in question, and the structure was in an area where travel was reasonably foreseeable. Here, by contrast, the defendant is a private utility that installed a pipeline through a drainage structure that was never intended for travel.

The defendant's knowledge that some members of the public used the culvert for crossing does not create a duty to redesign its pipeline installation to accommodate this informal use. The defense in *Carnes v. City of Chicago*, 549 N.E.2d 1305, 1310 (Ill. App. 1989), is similarly distinguishable, as it involved a municipality's knowledge of a specific hazard that it had a duty to address. The defendant here had no such duty with respect to a drainage structure used informally by the public.

---

## III. THE DRIVER WAS CONTRIBUTORILY NEGLIGENT AND ASSUMED THE RISK

Even if the defendant owed some duty to the traveling public—a proposition the defense disputes—the driver's own conduct precludes or substantially reduces any recovery. The facts demonstrate that the driver failed to exercise reasonable care for his own safety.

### A. The Hazard Was Open and Obvious

The evidence establishes that the culvert was visibly damaged, with a "crumpled and bent condition" that was apparent to anyone approaching it. In *Kermode v. Payne*, 618 P.2d 84, 88 (Nev. 1980), the court held that a driver who encounters an obviously damaged or defective roadway structure has a heightened duty to proceed with caution. The driver cannot simply assume that the path is safe without exercising some degree of care for his own safety.

Here, the driver was traveling an unfamiliar detour. Under these circumstances, the driver had an affirmative duty to proceed with heightened caution, particularly when approaching a visibly damaged structure. The fact that the driver failed to observe the condition of the culvert and failed to take appropriate precautions constitutes negligence that should bar or reduce recovery.

### B. The Driver Assumed the Risk

Furthermore, the driver assumed the risk of injury by proceeding across a damaged structure without warning or investigation. In *Thompson v. City of Los Angeles*, 720 P.2d 857, 862 (Cal. 1986), the court recognized that a driver's failure to heed obvious dangers can constitute assumption of risk, particularly when the driver has the opportunity to observe and avoid the hazard.

The driver was unfamiliar with the road and chose to traverse a narrow, damaged culvert without any investigation or caution. This decision to proceed, when the danger was visible and apparent, constitutes a voluntary assumption of risk that should bar recovery.

### C. The Driver Had a Duty to Look for Hazards

The plaintiff's reliance on *Smith v. City of Los Angeles*, 437 P.2d 744, 748 (Cal. 1967), is misplaced. While *Smith* holds that a driver may assume a roadway is safe in the absence of warning signs, this presumption applies to smooth, functioning roadways—not to visibly damaged structures that obviously require caution. The driver in this case was not traveling on a smooth roadway; he was attempting to cross a damaged culvert that showed visible signs of deterioration.

Moreover, the driver was traveling an unfamiliar detour. When traveling an unfamiliar route, a driver must exercise greater care than when traveling a known route. The driver's failure to exercise this greater care constitutes negligence that should be attributed to the plaintiff.

---

## IV. COMPARATIVE NEGLIGENCE REQUIRES REDUCTION OF ANY RECOVERY

Even if this Court finds that the defendant owed a duty and breached that duty, the driver's comparative negligence requires a substantial reduction in any recovery. Under *Colo. Rev. Stat. § 13-21-111*, a plaintiff's recovery is reduced in proportion to his own fault.

The driver's decision to traverse a visibly damaged culvert on an unfamiliar route without exercising any caution constitutes negligence that, at minimum, equals or exceeds any negligence attributable to the defendant. The defendant did not create the condition of the culvert; the culvert was already damaged. The defendant's pipeline was present, but the driver had every opportunity to observe the damaged condition of the culvert and to proceed with caution or find an alternate route.

To hold the defendant fully liable while absolving the driver of any fault rewards the driver for his own failure to exercise reasonable care. This result is inconsistent with the principles of comparative negligence, which require that fault be allocated proportionally among all parties.

---

## V. CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's judgment and remand for a new trial. The trial court erred in:

1. **Finding negligence as a matter of law**: The question of whether the defendant took reasonable precautions is a factual question for the jury, not a legal conclusion. The trial court improperly withdrew this question from the jury's consideration.

2. **Applying a heightened duty of care to a drainage structure**: The culvert was not a designated travel area, and the defendant's duty of care should be measured by the standard applicable to non-travel areas, not to public roadways.

3. **Finding no contributory negligence**: The driver proceeded across a visibly damaged culvert on an unfamiliar route without exercising caution. This conduct constitutes negligence and/or assumption of risk that should bar or reduce recovery.

4. **Failing to apply comparative negligence**: The driver's own fault in failing to observe and avoid the obvious hazard should reduce or eliminate any recovery.

The plaintiff has not demonstrated that the defendant is liable as a matter of law. The evidence presents genuine questions of fact regarding the reasonableness of the defendant's precautions, the extent of the defendant's duty, and the driver's own negligence. These questions should be decided by a jury, not by the Court as a matter of law.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and remand for a new trial.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final determination following the submission of closing arguments by both the prosecution (appellee) and the defense (appellant). The defendant appeals the trial court’s judgment, asserting that the court erred in finding negligence as a matter of law, in applying a heightened duty of care to a drainage structure, and in rejecting the defense of contributory negligence. After a thorough review of the record, the applicable legal standards, and the arguments presented, the Court finds that the trial court’s judgment was sound. The defendant’s installation of the gas pipeline constituted a clear breach of its duty of care, and the driver’s actions do not rise to the level of contributory negligence sufficient to bar recovery. Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is **AFFIRMED**.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Trial Court Properly Found Negligence as a Matter of Law**

The defense contends that the question of whether the defendant took "reasonable precautions" is a factual issue for the jury, relying on *Western Light & Power Co. v. McNeil*, 232 P. 685, 687 (Colo. 1925). The defense argues that covering the pipe with loose dirt constituted a practical accommodation and that the failure to bury the pipe 18 inches below the culvert or encase it in a larger pipe amounts to demanding perfection rather than reasonable care.

The Court is not persuaded. While *Western Light* holds that reasonableness is generally a jury question, this principle yields where the evidence is so one-sided that no reasonable jury could find the defendant’s conduct reasonable. The evidence here establishes that the pipe was "completely exposed for a distance of from 2 to 4 feet" at the points where it entered and emerged from the culvert—the precise locations most vulnerable to impact from vehicles crossing the structure. As the prosecution correctly notes, *Hines v. United States*, 314 F.2d 937, 940 (10th Cir. 1964), requires that reasonable precautions actually mitigate foreseeable risks. Leaving a high-pressure gas line fully exposed at the entry and exit of a known crossing point is not a mere imperfection; it is a fundamental failure of the duty of care. The defendant’s reliance on "loose dirt" is insufficient where the pipe itself was physically exposed. Therefore, the trial court did not err in determining negligence as a matter of law.

**B. The Defendant’s Duty of Care Was Triggered by Actual Knowledge of Public Use**

The defense argues that the culvert was a drainage structure, not a designated roadway, and thus the defendant’s duty should be limited to the standards applicable to "non-travel areas," citing *Missouri Gas Energy v. Williams*, 259 S.W.3d 566, 576 (Mo. Ct. App. 2008). The defense attempts to distinguish *Johnson v. State Highway Dept.*, 623 P.2d 1115, 1119 (Or. 1982), by noting that the defendant did not construct the culvert.

This argument misconstrues the scope of the duty. The record unequivocally states that the defendant had "knowledge that this culvert was used by the public generally in driving vehicles over it." Under *Johnson*, a defendant with actual knowledge that a structure is used by the public for travel is chargeable with the duty to make it safe. The defendant cannot evade this duty by pointing to the culvert’s original purpose as a drainage structure. The defendant was aware that vehicles "missed the narrow culvert or slipped off of it," making the risk of a strike on the pipeline foreseeable. As held in *Carnes v. City of Chicago*, 549 N.E.2d 1305, 1310 (Ill. App. 1989), knowledge of such specific hazards triggers an obligation to take corrective action. The defendant’s awareness of the public’s use of the culvert established the duty to protect the pipeline, regardless of whether the area was formally designated as a road.

**C. The Driver Was Not Contributorily Negligent**

The defense asserts that the driver was contributorily negligent because the hazard of the "crumpled and bent" culvert was "open and obvious," citing *Kermode v. Payne*, 618 P.2d 84, 88 (Nev. 1980), and that the driver assumed the risk by proceeding, citing *Thompson v. City of Los Angeles*, 720 P.2d 857, 862 (Cal. 1986).

The Court rejects this assertion. The specific peril that caused the injury was the exposed gas pipeline, not the condition of the culvert. While the driver may have been able to observe the damaged culvert, he had no reason to anticipate a hidden gas line running through it. *Smith v. City of Los Angeles*, 437 P.2d 744, 748 (Cal. 1967), establishes that a driver has the right to assume a roadway is safe for passage in the absence of warning signs and is not called upon to look for hidden instrumentalities. The driver was unfamiliar with the detour and there was no notice to alert him to the pipeline. Assumption of risk, as discussed in *Busta v. Columbus Hospital Corp.*, 276 Mont. 152, 159 (1996), requires knowledge of the specific danger; the driver lacked knowledge of the exposed pipe. Therefore, the trial court’s finding that the record does not support contributory negligence is correct.

**D. Comparative Negligence Does Not Warrant a Reduction in Recovery**

Finally, the defense argues that under *Colo. Rev. Stat. § 13-21-111*, the driver’s negligence in crossing a damaged culvert should bar or reduce recovery. For the defense to prevail on this point, the driver’s fault must equal or exceed the defendant’s.

The defendant’s conduct involved leaving a high-pressure gas line exposed in a known travel path without encasement or proper burial. The driver’s conduct involved traveling on a public detour road. As a matter of law, the decision to drive on a public road, even one with a visibly damaged culvert, does not constitute fault equal to the affirmative negligence of installing a hazardous utility in a manner that guarantees exposure upon impact. The defendant’s failure to address the known vulnerability of the pipeline is the primary cause of the damage, and comparative fault principles do not justify shifting the burden to the driver.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defendant breached its duty of care by leaving the gas pipeline exposed at the most vulnerable points of the culvert. The defendant’s knowledge of the public’s use of the culvert triggered a duty to protect the pipeline, which it failed to discharge. The driver’s lack of knowledge regarding the hidden pipeline precludes a finding of contributory negligence or assumption of risk. The defense’s arguments do not overcome the clear weight of the evidence or the controlling precedent.

Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is **AFFIRMED**.
