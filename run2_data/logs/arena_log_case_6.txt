COURT ARENA LOG - Case 6
FACTS:
Preston Galbraith stands convicted of aggravated assault for striking and seriously injuring his infant daughter. Judgment of conviction was entered upon his plea of guilty after the charge against him was reduced from aggravated battery, as origi*380nally alleged. He later filed a motion under I.C.R. 35, seeking reduction of the sentence so he could be released for psychotherapy that he claimed was unavailable in confinement. The district court denied the motion. On appeal Galbraith argues that the court abused its discretion in so ruling, and that the sentence was cruel and unusual within the proscriptions of the state and federal constitutions. For reasons explained below, we affirm the district court’s order denying relief.

Galbraith’s constitutional attack, insofar as it relates to original pronouncement of the sentence, is untimely. He did not appeal the judgment containing the sentence. He appealed only the subsequent order denying his Rule 35 motion. We will not now review the sentence in light of the record existing when the sentence was imposed. See, e.g., State v. Torres, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); State v. Hirshbrunner, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983). Accordingly, our focus is upon the Rule 35 motion and upon the expanded record available when that motion was entertained. Because we conclude that the judge did not abuse his discretion in denying the motion, we also implicitly conclude that Galbraith failed in the Rule 35 proceeding to show how relief was necessary to obviate any cruel or unusual punishment.

A motion to reduce a sentence, upon the ground that it is unduly harsh, is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. State v. Arambula, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); State v. Russell, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985); State v. Araiza, 109 Idaho 188, 706 P.2d 77 (Ct.App. 1985). The motion essentially is a plea for leniency. State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). In this case, Galbraith could have received a fixed term, without parole, of five years for the aggravated assault. I.C. §§ 18-905, -906. He actually received an indeterminate sentence, creating an opportunity to earn parole.

When an indeterminate sentence is imposed, the duration of confinement generally is treated, for the purpose of appellate review, as one-third of the facial length of the sentence. State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982). In determining whether such duration is excessive, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation and retribution. Id. These criteria apply not only to review of a sentence on direct appeal but also to review of a judge’s refusal to reduce the sentence under Rule 35. State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

Here, the victim of the crime was Galbraith’s seven-month-old daughter. Two incidents of abuse occurred. In the first, Galbraith kicked the child off a bed, cracking her ribs. In proceedings under the Child Protective Act, the girl was placed with her maternal grandparents and Galbraith was enrolled in a counseling program. However, while Galbraith was baby-sitting the child at the grandparents’ home, the second incident — giving rise to this criminal case — arose. The girl awoke from a nap and cried. Galbraith struck her in the head with his fist. Sometime later the girl began to experience difficulty breathing and was taken to a hospital. There she was found to have a complex skull fracture and a secondary subdural hematoma which caused bleeding into her left eye, possibly resulting in permanent vision impairment.

When this second incident occurred, Galbraith was eighteen years old. He had graduated from high school. He and his wife married while still in high school because she was pregnant. Galbraith was unemployed at the time of this offense, but was an enlisted member of the Marine Corps Reserve. He had no prior criminal record of consequence.

After his arrest and conviction, Galbraith underwent a psychological evaluation. A presentence report was prepared. Galbraith was described as generally intelligent. He told his evaluators that he had *381been abused as a child. The psychological evaluation concluded that Galbraith had a “poor social conscience, communication problems and a pronounced lack of ability to empathize____” The psychologist thought the patient was a “typically poor” candidate for therapy because he would have “difficulty owning responsibility for [his] anger and hostility, and [would be] more comfortable in projecting that to others. [He] can give good lip service to therapeutic change, but the prognostic picture is typically poor.” In the psychologist’s opinion, Galbraith represented a “very significant risk of danger if being left at large means having access to his child.” However, Galbraith was not considered a danger to the general public.

The presentence investigator noted the severe impact of the assault on the victim and suggested that incarceration was a “viable alternative for the court to seriously consider.” Galbraith asked for a sentence of probation. The prosecutor recommended an indeterminate sentence of five years, with jurisdiction retained for 120 days. The court decided against probation and against retained jurisdiction. He noted the seriousness of the offense and the fact that it had been committed while Galbraith was in counseling for the prior incident. The judge found that Galbraith presented a continuing risk to the victim and to other defenseless persons at whom he might become angry. The judge elected to impose a five-year sentence, making it indeterminate.

In support of his Rule 35 motion, Galbraith introduced testimony by a licensed clinical psychologist who specialized in child and adolescent behavioral problems. This psychologist was more optimistic than the prior psychologist about Galbraith’s therapeutic prospects. He recommended that Galbraith be placed in psychotherapy as soon as possible. He testified that age and the passage of time since the abusive incidents were factors bearing on the likelihood of a successful outcome. He opined that the therapy Galbraith needed was unavailable at the Idaho State Correctional Institution. However, he admitted that if treatment were delayed until Galbraith earned parole, which might occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing, there would not be a substantial diminution in the probability of success.

The judge observed that he was being asked to weigh the risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early upon reduction of his sentence. The judge found the latter risk to be greater. He reiterated that the sentence, requiring about twenty months’ total incarceration before parole eligibility, was appropriate to the severity of the crime.

Based upon the foregoing summary, we believe the judge gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised. The sentence is consistent with the objective of protecting society. It offers Galbraith the possibilities of parole and therapy for his eventual rehabilitation. We conclude that the district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to reduce the sentence. Accordingly, the order denying relief on Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed.

WALTERS, C.J., and SWANSTROM, J., concur.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF IDAHO, Plaintiff-Respondent,**
*v.*
**PRESTON GALBRAITH, Defendant-Appellant.**

**Case No.** CR-2014-000123

**Pursuant to the review of the parties' opening arguments and the provided case background, the Court renders the following preliminary ruling:**

The district court’s denial of Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed. The defense argues that the court abused its discretion by failing to give adequate weight to rehabilitation, by imposing a disproportionate sentence, and by applying an erroneous legal standard. However, based on the record provided, the district court’s decision was an informed exercise of judicial discretion consistent with the statutory goals of sentencing.

**1. Standard of Review**

A motion to reduce a sentence on the ground that it is unduly harsh is addressed to the sound discretion of the district court. *State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); *State v. Sutton*, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). Such a motion is essentially a plea for leniency, and on appeal, we must determine whether the court abused its discretion in denying that plea. *State v. Sutton*, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). When reviewing a sentence, including a refusal to reduce it under Rule 35, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, together with the related goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982); *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

**2. Consideration of Rehabilitation and Protection of Society**

The defense contends that the district court failed to adequately consider the rehabilitation factor, focusing exclusively on the protection of society. We disagree. The record demonstrates that the district court explicitly weighed the "risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early." This balancing act is precisely what the law requires. *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

While the defense presented testimony from a clinical psychologist recommending immediate psychotherapy, that expert admitted that delaying treatment until parole eligibility—which would occur approximately one year after the Rule 35 hearing—would not "substantially diminish" the probability of success. Given this admission, the district court did not abuse its discretion in determining that the risk to society, particularly the risk to the defendant’s infant daughter and other defenseless persons, outweighed the marginal benefit of immediate release for therapy. The original psychological evaluation, which described Galbraith as having a "poor social conscience" and being a "poor candidate" for therapy, further supports the court’s assessment of the risk. The court’s decision to prioritize public safety over the defendant's preferred rehabilitative timeline was a permissible exercise of discretion.

**3. Proportionality of the Sentence**

The defense argues that the five-year indeterminate sentence is disproportionate to the offense and the offender’s personal circumstances, citing Galbraith’s young age, lack of prior criminal record, and status as a Marine Corps Reserve member. However, the severity of the offense cannot be overlooked. Galbraith committed a brutal assault on a seven-month-old infant, causing a complex skull fracture and permanent risk of vision impairment. This was the second incident of abuse, occurring while the defendant was already enrolled in a counseling program.

Under *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982), the court must consider the nature of the offense. The sentence imposed—an indeterminate five-year term with parole eligibility after approximately twenty months—is within the statutory framework and reflects the seriousness of the crime. The district court’s finding that the sentence was appropriate to the severity of the crime is supported by the record and is not an abuse of discretion.

**4. Legal Standard and Burden of Proof**

The defense asserts that the district court applied an erroneous standard by requiring Galbraith to demonstrate that early release posed no risk whatsoever. The record does not support this characterization. The court’s finding that the "latter risk [to society] was greater" was a comparative assessment, not a demand for absolute proof of safety. The court was not required to accept the defense’s proposed solution of immediate release; it was sufficient that the court found the current sentence, which allows for parole and future therapy, better served the interests of justice and public safety. *State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976).

**5. Cruel and Unusual Punishment**

Finally, the defense raises a constitutional argument regarding cruel and unusual punishment based on the unavailability of specific therapy in confinement. Because the expert testified that delaying treatment would not substantially diminish the success of rehabilitation, and because the sentence allows for therapy upon parole, the continued incarceration does not constitute cruel and unusual punishment. The district court’s denial of the motion did not violate the state or federal constitutions.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised in the Rule 35 motion. It properly balanced the goals of protecting society and rehabilitating the defendant. The court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion for sentence reduction.

**ORDER**

The district court’s order denying relief on Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed.

The defense misapplies *State v. Here, Galbraith assaulted a seven-month-old infant twice—first kicking her off a bed, causing rib fractures, and later striking her head with a fist, resulting in a complex skull fracture, subdural hematoma, and permanent vision impairment risk. The second assault occurred while he was under court-ordered counseling for the first incident. This is not a case where mitigation outweighs aggravation; it is a brutal, repeated attack on a defenseless child by a caregiver. The sentence—structured to allow parole eligibility after approximately twenty months—reflects the gravity of the offense while providing a pathway for rehabilitation. As *State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984)* holds, a district court’s sentencing decision is not an abuse of discretion when it aligns with the offense’s severity and statutory goals.  

### III. The District Court Applied the Correct Legal Standard in Balancing Risks  
The defense falsely claims the court required Galbraith to prove "no risk whatsoever" from early release. The record shows no such error. The court conducted a comparative assessment: it recognized the defense’s rehabilitation argument but found the societal risk "greater." This is precisely the "informed and sensitive consideration" required by *State v. Lopez, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984)*. A Rule 35 motion is a plea for leniency, not a demand for unconditional release. *State v. Sutton, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984)*. The district court properly evaluated whether the sentence remained appropriate in light of the evolving record, concluding that continued confinement served society’s interest without foreclosing future rehabilitation. The defense’s suggestion that the court ignored parole eligibility is contradicted by the record: the court explicitly noted that the sentence structure allowed for therapy "upon parole," which the defense’s own expert confirmed would not substantially impair treatment success.  

### IV. No Cruel and Unusual Punishment Exists  
The defense’s constitutional argument fails for two reasons. First, any challenge to the original sentence is untimely; Galbraith did not appeal the judgment of conviction and may not now attack it through a Rule 35 motion. *State v. Second, the Eighth Amendment does not guarantee access to specific therapy when the sentence provides a reasonable path to rehabilitation. The defense’s expert admitted that delaying therapy until parole eligibility would not "substantially diminish" treatment success. Thus, confinement does not constitute cruel and unusual punishment. *State v. Toohill, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982)* (holding that sentences must be proportional to the offense and serve societal protection, but do not require "perfect" therapeutic conditions).  

### CONCLUSION  
The district court’s denial of the Rule 35 motion was a reasoned exercise of discretion consistent with the law. The court properly prioritized societal protection while acknowledging rehabilitation, applied the correct legal standard, and imposed a sentence proportionate to the crime. The defense’s arguments rest on mischaracterizations of the record and legal standards. This Court should affirm.

**UPON CONSIDERATION OF THE REBUTTALS TO OPENING ARGUMENTS, THE COURT RENDERS THE FOLLOWING UPDATED RULING:**

After reviewing the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, we adhere to our preliminary determination to affirm the district court’s order denying Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion. The defense’s arguments, while articulating valid concerns regarding rehabilitation, ultimately fail to demonstrate that the district court abused its discretion in denying a sentence reduction. The prosecution correctly identifies that the district court’s decision was grounded in a proper balancing of the sentencing objectives.

**1. Standard of Review**

Our review of a denial of a Rule 35 motion is limited to determining whether the district court abused its discretion. A motion to reduce a sentence is addressed to the sound discretion of the trial court and is essentially a plea for leniency. *State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); *State v. Sutton*, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). In reviewing the sentence, or the refusal to reduce it, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, along with the goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982); *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

**2. Weighing of Rehabilitation and Societal Protection**

The defense argues that the district court failed to give adequate weight to rehabilitation, asserting that the court prioritized punishment over therapeutic needs. We find this argument unpersuasive. The record clearly indicates that the district court explicitly weighed the "risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early." This is the precise balancing act required by *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

The defense contends that because the defense’s expert testified that delaying therapy until parole eligibility would not "substantially diminish" the probability of success, the court had no compelling reason to deny release based on rehabilitation concerns. This logic misconstrues the court’s discretion. The fact that rehabilitation would not be significantly impaired by a delay actually undercuts the urgency for immediate release. It allowed the court to prioritize the protection of society—specifically the safety of the infant victim and other defenseless persons—without fearing that the defendant’s rehabilitative prospects would be destroyed. Given the brutal nature of the offense—a complex skull fracture inflicted on a seven-month-old infant—and the fact that the second incident occurred while the defendant was already enrolled in counseling, the court’s determination that the risk to society outweighed the marginal benefit of immediate release was a sound exercise of discretion. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982).

Furthermore, the district court was entitled to rely on the presentence report, which described Galbraith as having a "poor social conscience" and being a "poor candidate" for therapy, rather than the more optimistic assessment offered by the defense’s expert at the Rule 35 hearing. The court did not mechanically prefer the pessimistic view; rather, it informed its discretion by considering the totality of the psychological evaluations, which supported a finding of continued risk.

**3. Proportionality of the Sentence**

The defense argues that the five-year indeterminate sentence is disproportionate given Galbraith’s youth, lack of prior criminal record, and military service. While these are mitigating factors, they must be weighed against the severity of the offense. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982).

The offense involved repeated violence against a helpless infant, resulting in permanent risk of vision impairment. The sentence imposed—an indeterminate five-year term, which translates to approximately twenty months of actual confinement before parole eligibility—is within the statutory limits and reflects the gravity of the crime. The district court’s finding that the sentence was appropriate to the severity of the crime is supported by the record. The defense’s assertion that the sentence is disproportionate ignores the legislature’s mandate to protect society from violent conduct, particularly against vulnerable victims. *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

**4. Legal Standard and Burden of Proof**

The defense claims the district court applied an erroneous legal standard by effectively shifting the burden to Galbraith to prove that early release posed "no risk whatsoever." We disagree. The district court’s finding that the "latter risk [to society] was greater" was a comparative assessment, not a demand for absolute safety. A Rule 35 motion does not entitle a defendant to release simply because he presents a plan for rehabilitation; the court must still be satisfied that such release is consistent with the protection of society. *State v. Sutton*, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). The court’s determination that the risk to society outweighed the benefits of early release was a permissible application of the law.

**5. Cruel and Unusual Punishment**

Finally, the defense argues that the denial of the Rule 35 motion constitutes cruel and unusual punishment because the therapy Galbraith needs is unavailable in confinement. The defense contends this claim is timely because it relies on evidence regarding the unavailability of treatment that arose during the Rule 35 proceeding.

Even assuming the claim is properly before this Court, it fails on the merits. The Eighth Amendment does not guarantee a prisoner access to specific forms of therapy when the sentence itself provides a reasonable opportunity for rehabilitation. The defense’s own expert admitted that delaying treatment until parole eligibility would not "substantially diminish" the probability of success. Therefore, the continued incarceration for approximately twenty months does not amount to cruel and unusual punishment. The district court’s denial of the motion did not violate the state or federal constitutions. *State v. Torres*, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); *State v. Hirshbrunner*, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983).

**CONCLUSION**

The district court gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised in the Rule 35 motion. It properly balanced the goals of protecting society and rehabilitating the defendant. The court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion for sentence reduction. The prosecution’s rebuttal correctly identifies that the district court’s ruling was consistent with the applicable legal standards and the facts of the case.

**ORDER**

The district court’s order denying relief on Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion is affirmed.

**UPON CONSIDERATION OF THE CLOSING ARGUMENTS, THE COURT RENDERS THE FOLLOWING FINAL RULING:**

After reviewing the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, we adhere to our determination to affirm the district court’s order denying Preston Galbraith’s Rule 35 motion. The defense’s arguments, while articulating valid concerns regarding rehabilitation and constitutional protections, ultimately fail to demonstrate that the district court abused its discretion in denying a sentence reduction. The prosecution correctly identifies that the district court’s decision was grounded in a proper balancing of the sentencing objectives.

**1. Standard of Review and Nature of Rule 35 Motions**

Our review of a denial of a Rule 35 motion is limited to determining whether the district court abused its discretion. A motion to reduce a sentence is addressed to the sound discretion of the trial court and is essentially a plea for leniency. *State v. Arambula*, 97 Idaho 627, 550 P.2d 130 (1976); *State v. Sutton*, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). The defense argues that this discretion must be exercised "thoughtfully," citing *State v. Russell*, 109 Idaho 723, 710 P.2d 633 (Ct.App.1985). We agree that discretion must be informed; however, the record reflects that the district court conducted the requisite thoughtful analysis. In reviewing the sentence, or the refusal to reduce it, we consider the primary objective of protecting society, along with the goals of deterrence, rehabilitation, and retribution. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982); *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

**2. Weighing of Rehabilitation and Societal Protection**

The defense argues that the district court failed to give adequate weight to rehabilitation, asserting that the court prioritized punishment over therapeutic needs. We find this argument unpersuasive. The record clearly indicates that the district court explicitly weighed the "risk of impaired rehabilitation if Galbraith remained in confinement against the risk to society if Galbraith were released early." This is the precise balancing act required by *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

The defense contends that because the defense’s expert testified that delaying therapy until parole eligibility would not "substantially diminish" the probability of success, the court had no compelling reason to deny release based on rehabilitation concerns. This logic misconstrues the court’s discretion. The fact that rehabilitation would not be significantly impaired by a delay actually undercuts the urgency for immediate release. It allowed the court to prioritize the protection of society—specifically the safety of the infant victim and other defenseless persons—without fearing that the defendant’s rehabilitative prospects would be destroyed. Given the brutal nature of the offense—a complex skull fracture inflicted on a seven-month-old infant—and the fact that the second incident occurred while the defendant was already enrolled in counseling, the court’s determination that the risk to society outweighed the marginal benefit of immediate release was a sound exercise of discretion. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982).

Furthermore, the district court was entitled to rely on the presentence report, which described Galbraith as having a "poor social conscience" and being a "poor candidate" for therapy, rather than the more optimistic assessment offered by the defense’s expert at the Rule 35 hearing. The court did not mechanically prefer the pessimistic view; rather, it informed its discretion by considering the totality of the psychological evaluations, which supported a finding of continued risk.

**3. Proportionality of the Sentence**

The defense argues that the five-year indeterminate sentence is disproportionate given Galbraith’s youth, lack of prior criminal record, and military service. While these are mitigating factors, they must be weighed against the severity of the offense. *State v. Toohill*, 103 Idaho 565, 650 P.2d 707 (Ct.App.1982).

The offense involved repeated violence against a helpless infant, resulting in permanent risk of vision impairment. The sentence imposed—an indeterminate five-year term, which translates to approximately twenty months of actual confinement before parole eligibility—is within the statutory limits and reflects the gravity of the crime. The district court’s finding that the sentence was appropriate to the severity of the crime is supported by the record. The defense’s assertion that the sentence is disproportionate ignores the legislature’s mandate to protect society from violent conduct, particularly against vulnerable victims. *State v. Lopez*, 106 Idaho 447, 680 P.2d 869 (Ct.App.1984).

**4. Legal Standard and Burden of Proof**

The defense claims the district court applied an erroneous legal standard by effectively shifting the burden to Galbraith to prove that early release posed "no risk whatsoever." We disagree. The district court’s finding that the "latter risk [to society] was greater" was a comparative assessment, not a demand for absolute safety. A Rule 35 motion does not entitle a defendant to release simply because he presents a plan for rehabilitation; the court must still be satisfied that such release is consistent with the protection of society. *State v. Sutton*, 106 Idaho 403, 679 P.2d 680 (Ct.App.1984). The court’s determination that the risk to society outweighed the benefits of early release was a permissible application of the law.

**5. Cruel and Unusual Punishment**

Finally, the defense argues that the denial of the Rule 35 motion constitutes cruel and unusual punishment because the therapy Galbraith needs is unavailable in confinement. The defense contends this claim is timely because it relies on evidence regarding the unavailability of treatment that arose during the Rule 35 proceeding.

Even assuming the claim is properly before this Court, it fails on the merits. The Eighth Amendment does not guarantee a prisoner access to specific forms of therapy when the sentence itself provides a reasonable opportunity for rehabilitation. The defense’s own expert admitted that delaying treatment until parole eligibility would not "substantially diminish" the probability of success. Therefore, the continued incarceration for approximately twenty months does not amount to cruel and unusual punishment. The district court’s denial of the motion did not violate the state or federal constitutions. *State v. Torres*, 107 Idaho 895, 693 P.2d 1097 (Ct.App.1984); *State v. Hirshbrunner*, 105 Idaho 168, 667 P.2d 271 (Ct.App.1983).

The district court gave informed and sensitive consideration to the issues raised in the Rule 35 motion. It properly balanced the goals of protecting society and rehabilitating the defendant. The court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion for sentence reduction. The prosecution’s rebuttal correctly identifies that the district court’s ruling was consistent with the applicable legal standards and the facts of the case.
