The Vermont statute mirrors this standard by requiring that conduct would cause a reasonable person to fear specific, serious harms—unlawful sexual conduct, restraint, bodily injury, or death. It does not criminalize speech based on subjective emotional reactions alone; instead, it requires an objective assessment of whether a reasonable person would fear unlawful sexual conduct, restraint, bodily injury, or death. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Vermont Supreme Court

### State v. [Defendant]

---

## INTRODUCTION

The Defendant respectfully submits that the State's opening argument fails to address the fundamental constitutional deficiencies in the criminal stalking statute at issue, 13 V.S.A. § 1062, as applied in this case. While the State correctly notes that "true threats" fall outside First Amendment protection, the statute as construed and applied violates the Constitution in multiple critical respects. The Defendant's conviction must be reversed.

---

## I. THE STATUTE IS FACIALLY UNCONSTITUTIONAL

### A. The Statute Fails to Require Subjective Intent

The State's argument that the statute satisfies *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S.Ct. 2001 (2015), is fundamentally flawed. In *Elonis*, the United States Supreme Court held that the federal threat statute required proof of subjective intent to communicate a threat, not merely that a reasonable person would perceive the statement as threatening. The Vermont statute, however, as interpreted by the trial court and as applied in this case, criminalizes conduct based solely on an objective "reasonable person" standard without requiring proof that the defendant subjectively intended to communicate a threat.

The trial court's jury instruction stated that a defendant acts "intentionally" if he "acts deliberately, knowingly, and purposely," but this language was applied to the defendant's intent to engage in conduct, not to his intent to threaten. The instruction then stated that "an expressed or actual threat need not have been made, so long as the act or acts would cause the stated effect on the reasonable person." This formulation precisely tracks the objective-only standard that *Elonis* rejected.

As the Supreme Court emphasized in *Elonis*, "the mental state required to commit a crime depends on the crime," and negligence-based standards are insufficient for threat statutes. The Vermont statute, as applied, permits conviction based on what a reasonable person would perceive, regardless of the defendant's own subjective intent—a formulation that "fail[s] to give 'fair notice' to the defendant" of the criminality of his conduct. *Elonis*, 135 S.Ct. at 2012.

### B. The Statute Is Overbroad

The State incorrectly argues that the statute is not overbroad because it targets only speech that would cause a reasonable person to fear specific physical harms. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the statutory language "would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear" encompasses a vast array of protected expression. As the Defendant demonstrated below, statements criticizing someone's artistic work, expressing disagreement with political viewpoints, or even emotionally-charged rants about a past relationship could potentially satisfy this standard. The statute's reference to "conduct without consent" further expands its reach to include constitutionally protected associational behavior.

In *United States v. Alvarez*, 567 U.S. 709 (2012), the Supreme Court struck down the Stolen Valor Act, finding that even false statements about military honors could not be criminalized absent a compelling governmental interest. Here, the statute reaches far more broadly—encompassing true statements, opinions, and expressions of emotion—so long as they might cause a reasonable person fear. This sweeps in substantial protected speech.

Second, the "course of conduct" requirement does not narrow the statute as the State suggests. Two or more acts of criticism, expressions of frustration, or attempts to communicate could satisfy this element while still encompassing protected speech. The statute's "continuity of purpose" language merely requires that the defendant have a sustained intent to communicate, not that the communications constitute true threats.

The overbreadth doctrine requires invalidation when "the statute's deterrent effect on legitimate speech is real and substantial" relative to its legitimate sweep. *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601 (1973). Here, the statute's catch-all language about "fear" of bodily injury or death, combined with its inclusion of "verbal threats" and "written, telephonic or other electronically communicated threats" without requiring subjective intent, creates an impermissible chilling effect on protected speech. A person who sends an angry email, posts a critical blog comment, or writes a book criticizing a former romantic partner risks criminal liability under this statute. This is precisely the "substantial amount of protected speech" that the overbreadth doctrine protects against.

### C. The Statute Is Content-Based

The State's argument that the statute is not content-based because true threats are categorically unprotected is a mischaracterization. While true threats may be unprotected, the Vermont statute does not limit itself to true threats as defined by the Supreme Court. It instead uses a subjective, open-ended standard that effectively allows prosecution based on the content of speech.

The statute prohibits "actions directed at a specific person which would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear" various harms. This standard requires examining the content of the defendant's speech to determine whether it would cause fear. Speech that explicitly threatens violence is treated identically to speech that merely expresses anger, criticism, or opinion—any of which could "cause a reasonable person to fear" if the listener is sufficiently sensitive.

This renders the statute content-based. As the Supreme Court explained in *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377 (1992), content-based restrictions are presumptively invalid unless they survive strict scrutiny. The State has not demonstrated that this statute satisfies that demanding standard.

---

## II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT

### A. No True Threats

The State cannot demonstrate that the Defendant's communications constituted "true threats" unprotected by the First Amendment. A true threat requires "subjective intent on the part of the speaker to cause the victim to fear the physical manifestation of the threatened violence." *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 360 (2003). The Defendant's email批评 complainant's artwork, and his self-published book contained criticisms, accusations, and emotional rants—but nowhere in the record is there evidence that the Defendant subjectively intended to communicate a serious intent to commit violence.

The 2011 blog comments criticized complainant's art as "pro-military" and contained the phrase "Go kill 'em"—but this was clearly rhetorical hyperbole about artistic disagreement, not a genuine expression of intent to harm complainant. The comment about "kill" was directed at unnamed parties who might want to kill, not at complainant herself. The 2015 book chapter, while hostile and accusatory, never explicitly threatens violence; it speculates about complainant's motivations and contains the ambiguous line "Shoot the terrorist? Or shoot the 'artist?'"—which, at most, expresses philosophical disagreement about complainant's artistic choices.

The State presented no evidence that the Defendant ever stated, explicitly or implicitly, that he intended to harm complainant. The prosecution's entire case rested on the impact of the Defendant's speech on complainant's subjective fears, not on any evidence of the Defendant's intent to threaten.

### B. No Course of Conduct Within the Statute of Limitations

Even accepting the State's theory that the statute allows prosecution based on a "course of conduct," the evidence was insufficient to establish the statutory elements within the limitations period. The State charged the Defendant in June 2015, making the limitations period extend to June 2012. The only conduct occurring after June 2012 was the Defendant's distribution of his self-published book in 2015.

The book, however, is protected speech. It is an autobiography containing the Defendant's opinions about his former relationship. While complainant found the content distressing, the book does not contain true threats. It accuses complainant of various wrongdoing, expresses emotional resentment, and contains the ambiguous line about "shooting"—but it never states that the Defendant intends to harm complainant.

The trial court erred in allowing the jury to convict based on a "course of conduct" theory that combined pre-limitations-period incidents with the 2015 book distribution. This approach effectively allowed the Defendant to be convicted for conduct that occurred years before the charging date, so long as one element—the "course of conduct"—continued into the limitations period. This violates basic principles of due process.

---

## III. THE JURY INSTRUCTION ALLOWED CONVICTION BASED ON TIME-BARRED ACTS

The trial court's jury instruction was constitutionally deficient in allowing conviction based on acts outside the statute of limitations. The instruction stated that the jury could consider "the entire course of conduct of two or more acts committed by [defendant] which you must consider as meeting all of the requirements and stated elements of the crime, even if a particular event or incident in that course of conduct does not necessarily satisfy all of the required elements."

This instruction allowed the jury to convict if they found that two or more acts, taken together, satisfied the elements of stalking—even if individual acts occurred outside the limitations period. This is fundamentally unfair.

The statute of limitations exists to protect defendants from being prosecuted for conduct that is so old that evidence has degraded, witnesses have disappeared, and memories have faded. By allowing conviction based on a "course of conduct" that began years before the charging date, the trial court deprived the Defendant of this protection.

In *United States v. Lovett*, 811 F.2d 979 (6th Cir. 1987), the court held that a statute of limitations "protects the accused from being prosecuted for crime if too much time has elapsed between the commission of the crime and the return of the indictment." This protection is meaningless if the jury can convict based on time-barred acts so long as one act falls within the limitations period.

The Defendant objected to this instruction both during the charge conference and after the instruction was given. This Court should reverse because the instruction allowed the jury to convict based on time-barred conduct in violation of the Defendant's due process rights.

---

## IV. THE JURY INSTRUCTION FAILED TO ADEQUATELY DEFINE TRUE THREATS

The trial court's instruction on true threats was constitutionally inadequate. The court instructed that the jury could convict if the Defendant's actions "would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death." The court further stated that "an expressed or actual threat need not have been made."

This instruction failed to convey several critical elements of true threats as defined by the Supreme Court:

**First**, the instruction failed to require subjective intent. The Supreme Court has consistently held that true threats require the speaker to "mean to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit a crime of violence." *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. The trial court's instruction allowed conviction based solely on the objective impact of the Defendant's speech, not on his subjective intent.

**Second**, the instruction failed to distinguish between protected and unprotected speech. The instruction provided no guidance on how to determine whether a particular statement was a true threat versus protected expression. The jury was left to guess whether the Defendant's blog comments, emails, and book chapter constituted threats or merely unpleasant but protected speech.

**Third**, the instruction's phrase "would cause a reasonable person to fear" invited the jury to assess the impact of the Defendant's speech from the complainant's subjective perspective, rather than requiring the State to prove that a reasonable person in the complainant's position would perceive a genuine threat of imminent violence.

The Defendant was entitled to an instruction that "clearly delimit[s]" the protected from the unprotected. *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444 (1969). The instruction provided failed to do so, and the Defendant's conviction must be reversed.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defendant respectfully requests that this Court reverse his conviction, hold that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is unconstitutional as applied to his conduct, and remand with instructions to dismiss the charge.

The statute, as interpreted and applied, violates the First Amendment by criminalizing protected speech without requiring subjective intent to threaten. It is overbroad, content-based, and fails to provide fair notice. The jury instruction compounded these errors by allowing conviction based on time-barred acts and failing to adequately define true threats. The evidence was insufficient to establish that the Defendant committed true threats.

The Defendant's conviction should be reversed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE v. [DEFENDANT]**

**NO. ______-**

**OPINION**

This matter comes before the Court on the Defendant’s appeal of his conviction for criminal stalking pursuant to 13 V.S.A. § 1062. The Defendant contends that the statute is facially unconstitutional, unconstitutional as applied, that the evidence was insufficient, and that the trial court erred in its jury instructions regarding the statute of limitations and the definition of true threats. After reviewing the record, the arguments of counsel, and the applicable precedent, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**I. FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY OF THE STATUTE**

The Defendant first argues that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially unconstitutional because it fails to require subjective intent and is overbroad. The Court is not persuaded by this argument.

A statute is facially invalid only if it prohibits a substantial amount of protected conduct relative to its plainly legitimate sweep. *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973). The State correctly argues that the statute targets "true threats," a category of speech unprotected by the First Amendment. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). The statute’s requirement that conduct would cause a reasonable person to fear "unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death" aligns with the objective standard historically used to identify true threats. While the Supreme Court in *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S.Ct. 2001, 2012 (2015), clarified that negligence regarding a statement's impact is insufficient for criminal liability, the Vermont statute can be read to require the defendant to "intentionally" engage in a course of conduct. The trial court read the state-of-mind requirement to apply to all elements, thereby construing the statute in a manner that avoids constitutional infirmity. See *Ashwander v. TVA*, 296 U.S. 288, 346 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring) (statutes should be construed to avoid constitutional doubts). Therefore, the statute is not facially invalid.

**II. AS-APPLIED CONSTITUTIONALITY AND JURY INSTRUCTION ON TRUE THREATS**

The Defendant argues that the statute is unconstitutional as applied and that the jury instruction failed to adequately define "true threats" by omitting the requirement of subjective intent. The Court finds this argument persuasive.

The trial court instructed the jury that "harassing another means actions... which would cause a reasonable person... to fear... [and that] an expressed or actual threat need not have been made, so long as the act or acts would cause the stated effect on the reasonable person." This instruction allowed the jury to convict based solely on the objective effect of the Defendant's conduct on a reasonable person, without requiring the State to prove that the Defendant subjectively intended to threaten the complainant.

In *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S.Ct. 2001, 2012 (2015), the Supreme Court held that "wrongdoing must be assessed through the defendant's subjective state of mind" and that "it is not enough that a reasonable person would regard the statement as a threat." The instruction here effectively permitted a conviction based on negligence—what a reasonable person would fear—rather than the Defendant's specific intent to communicate a threat. This error is not harmless, as the Defendant's speech—specifically the blog comments and the book chapter—could be characterized as protected political or artistic expression rather than true threats. See *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 360 (2003) (noting that a true threat requires a "serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence"). By failing to require the jury to find the Defendant intended to threaten, the instruction violated the First Amendment.

**III. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS AND JURY INSTRUCTION**

The Defendant further argues that the jury instruction improperly allowed the jury to consider time-barred acts to establish the "course of conduct." The Court agrees that this portion of the instruction was erroneous.

The State charged the Defendant in June 2015. Under the applicable three-year statute of limitations, 13 V.S.A. § 4501, the State was required to prove conduct occurring after June 2012. The only acts within this period were the distribution of the book in 2015 and the incident with the student in 2014.

The trial court instructed the jury that "it is the entire course of conduct of two or more acts committed by [defendant] which you must consider as meeting all of the requirements... even if a particular event... does not necessarily satisfy all of the required elements." Furthermore, the court stated that if the State proved the elements as to "at least two such events," the jury must return a guilty verdict. This instruction permitted the jury to combine the 2015 book distribution with acts from 2008, 2010, or 2011 to satisfy the statutory elements.

While a "course of conduct" theory may permit the inclusion of earlier acts to establish context or continuity, it does not permit the State to rely on time-barred acts to supply the elements of the offense that the timely act does not possess. *United States v. Lovett*, 811 F.2d 979, 981 (6th Cir. 1987) (noting the statute of limitations protects the accused from prosecution for stale acts). If the 2015 book distribution, standing alone, does not constitute stalking or harassment, the State cannot revive the charge by tethering it to older, time-barred conduct. The instruction risked convicting the Defendant for conduct that occurred outside the limitations period, thereby violating due process.

**IV. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

Given the errors in the jury instructions, the Court need not definitively resolve the sufficiency of the evidence at this time. However, the Court notes that the evidence regarding the 2015 book and the 2011 blog comments presents a close question as to whether they constitute "true threats" or protected speech. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). Without the requirement of subjective intent, the conviction cannot stand. On remand, the State must prove that the Defendant subjectively intended to threaten the complainant, not merely that his conduct was objectively frightening.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court preliminarily finds that the trial court erred in its jury instructions regarding the definition of true threats and the statute of limitations. The instruction allowed the jury to convict the Defendant without finding subjective intent to threaten, in violation of *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S.Ct. 2001 (2015), and permitted the use of time-barred acts to establish the elements of the offense. Accordingly, the conviction is reversed, and the case is remanded for a new trial with instructions consistent with this opinion.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal fails to address the fundamental constitutional deficiencies identified in this Court's preliminary ruling. While the State attempts to defend the facial constitutionality of 13 V.S.A. § 1062, the statute as written—and as applied in this case—violates the First Amendment and due process. ## I. REBUTTAL TO FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY ARGUMENT

### A. The Statute Cannot Be Saved Through Judicial Reconstruction

The prosecution argues that the statute should be read to require subjective intent for all elements, relying on *Ashwander v. TVA*, 296 U.S. 288 (1936). However, this Court should reject the prosecution's attempt to rewrite the statute through judicial construction when the statute's text is unambiguous.

The statute at issue criminalizes a person who "intentionally stalked another person" by engaging in a "course of conduct" that would cause a reasonable person to fear specific harms. The prosecution contends that "intentionally stalked" must be read to require subjective intent for all elements, including the threatening nature of the conduct. However, the statute's plain language demonstrates that "intentionally" modifies only the act of stalking itself, not the specific intent to communicate a threat. The statute requires that the defendant's conduct would cause a reasonable person to fear—not that the defendant subjectively intended to cause such fear.

In *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015), the Supreme Court addressed a federal statute that criminalized transmitting "any communication containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another." The government argued that the statute only required negligence regarding the threatening nature of the communication. The Supreme Court rejected this interpretation, holding that "wrongdoing must be assessed through the defendant's subjective state of mind" and that "it is not enough that a reasonable person would regard the statement as a threat." *Elonis*, 135 S. Ct. at 2012.

The Vermont statute suffers from the same constitutional deficiency as the statute in *Elonis*. The prosecution's argument that the statute can be saved through construction ignores the Supreme Court's clear holding that criminal threat statutes must require proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten—not merely that a reasonable person would be frightened.

### B. The Statute Is Overbroad

The prosecution argues that the statute is not overbroad because it targets only "true threats." However, the statute as written sweeps within its prohibition a substantial amount of protected speech.

Under *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973), a statute is facially invalid if it prohibits a substantial amount of protected conduct relative to its plainly legitimate sweep. The prosecution is correct that "true threats" are not protected by the First Amendment. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). However, the Vermont statute does not require that the defendant's communication constitute a "true threat" in the constitutional sense.

A "true threat" under *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 360, requires a "serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence." The Vermont statute, however, criminalizes conduct that would cause a reasonable person to fear "unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death." This broader language captures speech that may be frightening or distressing but does not rise to the level of a true threat.

For example, the defendant's blog comments criticizing the complainant's art as "pro-military" and suggesting she might "design a painting for the sympathies of those who want to kill" may have been distressing to the complainant, but such political and artistic commentary is protected speech under the First Amendment. *See Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (holding that "political hyperbole" is protected even when it expresses violent sentiment). The statute's sweep is overbroad because it captures such protected speech within its prohibition.

### C. The Statute Is Content-Based

The prosecution argues that the statute is content-neutral because it applies equally to all threatening statements regardless of content. This argument fails.

The statute criminalizes speech based on its effect on a reasonable person. This is a content-based regulation because it requires examination of the specific message conveyed to determine whether it would cause fear. *See R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 383 (1992) (striking down a hate speech ordinance because it discriminated based on content within unprotected categories).

The statute does not simply prohibit all threats; it prohibits threats that would cause fear of specific harms. This content-based determination requires the government to examine the particular message to determine whether it falls within the statute's prohibition. Such content-based regulations are subject to strict scrutiny and must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest. The prosecution has not demonstrated that the statute meets this demanding standard.

## II. REBUTTAL TO THE TRUE THREATS ARGUMENT

### A. The Prosecution Conflates Different Legal Standards

The prosecution conflates the objective "reasonable person" standard with the subjective intent requirement. The statute requires that conduct "would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear" specific harms. This is an objective standard that examines how a reasonable person would react, not whether the defendant subjectively intended to cause fear.

In *Elonis*, 135 S. Ct. at 2012, the Supreme Court explicitly held that "it is not enough that a reasonable person would regard the statement as a threat." ### B. The Prosecution's Position Would Allow Conviction Based on Negligence

Under the prosecution's theory, a defendant could be convicted of stalking based on conduct that the defendant did not know or intend to be threatening. For example, if the defendant sent an email that a reasonable person would find frightening, but the defendant believed the email was merely humorous or non-threatening, the defendant could still be convicted under the statute.

This is precisely the type of negligence-based liability that *Elonis* rejected. The Supreme Court in *Elonis* emphasized that "the mental state required to accompany a particular threat" must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt. 135 S. Ct. at 2012. The prosecution's position would allow conviction based on what a reasonable person would think, not what the defendant actually thought.

## III. REBUTTAL TO THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS ARGUMENT

If the 2015 book distribution, standing alone, does not constitute stalking or harassment, the State cannot revive the charge by tethering it to older, time-barred conduct.

### B. The 2015 Book Distribution Alone Cannot Support the Stalking Charge

The book was distributed on a street corner near the complainant's college. **OPINION**

This matter comes before the Court on the Defendant’s appeal of his conviction for criminal stalking pursuant to 13 V.S.A. § 1062. The Defendant contends that the statute is facially unconstitutional, unconstitutional as applied, that the evidence was insufficient, and that the trial court erred in its jury instructions regarding the statute of limitations and the definition of true threats. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms in part and reverses in part its preliminary ruling, ultimately finding that the conviction must be reversed and remanded.

**I. FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY OF THE STATUTE**

The Defendant argues that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially unconstitutional because it fails to require subjective intent and is overbroad. The prosecution counters that the statute can be construed to require subjective intent and targets only "true threats," a category of unprotected speech. The Court agrees with the prosecution that the statute is not facially invalid.

A statute is facially invalid only if it prohibits a substantial amount of protected conduct relative to its plainly legitimate sweep. *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973). The prosecution correctly argues that the statute targets "true threats," which are not protected by the First Amendment. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). While the defense argues the statute is overbroad because it captures protected political hyperbole, *see Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), the statute's language requiring that conduct would cause a reasonable person to fear "unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death" can be narrowly construed to apply only to true threats.

Furthermore, the prosecution persuasively argues that the statute can be read to require subjective intent. The statute criminalizes a person who "intentionally stalked another person." As established in *Ashwander v. TVA*, 296 U.S. 288, 346 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring), statutes should be construed to avoid constitutional doubts. By reading the term "intentionally" to apply to all elements of the offense, including the threatening nature of the conduct, the statute avoids the infirmities identified in *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S.Ct. 2001, 2012 (2015). The defense's argument that the text is unambiguous is unpersuasive in light of this well-established principle of constitutional avoidance. Therefore, the statute is not facially unconstitutional.

**II. AS-APPLIED CONSTITUTIONALITY AND JURY INSTRUCTION ON TRUE THREATS**

The Defendant argues that the statute is unconstitutional as applied and that the jury instruction failed to adequately define "true threats" by omitting the requirement of subjective intent. The Court finds this argument persuasive and affirms the preliminary ruling on this issue.

The prosecution argues that the statute requires subjective intent. However, the trial court's actual instruction to the jury did not reflect this construction. The court instructed the jury that "harassing another means actions... which would cause a reasonable person... to fear... [and that] an expressed or actual threat need not have been made, so long as the act or acts would cause the stated effect on the reasonable person." This instruction allowed the jury to convict based solely on the objective effect of the Defendant's conduct on a reasonable person, without requiring the State to prove that the Defendant subjectively intended to threaten the complainant.

The defense correctly notes that this instruction violates the holding of *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S.Ct. 2001, 2012 (2015), which held that "wrongdoing must be assessed through the defendant's subjective state of mind" and that "it is not enough that a reasonable person would regard the statement as a threat." The prosecution's rebuttal—that the statute *can* be read to require intent—does not cure the error in the jury instruction, which explicitly permitted a conviction based on negligence. Because the instruction allowed the jury to convict without finding subjective intent, the conviction cannot stand.

**III. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS AND JURY INSTRUCTION**

The Defendant further argues that the jury instruction improperly allowed the jury to consider time-barred acts to establish the "course of conduct." The prosecution did not address this specific argument in its rebuttal, focusing solely on facial constitutionality. Consequently, the Court accepts the defense's position and affirms the preliminary ruling on this issue.

The State charged the Defendant in June 2015. Under the applicable three-year statute of limitations, 13 V.S.A. § 4501, the State was required to prove conduct occurring after June 2012. The only acts within this period were the distribution of the book in 2015 and the incident with the student in 2014.

The trial court instructed the jury that "it is the entire course of conduct of two or more acts committed by [defendant] which you must consider as meeting all of the requirements... even if a particular event... does not necessarily satisfy all of the required elements." This instruction permitted the jury to combine the 2015 book distribution with acts from 2008, 2010, or 2011 to satisfy the statutory elements.

The defense correctly argues that this violates due process. As noted in *United States v. Lovett*, 811 F.2d 979, 981 (6th Cir. 1987), the statute of limitations protects the accused from prosecution for stale acts. While a "course of conduct" theory may permit the inclusion of earlier acts to establish context, it does not permit the State to rely on time-barred acts to supply the elements of the offense that the timely act does not possess. If the 2015 book distribution, standing alone, does not constitute stalking or harassment, the State cannot revive the charge by tethering it to older, time-barred conduct.

**IV. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

Given the errors in the jury instructions, the Court need not definitively resolve the sufficiency of the evidence. However, the Court notes that if the time-barred acts are excluded, the State's case relies heavily on the 2015 book distribution and the 2014 student incident. The defense argues the 2015 book alone cannot support the charge. Without the requirement of subjective intent and with the exclusion of time-barred acts, the sufficiency of the evidence presents a significant concern that must be addressed on remand.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the trial court erred in its jury instructions regarding the definition of true threats and the statute of limitations. The instruction allowed the jury to convict the Defendant without finding subjective intent to threaten, in violation of *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S.Ct. 2001 (2015), and permitted the use of time-barred acts to establish the elements of the offense. Accordingly, the conviction is reversed, and the case is remanded for a new trial with instructions consistent with this opinion.

**STATE v. [DEFENDANT]**

**NO. ______-**

**OPINION**

This matter comes before the Court on the Defendant’s appeal of his conviction for criminal stalking pursuant to 13 V.S.A. § 1062. The Defendant contends that the statute is facially unconstitutional, unconstitutional as applied, that the evidence was insufficient, and that the trial court erred in its jury instructions regarding the statute of limitations and the definition of true threats. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. For the reasons set forth below, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling in part and reverses the conviction.

**I. FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY OF THE STATUTE**

The Defendant argues that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially unconstitutional because it fails to require subjective intent and is overbroad. The prosecution counters that the statute can be construed to require subjective intent and targets only "true threats," a category of unprotected speech. The defense concedes in its closing statement that the statute is not facially unconstitutional. The Court agrees with the prosecution that the statute is not facially invalid.

A statute is facially invalid only if it prohibits a substantial amount of protected conduct relative to its plainly legitimate sweep. *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973). The prosecution correctly argues that the statute targets "true threats," which are not protected by the First Amendment. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). While the defense argues the statute is overbroad because it captures protected political hyperbole, *see Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), the statute's language requiring that conduct would cause a reasonable person to fear "unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death" can be narrowly construed to apply only to true threats.

Furthermore, the prosecution persuasively argues that the statute can be read to require subjective intent. The statute criminalizes a person who "intentionally stalked another person." As established in *Ashwander v. TVA*, 296 U.S. 288, 346 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring), statutes should be construed to avoid constitutional doubts. By reading the term "intentionally" to apply to all elements of the offense, including the threatening nature of the conduct, the statute avoids the infirmities identified in *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S.Ct. 2001, 2012 (2015). The defense's concession on this issue confirms the statute's facial validity. Therefore, the statute is not facially unconstitutional.

**II. AS-APPLIED CONSTITUTIONALITY AND JURY INSTRUCTION ON TRUE THREATS**

The Defendant argues that the statute was unconstitutionally applied and that the jury instruction failed to adequately define "true threats" by omitting the requirement of subjective intent. The Court finds this argument persuasive.

The prosecution argues that the statute requires subjective intent. However, the trial court's actual instruction to the jury did not reflect this construction. The court instructed the jury that "harassing another means actions... which would cause a reasonable person... to fear... [and that] an expressed or actual threat need not have been made, so long as the act or acts would cause the stated effect on the reasonable person." This instruction allowed the jury to convict based solely on the objective effect of the Defendant's conduct on a reasonable person, without requiring the State to prove that the Defendant subjectively intended to threaten the complainant.

The defense correctly notes that this instruction violates the holding of *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S.Ct. 2001, 2012 (2015), which held that "wrongdoing must be assessed through the defendant's subjective state of mind" and that "it is not enough that a reasonable person would regard the statement as a threat." The prosecution's rebuttal—that the statute *can* be read to require intent—does not cure the error in the jury instruction, which explicitly permitted a conviction based on negligence. Because the instruction allowed the jury to convict without finding subjective intent, the conviction cannot stand.

**III. STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS AND JURY INSTRUCTION**

The Defendant further argues that the jury instruction improperly allowed the jury to consider time-barred acts to establish the "course of conduct." The prosecution did not address this specific argument in its rebuttal, focusing solely on facial constitutionality. Consequently, the Court accepts the defense's position on this issue.

The State charged the Defendant in June 2015. Under the applicable three-year statute of limitations, 13 V.S.A. § 4501, the State was required to prove conduct occurring after June 2012. The only acts within this period were the distribution of the book in 2015 and the incident with the student in 2014.

The trial court instructed the jury that "it is the entire course of conduct of two or more acts committed by [defendant] which you must consider as meeting all of the requirements... even if a particular event... does not necessarily satisfy all of the required elements." This instruction permitted the jury to combine the 2015 book distribution with acts from 2008, 2010, or 2011 to satisfy the statutory elements.

The defense correctly argues that this violates due process. As noted in *United States v. Lovett*, 811 F.2d 979, 981 (6th Cir. 1987), the statute of limitations protects the accused from prosecution for stale acts. While a "course of conduct" theory may permit the inclusion of earlier acts to establish context, it does not permit the State to rely on time-barred acts to supply the elements of the offense that the timely act does not possess. If the 2015 book distribution, standing alone, does not constitute stalking or harassment, the State cannot revive the charge by tethering it to older, time-barred conduct.

**IV. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

Given the errors in the jury instructions, the Court need not definitively resolve the sufficiency of the evidence. However, the Court notes that if the time-barred acts are excluded, the State's case relies heavily on the 2015 book distribution and the 2014 student incident. The defense argues the 2015 book alone cannot support the charge. Without the requirement of subjective intent and with the exclusion of time-barred acts, the sufficiency of the evidence presents a significant concern that must be addressed on remand.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the trial court erred in its jury instructions regarding the definition of true threats and the statute of limitations. The instruction allowed the jury to convict the Defendant without finding subjective intent to threaten, in violation of *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S.Ct. 2001 (2015), and permitted the use of time-barred acts to establish the elements of the offense. Accordingly, the conviction is reversed, and the case is remanded for a new trial with instructions consistent with this opinion.
