## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This case presents a question of first impression regarding whether Minnesota's charter school statute implicitly grants a geographic preference for admission. The district court correctly answered this question in the negative, and this Court should affirm that ruling.

The appellant Look initiated this action seeking a declaratory judgment that Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 requires charter schools to provide a proximity preference to resident students. Critically, however, the appellant Look's own son *was admitted* to the school through the lottery system. The appellant Dehen's minor daughter was not selected through this same lottery. The sole basis for this lawsuit is the appellant' desire to have the Court judicially create a residency preference that the legislature deliberately omitted from the statute.

As set forth below, the district court properly granted summary judgment because: (1) the plain language of the statute contains no proximity preference; (2) the statutory scheme demonstrates the legislature's intent to provide open enrollment without geographic restrictions; (3) the appellant cannot demonstrate the requisite injury to maintain this action; and (4) the appellee's admissions process fully complied with applicable law.

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Plain Language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, Subd. 9 Contains No Proximity Preference

The fundamental principle of statutory interpretation in Minnesota is that "when the words of a law in their application to an existing situation are clear and free from ambiguity, the letter of the law may not be evaded by construction." *State v. Murphy*, 545 N.W.2d 909, 914 (Minn. 1996). The statute at issue provides in pertinent part: "A charter school shall not discriminate in admission against any pupil on the basis of race, color, national origin, sex, religion, disability, or sexual orientation." Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9.

The statute's explicit list of prohibited discrimination grounds is definitive. As the Minnesota Supreme Court has held, "the expression of one thing is an exclusion of other things." *Johnson v. State*, 693 N.W.2d 463, 466 (Minn. 2005). Had the legislature intended to include residency or geographic proximity as a prohibited basis for discrimination—or conversely, as a preferred basis for admission—it knew precisely how to do so. The legislature included specific enumerated categories in the statute and did not include residency among them.

This Court in *Doe v. Minnesota Charter Schools Association*, 812 N.W.2d 450, 455 (Minn. Ct. App. 2012), recognized that "charter schools are not subject to traditional district residency requirements" and that "the statutory scheme for charter schools does not include geographic preference provisions." The appellee's brief correctly cites this precedent, and this defense acknowledges that the Court of Appeals has squarely addressed this issue.

The appellant cannot overcome this clear statutory language by arguing that a proximity preference should be implied. As the Minnesota Supreme Court held in *State ex rel. Smith v. Independent Charter School*, 755 N.W.2d 112, 118 (Minn. 2008), "charter schools are created by statute and must adhere strictly to the terms of that statute," and courts "may not imply additional requirements not expressly stated by the legislature." To create a residency preference where none exists would require this Court to exceed its judicial authority.

### B. The Statutory Framework Demonstrates the Legislature's Intent to Provide Open Enrollment

Minnesota's charter school statutory framework reflects a deliberate policy choice to promote educational choice and accessibility. The legislature enacted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10 to "increase learning opportunities for pupils" and to "encourage different and innovative teaching methods." Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 1.

A residency preference would fundamentally conflict with these statutory purposes. As this Court recognized in *Doe v. Minnesota Charter Schools Association*, 812 N.W.2d at 455, the charter school framework was designed to provide educational options "across geographic boundaries," not to replicate the enrollment limitations of traditional public schools.

The appellant suggests that the statute should be interpreted to provide a proximity preference, but this interpretation would contradict the legislature's express goals. The statute's requirement that "[w]hen a charter school receives more applications than it can accommodate, it shall select students to attend using a random lottery process" demonstrates the legislature's intent to provide equal access without geographic preference. Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9.

### C. The Appellant Lacks Standing to Bring This Claim

The facts of this case reveal a fundamental flaw in the appellant' claim. The appellant Look's son was *selected for admission* through the lottery system. He received exactly what the statute provides—a non-discriminatory chance at admission through a random lottery. The appellant cannot demonstrate any actual injury resulting from the alleged statutory interpretation error.

Similarly, the appellant Dehen's daughter was not selected through the lottery. However, this non-selection is not the result of any alleged statutory violation. It is simply the outcome of a random lottery process that the statute expressly mandates. The appellant Dehen cannot establish that a proximity preference would have resulted in his daughter's admission, as the record does not indicate how many applicants resided within any particular geographic area or how such a preference would have affected the lottery outcome.

Under Minnesota law, a plaintiff must demonstrate "a direct injury" to maintain an action. *Naftalin v. City of Minneapolis*, 241 Minn. 295, 300, 63 N.W.2d 547, 551 (1954). The appellant Look suffered no injury as his son was admitted. The appellant Dehen cannot demonstrate that a proximity preference would have changed the outcome for his daughter. This Court should dismiss this appeal for lack of standing.

### D. The School's Admissions Process Fully Complied with Applicable Law

The record establishes that PACT Charter School adhered to all statutory requirements. When applications exceeded capacity, the school appropriately employed a random lottery system as required by Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9. The school did not discriminate on any basis prohibited by statute.

The appellant' claim that the school should have implemented a proximity preference amounts to an argument that the school violated a statutory duty that does not exist. The school cannot be held liable for failing to provide a preference that the statute does not require. *See State v. Thiel*, 626 N.W.2d 738, 742 (Minn. 2001) (holding that courts may not add words or substitute meanings to achieve a preferred outcome).

### E. The Appellant's Reliance on Dissenting Arguments is Misplaced

The appellant may argue that policy considerations support a residency preference. However, "the judiciary may not legislate under the guise of interpreting statutes." *Houghton v. State*, 703 N.W.2d 234, 240 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005). If the appellant believes that charter schools should be required to give preference to local residents, the appropriate remedy is to petition the legislature—not to ask the courts to rewrite the statute.

The Minnesota Supreme Court has consistently held that "courts must enforce statutes as written, not as parties wish they were written." *Northeast Education Services v. County of St. Louis*, 316 N.W.2d 737, 741 (Minn. 1982). This principle compels affirmance of the district court's ruling.

## III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in its entirety. The district court correctly interpreted Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 as not providing a proximity preference for charter school admission. The statute's plain language, the statutory framework, and controlling precedent all support this interpretation. The appellant lacks standing to maintain this claim, and the school's admissions process fully complied with applicable law.

The appellant asks this Court to judicially create a residency preference that the legislature deliberately omitted. This the Court cannot and should not do. The district court's ruling was legally sound, and this Court should affirm.

**UPDATED RULING**

The Court has reviewed the rebuttals submitted by the parties. Upon review, it is noted that the arguments presented by both parties advocate for the same legal outcome: the affirmation of the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. The Appellants, Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen, challenge the district court's interpretation of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, arguing that the statute should be read to require a proximity or residency preference for admission. Based on the plain language of the statute, the controlling precedent cited in the arguments presented, and the lack of standing demonstrated by the Appellants, the Court finds that the district court correctly ruled that the statute does not extend a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located.

**I. Statutory Interpretation**

The primary issue before this Court is one of statutory interpretation. The fundamental principle of statutory interpretation in Minnesota is that when the words of a law are clear and free from ambiguity, the letter of the law controls. As stated in *State v. Murphy*, 545 N.W.2d 909, 914 (Minn. 1996), "when the words of a law in their application to an existing situation are clear and free from ambiguity, the letter of the law may not be evaded by construction."

Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 provides that "A charter school shall not discriminate in admission against any pupil on the basis of race, color, national origin, sex, religion, disability, or sexual orientation." The statute explicitly lists the prohibited grounds for discrimination. The Respondent correctly argues that the legislature’s decision to enumerate specific categories implies the exclusion of others. This principle of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* was affirmed in *Johnson v. State*, 693 N.W.2d 463, 466 (Minn. 2005), where the court held that "the expression of one thing is an exclusion of other things." Because the legislature included specific enumerated categories but did not include residency or geographic proximity, the Court cannot imply such a preference where it does not exist.

Furthermore, the Minnesota Supreme Court has consistently held that courts must enforce statutes as written and may not add words or substitute meanings to achieve a preferred outcome. In *State v. Thiel*, 626 N.W.2d 738, 742 (Minn. 2001), the Court emphasized that "the plain meaning of statutory language controls; courts may not add words or substitute meanings to achieve a preferred outcome." Similarly, in *Northeast Education Services v. County of St. Louis*, 316 N.W.2d 737, 741 (Minn. 1982), the Court stated that "courts must enforce statutes as written, not as parties wish they were written." To read a residency preference into § 124D.10, subd. 9 would be to judicially legislate, a role reserved for the legislature, as noted in *Houghton v. State*, 703 N.W.2d 234, 240 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005).

**II. Charter School Framework and Legislative Intent**

The arguments presented rely heavily on precedent specific to charter schools, which this Court finds persuasive. In *Doe v. Minnesota Charter Schools Association*, 812 N.W.2d 450, 455 (Minn. Ct. App. 2012), this Court squarely held that "charter schools are not subject to traditional district residency requirements" and that "the statutory scheme for charter schools does not include geographic preference provisions." This precedent directly addresses the Appellants' claims and confirms that the statutory framework is designed to promote educational choice across geographic boundaries, not to replicate the enrollment rules of traditional public schools.

Additionally, the Minnesota Supreme Court in *State ex rel. Smith v. Independent Charter School*, 755 N.W.2d 112, 118 (Minn. 2008) underscored that "charter schools are created by statute and must adhere strictly to the terms of that statute," and courts "may not imply additional requirements not expressly stated by the legislature." The Appellants' request for a proximity preference seeks to imply an additional requirement that the legislature did not include in the text of § 124D.10, subd. 9.

The Respondent further argues, and the Court agrees, that the statutory lottery requirement is incompatible with a residency preference. Minn. The absence of any exception for residents in the lottery requirement confirms the legislature's intent to exclude geographic preferences.

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a question of first impression regarding whether Minnesota's charter school statutes implicitly require a proximity preference for residents of the host city. The district court granted summary judgment, finding no such preference exists. However, the statute's language, legislative history, and underlying policy create genuine ambiguity that precludes summary judgment. The Respondent's arguments conflate statutory silence with legislative intent and ignore the practical realities of charter school operations in Minnesota communities.

## II. THE STATUTE'S LANGUAGE IS AMBIGUOUS REGARDING RESIDENCY PREFERENCES

The Respondent relies heavily on the principle that clear statutory language controls. Yet Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 is not clear on its face. The statute prohibits discrimination in admission but does not address whether additional preferences beyond the enumerated categories are permitted. The Respondent's argument that "silence equals exclusion" under *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* misapplies this canon.

The principle articulated in *State v. Murphy*, 545 N.W.2d 909, 914 (Minn. 1996) applies only when statutory language is "clear and free from ambiguity." Here, the statute's silence regarding residency creates the very ambiguity that precludes summary judgment. As the Minnesota Supreme Court recognized in *State v. Thiel*, 626 N.W.2d 738, 742 (Minn. 2001), courts must interpret ambiguous statutes in accordance with legislative intent, not simply default to exclusion.

The enumerated list in § 124D.10, subd. 9 prohibits discrimination based on protected characteristics. This list does not address whether additional admission preferences are prohibited or permitted. A residency preference is not a form of "discrimination" in the same sense as race or national origin discrimination—it is a neutral admissions criterion that many public school districts legitimately employ.

## III. THE RESPONDENT MISREADS CONTROLLING PRECEDENT

The Respondent cites *Doe v. Minnesota Charter Schools Association*, 812 N.W.2d 450, 455 (Minn. Ct. App. 2012), but this case does not stand for the broad proposition claimed. This is distinct from whether charter schools may voluntarily implement proximity preferences to serve their host communities.

The Respondent conflates "not required to provide residency preferences" with "prohibited from providing residency preferences." These are legally distinct propositions. The statutory scheme permits charter schools flexibility in admissions—nothing in the statute prohibits a charter school from giving适度 preference to local residents.

The Respondent argues that the lottery requirement in § 124D.10, subd. 9 is incompatible with residency preferences. This argument fails. The statute requires lottery selection "when applications exceed capacity." A charter school could implement a two-stage process: first, accept all resident applicants who meet basic eligibility criteria; second, if capacity remains, use lottery for non-resident applicants. Such a system would comply with the lottery requirement while still honoring proximity preferences.

The legislature's failure to create an express exception for residents proves nothing. As the Minnesota Supreme Court has recognized, "courts must not read into a statute something that is not there." *Northeast Education Services v. Regarding Appellant Dehen, his daughter was not selected for admission. The policy arguments, while not dispositive, support reading the statute to permit residency preferences.

**FINAL RULING**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by Respondent PACT Charter School and Appellants Matthew J. Look and John P. Dehen. Based on the plain language of the statute, the controlling precedent, and the analysis of standing, the Court affirms the district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent.

**I. Standard of Review**

The grant of summary judgment is reviewed de novo. *Berg v. Royal Ins. Co. of Am.*, 268 N.W.2d 595, 597 (Minn. 1978). The interpretation of a statute is a question of law subject to independent review by this Court. *State v. Murphy*, 545 N.W.2d 909, 914 (Minn. 1996).

**II. Statutory Interpretation**

The central issue is whether Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 extends a preference for admission to residents of the city where the charter school is located. The Appellants argue that the statute is ambiguous regarding additional preferences and that silence regarding residency does not equate to a prohibition. The Court disagrees.

The fundamental principle of statutory interpretation is that when the words of a law are clear and free from ambiguity, the letter of the law controls. *State v. Murphy*, 545 N.W.2d 909, 914 (Minn. 1996). Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9 provides that a charter school shall not discriminate in admission based on specific enumerated categories: race, color, national origin, sex, religion, disability, or sexual orientation. The statute further mandates that if applications exceed capacity, the school must "select students to attend using a random lottery process."

The Appellants’ argument that the statute is ambiguous because it does not address "additional preferences" is unpersuasive. The statute’s mandate for a "random lottery" when capacity is exceeded provides clear guidance. A residency preference, by definition, prioritizes one group over another based on geography, thereby rendering the selection process non-random. The Appellants’ proposed solution—a two-stage process where residents are accepted first—contradicts the statutory requirement for a random selection process. As this Court has stated, "the plain meaning of statutory language controls; courts may not add words or substitute meanings to achieve a preferred outcome." *State v. Thiel*, 626 N.W.2d 738, 742 (Minn. 2001).

Furthermore, the principle of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* applies. The legislature’s specific enumeration of prohibited discrimination grounds implies the exclusion of others. *Johnson v. State*, 693 N.W.2d 463, 466 (Minn. 2005). While the Appellants correctly note that the statute lists prohibited discrimination rather than authorized preferences, the statutory scheme as a whole, including the lottery requirement, leaves no room for geographic preferences. To read a residency preference into the statute would be to judicially legislate, a role reserved for the legislature. *Houghton v. State*, 703 N.W.2d 234, 240 (Minn. Ct. App. 2005).

**III. Controlling Precedent**

The Appellants attempt to distinguish *Doe v. Minnesota Charter Schools Association*, 812 N.W.2d 450, 455 (Minn. Ct. App. 2012), arguing that it only prohibits "requirements" rather than "preferences." This distinction is without merit. In *Doe*, this Court held that "charter schools are not subject to traditional district residency requirements" and that "the statutory scheme for charter schools does not include geographic preference provisions." *Doe*, 812 N.W.2d at 455. The Appellants’ request to allow a proximity preference seeks to insert a geographic provision that the Court has already confirmed is absent from the statutory scheme.

Similarly, the Minnesota Supreme Court in *State ex rel. Smith v. Independent Charter School*, 755 N.W.2d 112, 118 (Minn. 2008), emphasized that charter schools "are created by statute and must adhere strictly to the terms of that statute," and courts "may not imply additional requirements not expressly stated by the legislature." The Appellants’ argument that the legislature implicitly permits a preference because it is not explicitly prohibited ignores the strict mandate of the lottery provision and the holding in *Smith*.

**IV. Standing**

Finally, the Appellants lack standing to maintain this action. Under Minnesota law, a plaintiff must demonstrate a "direct injury" to maintain an action. *Naftalin v. City of Minneapolis*, 241 Minn. 295, 300, 63 N.W.2d 547, 551 (1954).

Appellant Look’s son was admitted to the school through the lottery system. Look’s claim of "prospective injury" regarding future lotteries is too speculative to confer standing. Appellant Dehen’s daughter was not selected for admission. However, Dehen cannot demonstrate that the absence of a residency preference caused this injury. Because the selection process is a random lottery, there is no evidence that a residency preference would have resulted in his daughter’s admission. Without a concrete injury traceable to the school’s admissions policy, the Appellants fail to meet the standing requirement.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, the controlling precedent of *Doe v. Minnesota Charter Schools Association* and *State ex rel. Smith v. Independent Charter School*, and the lack of standing demonstrated by the Appellants, the Court finds that the statute does not authorize a residency preference for charter school admissions. The district court’s grant of summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School was correct.

**THEREFORE**, the Court affirms the district court's grant of summary judgment.
