### OPENING ARGUMENT FOR THE PROSECUTION  

Your Honor, the trial court correctly denied Blair Johnson’s motion to withdraw his guilty plea. His claim—that his plea was not knowing, voluntary, or intelligent because counsel allegedly failed to inform him that the sentencing cap would preclude appellate review of his sentence—fails as a matter of law. The record conclusively demonstrates that Johnson’s plea was entered with full understanding of its consequences, and his assertion of error is built on a foundational misconception: **there is no right to appeal a sentence imposed within the terms of a negotiated sentencing cap**. Because no such right exists, counsel’s alleged failure to discuss it cannot constitute ineffective assistance or render the plea involuntary.  

#### I. THE PLEA WAS KNOWING, VOLUNTARY, AND INTELLIGENT BECAUSE JOHNSON UNDERSTOOD THE SENTENCING RANGE AND ITS CONSEQUENCES.  
At the plea hearing, the trial court explicitly and thoroughly admonished Johnson regarding the consequences of his plea. The court stated:  
> “This is a special Class X felony in that it is a class—we call Class X plus 15. That means the minimum mandatory prison sentence is 21 years. And normally a Class X plus 15 would be 21 to 45 but because of your partially negotiated plea agreement calls for a cap of 40 years in the Department of Corrections, your sentence will be an exact term between 21 and 40 years, and when you are released it will be followed immediately by mandatory supervised release of three years. Do you understand that, sir?”  
Johnson replied, “Yes.”  

This colloquy satisfies the constitutional mandate that a guilty plea must be knowing and voluntary. As the U.S. Supreme Court held in *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 244 (1969), a plea is valid only if the defendant “intelligently and voluntarily” waives his rights, with the trial court ensuring he understands the “direct consequences” of the plea. Illinois law reinforces this principle: in *People v. Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d 326, 340 (1992), the Illinois Supreme Court held that a plea is knowing and voluntary if the defendant “understands the nature of the charges, the range of possible penalties, and the rights he is relinquishing.” Here, Johnson was explicitly told the minimum (21 years), maximum (45 years) without the plea, and the cap (40 years) he accepted. He confirmed his understanding.  

Critically, Johnson’s own testimony at the motion hearing confirmed he understood the sentencing exposure. When asked whether he knew the “most [he] could receive” at trial was 45 years, he responded, “Because they said that considering I’m only charged with one incident, that it would only be the one incident I could get charged with at the most was 45.” This proves he was aware of the 45-year maximum—and that the 40-year cap was a favorable reduction. His claim that counsel failed to inform him of “appellate consequences” is thus irrelevant: **the cap itself eliminated his ability to appeal the sentence**, as the law recognizes no such right for sentences within negotiated agreements.  

#### II. THERE IS NO RIGHT TO APPEAL A SENTENCE WITHIN A NEGOTIATED CAP, SO COUNSEL’S FAILURE TO DISCUSS IT CANNOT INVALIDE THE PLEA.  
Johnson’s motion rests on a false premise: that counsel had a duty to advise him about appellate rights regarding his sentence. But under settled Illinois law, **a defendant who pleads guilty with a sentencing cap has no right to appeal the sentence as long as it falls within the agreed range**. This principle is clear in *People v. Hester*, 197 Ill. 2d 211, 220–21 (2001), where the Illinois Supreme Court held:  
> “When a defendant pleads guilty pursuant to a plea agreement that includes a sentencing cap, the defendant has no right to appeal the sentence imposed as long as it is within the cap. The plea agreement itself forecloses appellate review of the sentence.”  

The *Hester* Court further explained that such agreements are “binding contracts,” and a defendant “cannot later complain that the sentence was too harsh if it falls within the agreed-upon range.” This is not a matter of counsel’s oversight—it is a legal bar. Because Johnson received exactly 40 years (the cap), his sentence is unreviewable on appeal. Counsel cannot be ineffective for failing to advise him of a non-existent right. See *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984) (ineffective assistance requires showing deficient performance *and* prejudice; no prejudice exists where there is no right to appeal).  

Johnson’s motion incorrectly conflates the *ability to appeal the sentence* with the *voluntariness of the plea*. But *Hester* makes clear: the cap is a substantive part of the plea bargain, and accepting it means surrendering the right to challenge the sentence. The trial court was correct to reject Johnson’s attempt to retroactively invalidate the plea based on a “right” that never existed.

### INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Appellant Blair Johnson ("Johnson" or "Defendant") respectfully submits this opening brief in support of his appeal from the denial of his motion to withdraw his guilty plea. Johnson confirmed he understood. (¶ 6.)

Critically, the court did not advise Johnson that by accepting the sentencing cap, he would forfeit his right to appeal the length of his sentence. (¶¶ 6, 17.) The court also did not advise Johnson that his sentence, if within the cap, would be unreviewable on appeal. (¶¶ 6, 17.)

Before accepting the plea, the court asked Johnson if he was satisfied with his attorney's representation. Johnson replied, "Not really, but I'll have to deal with it." (¶ 5.) ### II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

The trial court's denial of a motion to withdraw a guilty plea is reviewed for abuse of discretion. *People v. Davis*, 145 Ill. 2d 240, 244 (1991). The court should allow a defendant to withdraw his plea if necessary to correct a "manifest injustice." *People v. Brown*, 54 Ill. 2d 21, 24 (1973). Questions of constitutional law are reviewed de novo. *People v. Hare*, 215 Ill. 2d 504, 516 (2006).

---

### III. ARGUMENT

#### A. PLEA COUNSEL RENDERED INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE BY FAILING TO ADVISE JOHNSON THAT THE SENTENCING CAP WOULD WAIVE HIS RIGHT TO APPELLATE REVIEW OF HIS SENTENCE.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to effective assistance of counsel. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 686 (1984). To establish ineffective assistance, a defendant must show: (1) counsel's performance was deficient, falling below an objective standard of reasonableness; and (2) the deficient performance prejudiced the defense. *Id.* at 687-88.

The prosecution argues that counsel's failure to advise Johnson about appellate rights is irrelevant because "there is no right to appeal a sentence within a negotiated cap." This argument fails for several reasons.

**First, the existence of a legal bar to appeal does not eliminate counsel's duty to advise on that consequence.** The right to effective assistance of counsel includes the duty to inform a defendant of the direct consequences of a plea. *People v. Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d 326, 340 (1992). While the prosecution is correct that *People v. Hester*, 197 Ill. 2d 211 (2001), holds that a defendant cannot appeal a sentence within a sentencing cap, this does not mean counsel may omit this information when advising a defendant whether to accept a plea. Indeed, the very reason such appeals are barred is that the defendant voluntarily agreed to forego appellate review as part of the plea bargain. *Id.* at 220-21. A defendant cannot knowingly and voluntarily waive a right unless he is informed of that right in the first instance. *See Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742, 748 n.6 (1970) (waiver must be "knowing and voluntary").

Here, the record shows Johnson was never advised that accepting the sentencing cap would preclude appellate review of his sentence. (¶ 17.) This omission is particularly significant because the prosecution's own argument acknowledges that the cap's effect on appellate rights is a "consequence" of the plea. Yet counsel failed to communicate this consequence to Johnson. This constitutes deficient performance.

**Second, Johnson has demonstrated prejudice.** Under *Strickland*, prejudice exists if there is a "reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different." 466 U.S. at 694. Here, Johnson testified that had he known "the effect of the sentencing cap on [his] ability to appeal his sentence," he "would not have entered the plea." (¶ 15.) This testimony, if believed, establishes that Johnson's decision to accept the plea was directly influenced by counsel's failure to advise him of this consequence.

The prosecution argues Johnson cannot show prejudice because he received the benefit of the cap—i.e., 40 years instead of a potential 45-year maximum. But this analysis is flawed. The relevant inquiry is not whether the plea provided some benefit, but whether the defendant would have accepted the plea *on the same terms* had he been fully informed. *See Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52, 60 (1985) (prejudice exists if defendant would not have pleaded guilty had he been properly advised). Johnson has alleged that he would not have entered the plea at all—presumably because he would have preferred to preserve his appellate rights or negotiate different terms. This is sufficient to establish prejudice.

**Third, the trial court's admonishments did not cure counsel's omission.** The prosecution emphasizes that the trial court advised Johnson of the sentencing range (21 to 40 years). But the court never advised Johnson that by accepting the cap, he was waiving his right to appeal his sentence. (¶¶ 6, 17.) A trial court's admonishments regarding the maximum and minimum penalties do not substitute for counsel's duty to advise on appellate consequences. *See Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d at 340 (defendant must understand "the rights he is relinquishing" including the right to appeal). The court in *Frieberg* held that a plea is not knowing and voluntary unless the defendant understands "the nature of the charges, the range of possible penalties, and the rights he is relinquishing." *Id.* (emphasis added). The right to appeal one's sentence is a fundamental right that Johnson was entitled to understand before waiving. *See People v. Dye*, 215 Ill. 2d 183, 193 (2005) (right to appeal is a substantial right).

The prosecution's reliance on *Hester* is misplaced. In *Hester*, the defendant did not claim that counsel failed to advise him of the consequences of the sentencing cap; rather, he challenged the trial court's authority to impose a consecutive sentence. 197 Ill. 2d at 215-16. The Illinois Supreme Court held that a defendant who accepts a sentencing cap cannot later appeal a sentence within that cap. *Id.* at 220-21. But *Hester* does not address whether counsel's failure to advise on this consequence constitutes ineffective assistance. Indeed, *Hester* assumed the defendant was aware of the cap's effect. Here, by contrast, Johnson specifically alleges he was not advised that the cap would preclude appellate review.

#### B. JOHNSON'S PLEA WAS NOT KNOWINGLY, VOLUNTARILY, AND INTELLIGENTLY ENTERED BECAUSE HE WAS NOT ADVISED OF ALL MATERIAL CONSEQUENCES.

The prosecution argues that Johnson's plea satisfied constitutional requirements because he understood the sentencing range. This argument fails because it ignores the requirement that a defendant understand "the rights he is relinquishing." *Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d at 340.

**A guilty plea is valid only if it is entered knowingly, voluntarily, and intelligently.** *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242-44 (1969). The trial court must ensure the defendant understands the "direct consequences" of the plea, including the rights being waived. *Id.* at 244; *Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d at 340. While the sentencing range is a direct consequence, the waiver of appellate rights is equally material. A defendant who understands he will receive 21 to 40 years but does not understand that he cannot challenge that sentence on appeal has not made a fully informed decision.

Here, the record shows the trial court never advised Johnson that by accepting the cap, he was waiving his right to appeal his sentence. (¶ 6.) The prosecution argues this omission is irrelevant because "no such right exists" once the cap is accepted. But this conflates the existence of a right with the knowledge of that right. A waiver is knowing only if the defendant knows of the right being waived. *See Brady*, 397 U.S. at 748 n.6. Johnson could not knowingly waive a right he was never told he had.

The prosecution's argument that Johnson "understood" the plea because he knew the maximum was 45 years is unavailing. Johnson knew the *numerical* range, but he did not know the *legal* effect of accepting the cap. The distinction is critical. A defendant may know that he will receive between 21 and 40 years yet still not understand that he cannot appeal if the sentence falls within that range. This is precisely the information Johnson claims he was never given. (¶ 17.)

#### C. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY DENYING JOHNSON'S MOTION TO WITHDRAW HIS GUILTY PLEA.

The prosecution argues the trial court properly denied the motion because "the plea was as by the book as it could be." (¶ 18.) This characterization ignores the substantial evidence that Johnson's plea was not fully knowing and voluntary.

**The trial court's inquiry into counsel's representation was inadequate.** When Johnson initially stated he was "not really" satisfied with counsel and that he "just don't feel like I was represented right," the court granted a brief recess but did not conduct a meaningful inquiry. (¶ 5.) After the recess, counsel stated Johnson was "not upset with his representation" but rather "upset with the State's plea offer." (¶ 5.) The court accepted this characterization without further inquiry and asked Johnson if he was satisfied—to which Johnson simply said "yes." (¶ 5.)

This exchange is insufficient to establish that Johnson was satisfied with counsel's representation. Johnson's initial statement—"Not really, but I'll have to deal with it"—revealed dissatisfaction. The court's follow-up questions did not probe the nature of the alleged deficient performance. A defendant who states he was "not represented right" is entitled to a more searching inquiry than a 20-minute recess followed by a cursory "are you satisfied now?" *See People v. Curry*, 2013 IL App (1st) 110893, ¶ 27 (trial court must conduct thorough inquiry when defendant expresses dissatisfaction with counsel).

**Johnson's testimony at the motion hearing establishes grounds for withdrawal.** At the hearing on his motion, Johnson testified that plea counsel "never advised him that agreeing to a sentencing cap would affect his ability to appeal his sentence." (¶ 17.) He further testified that counsel did not discuss his options "prior to defendant's guilty plea" and simply told him "if I didn't take [the plea] today that we had to go to trial, and that I would receive 90 years or something." (¶ 17.) This testimony, if believed, establishes that counsel failed to provide adequate advice regarding the plea.

The trial court did not make a credibility determination rejecting Johnson's testimony. Instead, the court simply stated the plea was "as by the book as it could be." (¶ 18.) This reasoning is legally insufficient. Even if the plea colloquy was technically proper, counsel's deficient performance in advising Johnson before the plea hearing can still render the plea involuntary. *See Hill*, 474 U.S. at 56-60 (ineffective assistance of counsel can render plea involuntary).

#### D. THE SENTENCING DISPARITY BETWEEN JOHNSON AND HIS CODEFENDANT SUPPORTS HIS CLAIM OF PREJUDICE.

The prosecution has not addressed Johnson's argument that his 40-year sentence is grossly disproportionate to his codefendant's 12-year sentence. (¶ 14.) This disparity is relevant to the prejudice analysis because it demonstrates that Johnson's acceptance of the plea—without the ability to appeal—may have been improvident.

A defendant is prejudiced when counsel's deficient performance leads him to accept a plea that results in a manifestly unjust sentence. *See Lafler v. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court abused its discretion in denying Defendant’s motion to withdraw his guilty plea. Defendant argues that his plea was not knowing and voluntary because his counsel failed to advise him that the sentencing cap would foreclose his ability to appeal his sentence. The State contends that the plea was valid and that counsel had no duty to advise regarding a right that does not exist.

A trial court’s decision to deny a motion to withdraw a guilty plea is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *People v. Davis*, 145 Ill. 2d 240, 244 (1991). However, questions of constitutional law, such as whether a defendant received effective assistance of counsel, are reviewed *de novo*. *People v. Hare*, 215 Ill. 2d 504, 516 (2006). To succeed on a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, Defendant must demonstrate both that counsel’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency resulted in prejudice. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Guilty Plea Was Knowing and Voluntary**

The record demonstrates that Defendant’s plea was entered knowingly and voluntarily. The trial court engaged in a thorough colloquy with Defendant, admonishing him regarding the nature of the charge and the direct consequences of the plea. Specifically, the court informed Defendant that the offense was a Class X felony with a mandatory minimum sentence of 21 years and a normal maximum of 45 years, but that the negotiated plea agreement capped the sentence at 40 years. Defendant affirmed that he understood this sentencing range.

A guilty plea is valid only if the defendant enters it intelligently and voluntarily, with an understanding of the nature of the charge and the consequences of the plea. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 244 (1969); *People v. Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d 326, 340 (1992). Here, the court’s admonitions satisfied these requirements. Defendant’s initial hesitation regarding his counsel’s representation was addressed by the court through a recess, after which Defendant explicitly stated he was satisfied with counsel and wished to proceed. The trial court’s finding that the plea was "as by the book as it could be" is supported by the record.

**B. Counsel Was Not Ineffective for Failing to Advise Regarding Appellate Rights**

Defendant argues that counsel was ineffective for failing to inform him that the sentencing cap would waive his right to appeal the sentence. This argument fails as a matter of law.

Under Illinois law, a defendant who pleads guilty pursuant to a plea agreement that includes a sentencing cap has no right to appeal the sentence imposed as long as it is within the cap. *People v. Hester*, 197 Ill. 2d 211, 220–21 (2001). The *Hester* court held that such plea agreements are binding contracts that foreclose appellate review of the sentence. *Id.* Because the right to appeal a sentence within a negotiated cap does not exist, counsel cannot be ineffective for failing to advise Defendant regarding the loss of a non-existent right. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 687.

Defendant relies on *People v. Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d 326 (1992), and *People v. Dye*, 215 Ill. 2d 183, 193 (2005), for the proposition that a defendant must understand the rights he is relinquishing. However, while the right to appeal is a substantial right, the specific consequence of a sentencing cap—barred appellate review—is a legal implication of the agreement itself rather than a "direct consequence" of the plea that counsel must explain to ensure the plea is voluntary. The trial court’s admonitions regarding the sentencing range were sufficient to ensure Defendant understood the terms of the agreement he was accepting.

**C. Defendant Cannot Establish Prejudice**

Even assuming counsel’s performance was deficient, Defendant cannot establish the requisite prejudice. To show prejudice, Defendant must demonstrate that there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s errors, he would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on going to trial. *Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52, 59 (1985).

Defendant testified that he would not have entered the plea had he known he could not appeal. However, this assertion is contradicted by the record and the circumstances of the plea. Defendant admitted that he understood the maximum sentence without the plea was 45 years, yet he believed he faced 90 years if he went to trial. By accepting the plea, Defendant received a sentencing cap of 40 years and the dismissal of four other serious charges, including armed robbery and aggravated unlawful restraint. Given the significant benefits of the plea agreement and Defendant's own exaggerated belief regarding the potential sentence at trial, he has not shown a reasonable probability that he would have rejected the plea and proceeded to trial. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694.

Furthermore, Defendant’s argument regarding the sentencing disparity with his codefendant does not render his plea involuntary. Sentencing disparity is a matter for the trial court’s discretion and does not constitute a manifest injustice requiring the withdrawal of a plea. *People v. Davis*, 145 Ill. 2d at 244.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly admonished Defendant regarding the consequences of his plea, and the record establishes that the plea was knowing and voluntary. Counsel was not ineffective for failing to advise Defendant that the sentencing cap would preclude appellate review, as no such right exists under *People v. Hester*, 197 Ill. 2d 211 (2001). Finally, Defendant has failed to demonstrate prejudice resulting from any alleged deficiency. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying Defendant’s motion to withdraw his guilty plea.

**I. INTRODUCTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This case comes before the Court on Defendant’s appeal from the trial court’s denial of his motion to withdraw his guilty plea. The specific issue is whether Defendant received ineffective assistance of counsel when his attorney failed to advise him that the negotiated sentencing cap would foreclose his ability to appeal his sentence. The State contends that because a defendant has no right to appeal a sentence within a negotiated cap, counsel had no duty to advise regarding the loss of a non-existent right.

A trial court’s decision to deny a motion to withdraw a guilty plea is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *People v. Davis*, 145 Ill. 2d 240, 244 (1991). However, claims of ineffective assistance of counsel present mixed questions of law and fact and are reviewed *de novo*. *People v. Hare*, 215 Ill. 2d 504, 516 (2006). To prevail on a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, Defendant must demonstrate both that counsel’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency resulted in prejudice. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Counsel Was Not Ineffective for Failing to Advise Regarding Appellate Rights**

Defendant argues that counsel was ineffective for failing to inform him that the sentencing cap would waive his right to appeal. This argument is predicated on the assertion that the loss of appellate review is a "direct consequence" of the plea that counsel must explain. The defense relies on *People v. Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d 326, 340 (1992), and *People v. Dye*, 215 Ill. 2d 183, 193 (2005), for the proposition that a defendant must understand the rights he is relinquishing.

However, this argument is controlled by the Illinois Supreme Court’s decision in *People v. Hester*, 197 Ill. 2d 211 (2001). In *Hester*, the court held that "a defendant who pleads guilty pursuant to a negotiated plea agreement that includes a sentencing cap has no right to appeal the sentence imposed as long as it is within the cap." *Hester*, 197 Ill. 2d at 220–21. The court explained that such plea agreements are binding contracts that foreclose appellate review of the sentence. *Id.*

Because the right to appeal a sentence within a negotiated cap does not exist, counsel cannot be ineffective for failing to advise Defendant regarding the loss of a non-existent right. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 687. The defense attempts to distinguish *Hester* by arguing that while the right is extinguished, the *consequence* of that extinguishment is a direct matter Defendant must understand. This distinction is unpersuasive. The "consequence" of the plea agreement is the sentence itself; the inability to appeal that sentence is a legal incident of the agreement. As the State correctly notes, counsel cannot be deficient for failing to discuss a right that is extinguished by operation of law upon the entry of the plea. The trial court’s admonitions regarding the sentencing range—21 to 40 years—were sufficient to ensure Defendant understood the terms of the agreement he was accepting.

**B. The Guilty Plea Was Knowing and Voluntary**

Defendant further argues that the plea was not knowing and voluntary because the trial court did not explicitly advise him that he was waiving his right to appeal. A guilty plea is valid only if the defendant enters it intelligently and voluntarily, with an understanding of the nature of the charge and the consequences of the plea. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 244 (1969); *People v. Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d at 340.

The record shows that the trial court conducted a thorough colloquy. The court informed Defendant that the offense was a Class X felony with a mandatory minimum sentence of 21 years and a normal maximum of 45 years, but that the negotiated plea agreement capped the sentence at 40 years. Defendant affirmed that he understood this sentencing range. The court’s admonitions satisfied the requirements of *Boykin* and *Frieberg*. The fact that the court did not explicitly state "you cannot appeal your sentence" does not render the plea involuntary, particularly where the court accurately explained the cap and Defendant affirmed his understanding.

Defendant’s initial hesitation regarding his counsel’s representation was properly addressed by the court. After a recess, Defendant clarified that he was satisfied with his counsel and was merely upset with the State’s offer. The court’s inquiry was sufficient to ensure the plea was voluntary. *People v. Curry*, 2013 IL App (1st) 110893, ¶ 27.

**C. Defendant Cannot Establish Prejudice**

Even assuming counsel’s performance was deficient, Defendant cannot establish the requisite prejudice. To show prejudice, Defendant must demonstrate that there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s errors, he would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on going to trial. *Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52, 59 (1985).

Defendant argues that had he known he could not appeal, he would have negotiated a better deal or gone to trial. This assertion is contradicted by the record. Defendant testified that he believed he faced "90 years or something" if he went to trial, whereas the plea capped his sentence at 40 years. Given this significant perceived reduction in risk, Defendant has not shown a reasonable probability that he would have rejected the plea and proceeded to trial. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694.

Defendant’s reliance on the sentencing disparity with his codefendant, who received 12 years, does not establish prejudice. Sentencing disparity is a matter for the trial court’s discretion and does not constitute a manifest injustice requiring the withdrawal of a plea. *People v. Davis*, 145 Ill. 2d at 244. The fact that the codefendant received a lesser sentence does not demonstrate that Defendant’s plea was unknowing or that he would have achieved a different result at trial.

The trial court properly admonished Defendant regarding the consequences of his plea, and the record establishes that the plea was knowing and voluntary. Counsel was not ineffective for failing to advise Defendant that the sentencing cap would preclude appellate review, as no such right exists under *People v. Hester*, 197 Ill. 2d 211 (2001). Finally, Defendant has failed to demonstrate prejudice resulting from any alleged deficiency. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying Defendant’s motion to withdraw his guilty plea.

The issue is whether Defendant entered his plea with the full understanding necessary to render it knowing and voluntary under *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), and *People v. Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d 326 (1992).

A guilty plea is not merely a procedural formality; it is a waiver of fundamental constitutional rights. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that waiver of these rights is valid only if the defendant understands the nature of the rights being relinquished and the consequences of the plea. *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 244. When counsel fails to advise a defendant that a negotiated plea agreement will extinguish appellate review, the defendant cannot make an intelligent decision about whether to accept that agreement.

The prosecution's reliance on *Hester* is misplaced. In *Hester*, the Illinois Supreme Court held that a defendant who pleads guilty pursuant to a negotiated cap has no *appellate* right to challenge the sentence. *People v. A defendant may have no right to appeal after entering the plea, but the defendant must understand that consequence *before* entering the plea for the waiver to be valid.

The defense submits that *People v. Curry*, 2013 IL App (1st) 110893, provides more instructive guidance. The prosecution argues that the trial court's admonitions about the sentencing range (21 to 40 years) were sufficient. But this conflates two distinct concepts: understanding the *sentence* and understanding the *consequences of the plea agreement*. A defendant may understand that he will receive between 21 and 40 years yet still not understand that he is simultaneously waiving his right to challenge that sentence on appeal. The former tells him what his punishment will be; the latter tells him what his options will be after punishment is imposed. Both are essential to an intelligent waiver.

If the right to appeal is extinguished by operation of law upon entry of the plea, then the defendant must be informed of this legal consequence before the plea can be deemed knowing. Otherwise, the defendant is not waiving a right—he is being deprived of it without notice.

The prosecution cites *Boykin* for the proposition that the Constitution requires only that the defendant understand the nature of the charge and the consequences of the plea. But the "consequences of the plea" are not limited to the sentencing range. Here, Defendant was never informed that his plea would eliminate his ability to appeal his sentence.

The prosecution points to Defendant's affirmative responses during the colloquy as proof of his understanding. But these responses establish only that Defendant understood the numerical range of his sentence—not that he understood the full legal implications of his plea. When the court asked, "Do you understand that, sir?" in reference to the 21-to-40 year range, Defendant answered yes. *¶ 6*. This does not establish that Defendant understood he was waiving his right to appeal.

The prosecution also argues that Defendant's initial hesitation about counsel's representation was properly addressed by the court's recess. But the court's inquiry addressed only whether Defendant was satisfied with counsel generally. The court never asked whether Defendant understood the specific terms of the plea agreement, including its effect on appellate rights. This distinction is fatal to the prosecution's position.

## III. DEFENDANT ESTABLISHED PREJUDICE

The prosecution argues that Defendant cannot establish prejudice because he received a favorable deal—40 years instead of a potential 45-year maximum. This argument ignores the record evidence regarding Defendant's state of mind.

At the motion hearing, Defendant testified that plea counsel told him he would receive "90 years or something" if he went to trial. *¶ 17*. The prosecution cites this testimony as proof that Defendant understood the plea was favorable. But this same testimony demonstrates exactly why Defendant was prejudiced: he entered the plea based on a fundamental misunderstanding of his exposure.

If Defendant had known that the sentencing cap would eliminate his ability to appeal, he would have had a complete picture of his options. He would have understood that by accepting the cap, he was not merely limiting his maximum exposure—he was also eliminating his ability to challenge the sentence if the trial court imposed an excessive term within that cap. This complete information might have led Defendant to negotiate for different terms or to proceed to trial.

The prosecution argues that Defendant cannot show prejudice because the plea was objectively favorable. But the prejudice inquiry focuses on the defendant's subjective decision-making process, not objective fairness. *Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52, 59 (1985). The question is whether there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's errors, the defendant would not have pleaded guilty. Here, Defendant has testified that he would have sought a better deal or gone to trial had he known about the appellate waiver.

The prosecution argues that this testimony is self-serving and contradicted by the record. But the prosecution's characterization misreads the record. Defendant testified that he did not know he could not appeal his sentence. This testimony is consistent with his claim that counsel never advised him of this consequence. The prosecution cannot rely on the absence of advice to prove that Defendant understood the consequences of his plea.

The prosecution also argues that sentencing disparity with the codefendant does not establish prejudice. But this argument misconstrues the nature of the prejudice claim. Defendant is not arguing that the disparity itself constitutes prejudice. He is arguing that if he had known he could not appeal, he might have negotiated for a lower sentence or proceeded to trial. The codefendant's 12-year sentence is relevant not as direct evidence of prejudice, but as context for understanding what Defendant might have achieved had he known the full consequences of his plea.

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION

The prosecution urges this Court to affirm the trial court's denial of Defendant's motion to withdraw his plea, arguing that the plea was "as by the book as it could be." But the trial court's characterization does not establish that the plea was constitutionally valid.

The trial court found that counsel had no duty to advise Defendant about the appellate consequences of the sentencing cap. This finding was error as a matter of law. While *Hester* holds that no appellate right exists for sentences within a cap, *Hester* does not hold that counsel may remain silent about this consequence without rendering the plea unknowing.

The trial court also found that Defendant's plea was knowing and voluntary based on his affirmative responses during the colloquy. But as discussed above, affirmative responses to questions about the sentencing range do not establish understanding of the full legal consequences of the plea. The trial court's finding was therefore clearly erroneous.

The prosecution argues that the defense's position would "incentivize defendants to plead guilty to cap sentences, then seek withdrawal solely to challenge disparities on appeal." This argument is a policy concern, not a legal analysis. The question before this Court is whether Defendant's plea was knowing and voluntary—not whether the legal rule might have unintended consequences. If the rule must be clarified, it should be clarified in favor of protecting defendants' constitutional rights.

## V. CONCLUSION

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and remand with instructions to allow Defendant to withdraw his guilty plea. The record establishes that counsel failed to advise Defendant of a critical consequence of his plea: the loss of appellate review. This failure rendered the plea unknowing and involuntary. Defendant has established both deficient performance and prejudice. The trial court abused its discretion in denying his motion.

The prosecution's argument rests on the premise that counsel had no duty to advise about a "non-existent" right. But this premise is constitutionally flawed. The right to appeal does not become non-existent until the plea is entered; until then, it is a right that exists and must be understood for the plea to be valid. Counsel's failure to ensure this understanding violated the Sixth Amendment.

Your Honor, Defendant was 21 years old when he entered his plea. He had minimal criminal history. He expressed uncertainty about his counsel's representation. He was told he faced "90 years" at trial—a figure that dramatically overstated his actual exposure. Under these circumstances, the failure to advise about the appellate waiver was not merely negligent; it was constitutionally deficient. The plea cannot stand.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND STANDARD OF REVIEW**

This matter is before the Court on Defendant’s appeal from the circuit court’s denial of his motion to withdraw his guilty plea. Defendant contends that his plea counsel provided ineffective assistance by failing to advise him that the negotiated sentencing cap would foreclose his right to appeal his sentence. He further argues that his plea was not knowing and voluntary because he did not understand this consequence. The State responds that because a defendant has no right to appeal a sentence within a negotiated cap, counsel had no duty to advise regarding the loss of a non-existent right, and that Defendant cannot establish prejudice.

A trial court’s decision to deny a motion to withdraw a guilty plea is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. *People v. Davis*, 145 Ill. 2d 240, 244 (1991). However, a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel presents a mixed question of law and fact, which is reviewed *de novo*. *People v. Hare*, 215 Ill. 2d 504, 516 (2006). To prevail on a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, Defendant must demonstrate both that counsel’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency resulted in prejudice. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984).

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Counsel Was Not Ineffective for Failing to Advise Regarding Appellate Rights**

Defendant argues that counsel was ineffective for failing to inform him that the sentencing cap would waive his right to appeal. He asserts that the loss of appellate review is a "direct consequence" of the plea that counsel must explain to ensure the plea is knowing and voluntary under *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 244 (1969), and *People v. Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d 326, 340 (1992).

This argument is foreclosed by the Illinois Supreme Court’s decision in *People v. Hester*, 197 Ill. 2d 211 (2001). In *Hester*, the court held that "a defendant who pleads guilty pursuant to a negotiated plea agreement that includes a sentencing cap has no right to appeal the sentence imposed as long as it is within the cap." *Hester*, 197 Ill. 2d at 220–21. The court explained that such plea agreements are binding contracts that, by their very nature, eliminate the right to appellate review of the sentence. *Id.*

The defense attempts to distinguish *Hester* by arguing that while the right is extinguished *after* the plea, the *consequence* of that extinguishment is a direct matter Defendant must understand *before* pleading. This distinction is unpersuasive. The "consequence" of the plea agreement is the sentence itself; the inability to appeal that sentence is a legal incident of the agreement that does not exist independently of the plea. As the State correctly notes, counsel cannot be deficient for failing to discuss a right that is extinguished by operation of law upon the entry of the plea. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 687. The trial court’s admonitions regarding the sentencing range—21 to 40 years—were sufficient to ensure Defendant understood the direct consequences of the plea. *People v. Frieberg*, 147 Ill. 2d at 340.

**B. The Guilty Plea Was Knowing and Voluntary**

Defendant further argues that the plea was not knowing and voluntary because the trial court did not explicitly advise him that he was waiving his right to appeal. A guilty plea is valid only if the defendant enters it intelligently and voluntarily, with an understanding of the nature of the charge and the consequences of the plea. *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 244.

The record demonstrates that the trial court conducted a thorough colloquy. The court informed Defendant that the offense was a Class X felony with a mandatory minimum sentence of 21 years and a normal maximum of 45 years, but that the negotiated plea agreement capped the sentence at 40 years. Defendant affirmed that he understood this sentencing range. The court’s admonitions satisfied the requirements of *Boykin* and *Frieberg*. The fact that the court did not explicitly state "you cannot appeal your sentence" does not render the plea involuntary, particularly where the court accurately explained the cap and Defendant affirmed his understanding.

Defendant’s initial hesitation regarding his counsel’s representation was properly addressed by the court. After a recess, Defendant clarified that he was satisfied with his counsel and was merely upset with the State’s offer. The court’s inquiry was sufficient to ensure the plea was voluntary. *People v. Curry*, 2013 IL App (1st) 110893, ¶ 27.

**C. Defendant Cannot Establish Prejudice**

Even assuming counsel’s performance was deficient, Defendant cannot establish the requisite prejudice. To show prejudice, Defendant must demonstrate that there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s errors, he would not have pleaded guilty and would have insisted on going to trial. *Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52, 59 (1985).

Defendant argues that had he known he could not appeal, he would have negotiated a better deal or gone to trial. This assertion is contradicted by the record. Defendant testified at the hearing on the motion to withdraw that plea counsel told him he faced "90 years or something" if he went to trial, whereas the plea capped his sentence at 40 years. *¶ 17*. Although Defendant also testified that he understood the statutory maximum was 45 years, his belief that he faced a potential sentence of 90 years underscores the significant risk he perceived in going to trial. Given this perceived reduction in exposure—from 90 years to a 40-year cap—Defendant has not shown a reasonable probability that he would have rejected the plea and proceeded to trial. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694.

Defendant’s reliance on the sentencing disparity with his codefendant, who received 12 years, does not establish prejudice. Sentencing disparity is a matter for the trial court’s discretion and does not constitute a manifest injustice requiring the withdrawal of a plea. *People v. Davis*, 145 Ill. 2d at 244. The fact that the codefendant received a lesser sentence does not demonstrate that Defendant’s plea was unknowing or that he would have achieved a different result at trial.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly admonished Defendant regarding the consequences of his plea, and the record establishes that the plea was knowing and voluntary. Counsel was not ineffective for failing to advise Defendant that the sentencing cap would preclude appellate review, as no such right exists under *People v. Hester*, 197 Ill. 2d 211 (2001). Finally, Defendant has failed to demonstrate prejudice resulting from any alleged deficiency. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying Defendant’s motion to withdraw his guilty plea.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the judgment of the circuit court is affirmed.
