COURT ARENA LOG - Case 87
FACTS:
The Township of Wilmington (Township) appeals from the April 13, 2018 order of the Court of Common Pleas of Lawrence County (trial court) granting the land use appeal of Wimer Realty, LLC, Wimer Holdings Corporation, and Richard G. Wimer Jr. and Jody Wimer, husband and wife (collectively, the Wimers), and denying the Township's motions to strike and dismiss.

Facts and Procedural History

The Wimers are engaged in the business of catering and operating special events and, more recently, have managed a barn wedding venue. On June 22, 2017, the Wimers purchased real property located at 3538 State Route 956 in Wilmington Township, Lawrence County, Pennsylvania (the Property), consisting of three parcels. The parcel at issue consists of 31.42 acres, approximately 25 of which are used for farming, and has a barn situated upon it. The Wimers intended to invest approximately $ 2,200,000.00 into the Property in order to renovate and improve the barn so that they can host events in it, including weddings and receptions. (Trial court op. at 2.)

Wilmington Township's Zoning Ordinance was originally silent as to the use of a barn as a wedding venue in the Township. In September 2016, the Township's Planning Commission drafted a proposed amendment to the Zoning Ordinance to address wedding barns, which was considered by the Township's Board of Supervisors (Board) at a November 7, 2016 meeting. Shortly thereafter, the first wedding barn venue, "Five Fillies," was established in the Township and began operations pursuant to a special exception granted by the Zoning Hearing Board. Id. at 2, 14.

In January 2017, the Township sent three unnumbered draft ordinances, which became known as "Ordinance No. 1 of 2017," addressing wedding barn venues located in the Township, to the Lawrence County Planning Commission, as well as the Township Planning Commission, for review and comment. A public hearing on Ordinance No. 1 of 2017 was advertised on February 14, 2017, and February 21, 2017, and the hearing was held on March 2, 2017. At the conclusion of the hearing, Ordinance No. 1 of 2017 was voted down by the Board. Id. at 2-3.

On June 27, 2017, the Township forwarded draft Ordinance No. 2 of 2017, which pertained to wedding barns by conditional use in agricultural districts, and Ordinance No. 3 of 2017, which pertained to the same in residential zoning districts, to the Lawrence County and Township Planning Commissions for review and comment. Neither of the draft ordinances were advertised for adoption as of June 27, 2017. Id. at 3.

The following month, on July 20, 2017, the Wimers filed a request for hearing on their challenge to the Township's Zoning Ordinance, as amended, as well as a proposed curative amendment, pursuant to section 916.1(a)(2) of the Pennsylvania Municipalities Planning Code (MPC), 53 P.S. § 10916.1(a)(2).1 On September 7, 2017, a *631hearing was held before the Board, at which the Wimers presented the testimony of Stephen Victor, a landscape architect and expert in zoning and land use planning, who assisted the Wimers in preparation of the plans and explanatory materials they submitted to the Township. Mr. Victor stated that Ordinance No. 105 of 1997, which was in effect at the time of the hearing, would not permit the use of a special event venue proposed by the Wimers but noted that the curative amendment submitted by the Wimers would cure the deficiency in the Zoning Ordinance. (Trial court op. at 6; Reproduced Record (R.R.) at 84a, 93a-125a.)

The Township presented the testimony of John Trant, an attorney and land use planning consultant certified by the American Institute of Certified Planners. Mr. Trant impliedly agreed that the use proposed by the Wimers was not provided for in the Zoning Ordinance, but opined that the Wimers' proposed curative amendment was deficient in that it was, among other things, too specific so as to be exclusionary and difficult to enforce throughout the Township. However, Mr. Trant did not testify that the proposed curative amendment would pose a threat to the health, safety, or wellbeing of the Township's residents. Several Township residents also testified and presented their opinions on the proposed curative amendment. (Trial court op. at 6-7; R.R. at 147a-53a.)

At the conclusion of the hearing, the Board voted to deny the Wimers' challenge to the Zoning Ordinance and not to accept the proposed curative amendment. The Board additionally voted to refer draft Ordinance No. 3 of 2017, incorporating the comments of Mr. Trant, to the Township and Lawrence County Planning Commissions for review and comment. (Trial court op. at 7.)

On September 25, 2017, the Wimers filed a land use appeal in the trial court, seeking review of the Board's decision. The Wimers subsequently filed a "Renewed, Supplemental and Restated Land Use Appeal Notice" (Renewed Land Use Appeal), which "recit[ed] the history of the case subsequent to [the date of the Board's decision] and set[ ] forth essentially a restatement of the original Land Use Appeal Notice." Id. at 7. The Township responded by filing four motions: (1) a motion to open the record and present additional evidence; (2) a motion to strike the Wimers' Renewed Land Use Appeal; (3) a motion to dismiss the land use appeal as moot; and (4) a motion to dismiss the land use appeal based upon the pending ordinance doctrine. Id. at 7-8.

The Township's motion to dismiss the land use appeal was premised upon the argument that any alleged deficiencies in the current Zoning Ordinance would be moot after the Township adopted Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, or because the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary. Ordinance No. 3 of 2017, following revisions, became titled Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, which permitted a "Special Event Barn" as an accessory use in an A-Agricultural Zoning District and as a principal or accessory use in a C-Commercial Zoning District. Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 was adopted by the Board on November 9, 2017, following an advertised public hearing. Id. at 8.

The court granted, in part, the Township's motion to open the record, permitting *632it to enter into the record a certified copy of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, on the basis that it was not available for introduction at the hearing before the Board and because it might have been relevant to the Township's argument that it cured any alleged defect in the Zoning Ordinance. However, the court denied the motion in all other respects, reasoning that "the Township had the opportunity to be fully heard on the underlying matter at the hearing on September 7, 2017, the Township's expert, John Trant, testified on September 7, 2017[,] and presented his expert opinion[,] and there was no mention in the record that the Township intended to present additional testimony or that certain evidence proffered by the Township was excluded." Id. at 8.

After hearing legal argument, the trial court issued an opinion and order finding in favor of the Wimers. Initially, the trial court ruled that the Wimers had perfected their land use appeal. The court noted that, while the Board voted to deny the Wimers' challenge to the Zoning Ordinance, it did so orally and it did not issue specific findings of fact or conclusions of law. Additionally, the Board voted to amend Ordinance No. 3 of 2017 to include the recommendations of Mr. Trant and to send it for review and comment. Thus, the trial court found that the Board's decision after the hearing was

not entirely dispositive towards the Wimers' concerns in that the Board denied the Wimers' challenge and proposed curative amendment, and did not 'approve' a draft ordinance since revisions needed to be made to Ordinance No. 3 of 2017 and it needed to be sent to [the] Township Planning Commission and [the] Lawrence County Planning Commission for recommendation and comment.
Id. at 10.

The trial court next addressed the Township's motion to strike, which asserted that the Wimers' Renewed Land Use Appeal should have been stricken because it attempted to raise an issue that the Wimers waived by failing to raise it in their original land use appeal. Id. Specifically, the Township asserted that the Wimers were raising an issue as to whether the Board actually made a decision or whether their land use appeal was deemed denied. The Township contended that the Board did indeed deny the Wimers' land use appeal/curative amendment at the end of the hearing and, thus, the decision was not a deemed denial.2 (Original Record (O.R.) at Item No. 8.)

The court disagreed that the Renewed Land Use Appeal should have been stricken from the record. It noted that section 1002-A(a) of the MPC, added by Act of December 21, 1988, P.L. 1329, requires an appeal from a land use decision to be filed 30 days after the "entry of the decision," 53 P.S. § 11002-A(a), and that section 916.1(c)(7) of the MPC provides that, if the governing body fails to act on the landowner's request within 45 days after the last hearing, "a denial of the request is deemed to have occurred on the 46th day after the close of the last hearing." 53 P.S. § 10916.1(c)(7). Thus, the court explained that the Wimers filed the original land use *633appeal within 30 days of the September 7, 2017 hearing but, out of an abundance of caution, also filed the Renewed Land Use Appeal 30 days after the hearing in the event the Board's decision was a deemed denial. The court observed that the Renewed Land Use Appeal "substantially contain[ed] the same factual matters and arguments as the [original] [l]and [u]se [a]ppeal, and only recited matters subsequent to the hearing of September 7, 2017." (Trial court op. at 12.) Additionally, the court emphasized that, during argument before it, counsel for the Township conceded that the Wimers' land use appeal was timely filed. In denying the motion to strike the court stated,

[A]ny defect or error made by the Wimers in filing [ ] does not affect substantial rights of the parties, but rather only confirms the ability of the Wimers to indeed take an appeal to this [c]ourt of the Board's actions given the manner by which the hearing before the Board was concluded.
Id.3

Next, the court addressed whether the Wimers' appeal was moot given the Township's adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017. In concluding that the appeal was not moot, the court observed that there were certain "substantial [i]nconsistencies" between Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 and the Wimers' proposed curative amendment. Specifically, Ordinance No. 5 limited the number of events to 12 per year and gave the Board authority to reduce below 300 the maximum number of guests, whereas the curative amendment did not include these restrictions. On this point, the court cited Mr. Wimer's testimony that, in order for the wedding barn to be financially feasible, the Wimers would need to be able to hold more than 12 events per year with up to 300 guests per event. Thus, the court concluded that because Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 substantially impacted the Wimers' ability to conduct their proposed use in a manner which was financially and economically reasonable under the circumstances, the Wimers' land use appeal was not moot by virtue of the passage of Ordinance No 5. 2017. Id. at 13.

The court then addressed whether the Wimers' land use appeal was moot under the pending ordinance doctrine or because the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary. The court again determined it was not. It observed that, in order for the pending ordinance doctrine to apply, the municipality must publically declare its intent to consider a particular zoning scheme by advertising to the public its intention to hold a public hearing on such rezoning. See Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township , 459 Pa. 219, 328 A.2d 464 (1974). In this case, the trial court noted that the Wimers filed their request to challenge the Zoning Ordinance and for adoption of a curative amendment on July 20, 2017, at which point the Board had already declined to adopt Ordinance No. 1 of 2017 but had not advertised its intent to adopt Ordinance No. 3 of 2017. Further, the court emphasized that the Board "gave no *634approval to advertise the notice of intent to adopt Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 until after the Board had denied the Wimers' challenge and proposed curative amendment on September 7, 2017." (Trial court op. at 15.) Accordingly, the court found that the pending ordinance doctrine did not apply.

The court next addressed the Township's argument that the Wimers' zoning challenge was properly denied because the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary in that another wedding barn, the Five Fillies, had been previously authorized by the Zoning Hearing Board and that, instead of filing a land use appeal, the Wimers should have likewise sought a special exception from the Zoning Hearing Board. The court noted that the Zoning Hearing Board's decision permitting the Five Fillies wedding barn appeared to be an "aberration," as there was, at that time, no provision in the Zoning Ordinance allowing use as a special event barn. Consequently, the court found that the Wimers were not required to submit to a hearing before the Zoning Hearing Board to seek a special exception for a wedding barn use when such a use was not permitted and, thus, the Wimers were not barred from pursuing their land use appeal merely because the Five Fillies farm had obtained special permission to operate a wedding barn. Accordingly, the court denied the Township's motion to dismiss the land use appeal on the basis of the pending ordinance doctrine and/or because the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary. Id. at 16.

The trial court then considered whether the Township established that the exclusion of the proposed zoning use was related to the protection of the public health, safety, or welfare of the residents of the Township, or whether the proposed use was injurious to the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare of its residents. The trial court again answered this in the negative, reasoning that although Mr. Trant offered various technical objections to the content of the curative amendment, he did not opine that it would be in any way injurious to the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare of the Township's residents. Thus, the court determined that the Township did not present sufficient evidence to hold that the Wimers' proposed use of the land would pose a threat of injury to the health, safety, morals, or general welfare of the public. Id. at 16-17.

Finally, the trial court addressed whether the Wimers' challenge to the Zoning Ordinance and proposed curative amendment should have been granted by the Board, answering it in the affirmative. The court analyzed Casey , 328 A.2d at 464, wherein our Supreme Court held that a successful challenger of a zoning ordinance must be granted the relief requested unless the governmental unit proves that the proposed use will be injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare. The trial court also considered our precedent in In re Miller & Son Paving, Inc. , 161 Pa.Cmwlth. 138, 636 A.2d 274 (1993), wherein we held that, in the case of a de jure exclusion of a use, the sole remedy for a successful challenger is to allow the use somewhere, and the successful challenger must receive that benefit in the form of at least partial approval of his proposal. In re Miller & Son , 636 A.2d at 277.

With regard to the present case, the trial court made several conclusions. First, the court stated that "a use as a wedding barn in Wilmington Township, where the number of wedding-type events is unlimited and the number of guests at the facility is limited to 300 is entirely reasonable." (Trial court op. at 18.) Next, the trial court held that the Wimers "indeed satisfied their burden of proving that Wilmington *635Township Ordinance No. 105 of 1997, as amended, as it existed on September 7, 2017, failed to adequately provide for use as a special event venue." Id. Finally, the trial court found that the Board did not "sufficiently justify" its decision to decline the Wimers' challenge to the Zoning Ordinance and proposed curative amendment because the Township "failed to demonstrate that the Wimers' use of their property as a special event venue would have an [e]ffect on the public health, safety and welfare in a meaningful way." Id. Thus, the court concluded that the Wimers' request for a challenge to the Zoning Ordinance and proposed curative amendment should have been granted by the Board.

Accordingly, the court determined that the Board's decision was an abuse of discretion, granted the Wimers' land use appeal, and permitted them to use the Property in accordance with the terms of their proposed curative amendment, but required them to comply with all other applicable Township ordinances not addressed with the curative amendment.4

The Township filed the present appeal,5 arguing (1) the Wimers' land use appeal was moot based upon Ordinance No. 5 of 2017; (2) the Wimers' land use appeal should have been dismissed under the pending ordinance doctrine and/or because the Township's Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary; (3) the Board's decision was supported by substantial evidence; and (4) the trial court abused its discretion in refusing to permit the Township to supplement the record.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE COMMONWEALTH COURT OF PENNSYLVANIA

---

**TOWNSHIP OF WILMINGTON, Appellant**

**v.**

**WIMER REALTY, LLC, WIMER HOLDINGS CORPORATION, RICHARD G. WIMER JR., and JODY WIMER, Appellees**

---

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF ISSUES

May it please this Honorable Court:

The Township of Wilmington respectfully submits this Opening Response in defense of the April 13, 2018 order of the Court of Common Pleas of Lawrence County, which the Township appeals. The Township asserts that the trial court erred in granting the Wimers' land use appeal and in denying the Township's motions to strike and dismiss. The Township's position is supported by the following arguments: (1) the Wimers' land use appeal is moot due to the Township's adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017; (2) the Wimers' land use appeal should have been dismissed under the pending ordinance doctrine and because the Township's Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary; (3) the Board's decision was supported by substantial evidence; and (4) the trial court abused its discretion in refusing to permit the Township to supplement the record.

---

## I. THE APPEAL IS NOT MOOT: ORDINANCE NO. 5 OF 2017 DOES NOT RENDER THE CONTROVERSY MOOT

The Township respectfully asserts that the trial court erred in concluding that the Wimers' land use appeal was not moot. A case is moot when "the court can no longer grant effective relief." In re Miller & Son Paving, Inc., 161 Pa.Cmwlth. 138, 636 A.2d 274, 277 (1993). The Township contends that with the adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, which specifically permits special event barns as an accessory use in agricultural districts, the Township has cured any alleged exclusionary deficiency in its Zoning Ordinance, thereby rendering the Wimers' challenge moot.

The trial court found that the appeal was not moot because Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 "substantially impacted the Wimers' ability to conduct their proposed use in a manner which was financially and economically reasonable under the circumstances." However, this Court should reject this reasoning. The proper test for mootness is not whether an ordinance makes a proposed use economically inconvenient for a particular landowner, but whether the ordinance permits the use at all. Here, Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 expressly permits special event barns as an accessory use in A-Agricultural Zoning Districts. This represents a direct answer to the Wimers' complaint that their proposed use was not permitted under the Zoning Ordinance.

The Township's Zoning Ordinance, as amended by Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, now provides for the very use the Wimers proposed—a wedding barn or special event venue in an agricultural district. The fact that the ordinance includes certain restrictions, such as a cap of twelve events per year and a maximum of three hundred guests, does not render the appeal moot. Zoning ordinances regularly include reasonable regulations regarding the intensity and scope of permitted uses. Such restrictions are within the police power of the municipality and do not constitute a de facto exclusion of the use. See Dewar v. City of Pittsburgh, 2 Pa.Cmwlth. 312, 278 A.2d 687 (1971) (holding that reasonable regulations regarding the manner of operating a permitted use do not constitute exclusion).

Moreover, the Wimers remain free to apply for permits under Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 and to seek variances or special exceptions if they believe the restrictions are overly burdensome. The curative amendment process is not the exclusive means by which a landowner may challenge a zoning ordinance. The Wimers could have pursued administrative remedies before the Zoning Hearing Board, but they chose not to do so. Their election to pursue a curative amendment does not insulate them from the effects of subsequent legislative action by the Township.

The trial court improperly focused on the economic impact of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 on the Wimers' specific business model rather than on the legal question of whether the use is now permitted. This Court should hold that the appeal is moot because the Township has cured the alleged defect through legislative action.

---

## II. THE PENDING ORDINANCE DOCTRINE BARS THE WIMERS' CHALLENGE, AND THE ZONING ORDINANCE IS NOT EXCLUSIONARY

### A. The Pending Ordinance Doctrine Applies

The Township further asserts that the trial court erred in finding that the pending ordinance doctrine does not apply to bar the Wimers' challenge. Under the pending ordinance doctrine, as articulated by the Pennsylvania Supreme Court in Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township, 459 Pa. 219, 328 A.2d 464 (1974), a land use challenge is premature where the municipality has publicly declared its intent to consider a rezoning that would address the landowner's complaint before the landowner files the challenge.

The trial court found that the pending ordinance doctrine does not apply because the Township "gave no approval to advertise the notice of intent to adopt Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 until after the Board had denied the Wimers' challenge and proposed curative amendment on September 7, 2017." However, this finding ignores the broader context of the Township's actions. The Township had been actively considering regulations governing wedding barns since 2016. The Planning Commission drafted a proposed amendment in September 2016. In January 2017, the Township sent three draft ordinances, including what became Ordinance No. 1 of 2017, to the Planning Commissions for review. Although Ordinance No. 1 of 2017 was voted down in March 2017, the Township continued to work on alternative drafts, including Ordinance Nos. 2 and 3 of 2017, which were forwarded to the Planning Commissions in June 2017.

The Wimers filed their challenge on July 20, 2017. At that time, the Township had clearly manifested its intent to address wedding barns through the legislative process. The fact that a specific ordinance had not yet been advertised for adoption does not defeat the application of the pending ordinance doctrine. The doctrine is designed to prevent landowners from circumventing the legislative process by obtaining judicial relief that would preempt legitimate legislative action. Here, the Township was in the midst of that legislative process when the Wimers filed their challenge.

This Court should hold that the pending ordinance doctrine applies and that the Wimers' challenge was premature.

### B. The Zoning Ordinance Is Not Exclusionary

Additionally, the Township asserts that the trial erred in finding that the Zoning Ordinance was exclusionary. The trial court characterized the Zoning Hearing Board's decision to grant a special exception to Five Fillies as an "aberration" and concluded that the Wimers were not required to seek a special exception because the use was not expressly permitted.

This reasoning is flawed. The fact that the Zoning Hearing Board granted a special exception for the Five Fillies wedding barn demonstrates that the Zoning Ordinance is not exclusionary. Under Pennsylvania law, a zoning ordinance is exclusionary only if it "totally excludes" a legitimate use from the municipality. See Hunt v. Township of Upper St. Clair, 505 Pa. 249, 479 A.2d 969 (1984). The fact that a use requires a special exception or conditional use approval does not render the ordinance exclusionary. The Wimers could have sought a special exception from the Zoning Hearing Board, just as Five Fillies did. Their decision not to pursue this administrative remedy should not be rewarded on appeal.

The trial court erred in creating a new category of "aberrational" zoning decisions that are somehow less valid than other special exception grants. The Zoning Hearing Board has expertise in land use matters and is the proper forum for determining whether a special exception should be granted. The Wimers bypassed this forum at their own peril.

---

## III. THE BOARD'S DECISION WAS SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE

The Township further contends that the trial court erred in substituting its judgment for that of the Board. The standard of review for a land use appeal is whether the Board abused its discretion or committed an error of law. Substantial evidence supports a finding if it "could reasonably be concluded that the evidence presented was such that a reasonable mind might accept it as adequate to support the conclusion reached." M & M Restaurants, Inc. v. City of Reading, 96 Pa.Cmwlth. 452, 508 A.2d 1016, 1019 (1986).

The Board heard testimony from John Trant, an attorney and land use planning consultant certified by the American Institute of Certified Planners. Mr. Trant testified that the Wimers' proposed curative amendment was deficient because it was "too specific so as to be exclusionary and difficult to enforce throughout the Township." While Mr. Trant did not testify that the proposed amendment would pose a threat to the health, safety, or wellbeing of the Township's residents, he did identify specific concerns about the amendment's enforceability and specificity.

The trial court improperly discounted Mr. Trant's testimony because he did not specifically opine that the proposed use would be injurious to the public health, safety, morals, or general welfare. However, the Board's authority to deny a curative amendment is not limited to situations where the proposed use would cause such injury. The Board has discretion to deny a curative amendment based on concerns about the amendment's drafting, enforceability, and consistency with the Township's comprehensive plan. Mr. Trant's testimony provided a rational basis for the Board's decision.

Moreover, the Board received testimony from several Township residents who expressed opinions against the proposed curative amendment. This testimony, while not controlling, provides additional support for the Board's decision.

The trial court erred in requiring the Township to prove that the proposed use would be injurious to public health, safety, or welfare as a condition of denying the curative amendment. While this standard applies to successful challenges under Casey, the burden shifts to the municipality only after the landowner has proven that the ordinance excludes a reasonable use. The Wimers did not meet this initial burden because, as discussed above, the Zoning Ordinance is not exclusionary.

---

## IV. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN REFUSING TO PERMIT THE TOWNSHIP TO SUPPLEMENT THE RECORD

Finally, the Township asserts that the trial court abused its discretion in refusing to permit the Township to supplement the record with additional evidence. The trial court granted, in part, the Township's motion to open the record by permitting the introduction of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, which was adopted after the Board's hearing. However, the trial court denied the motion in all other respects.

The Township sought to introduce additional evidence to demonstrate that the Zoning Ordinance, as amended by Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, addresses the Wimers' concerns and renders their challenge moot. This evidence was not available at the time of the Board's hearing because Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 had not yet been adopted. The trial court acknowledged that the ordinance "might have been relevant to the Township's argument that it cured any alleged defect in the Zoning Ordinance."

**CASE:** *Township of Wilmington v. Wimer Realty, LLC*

**OPINION**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the Order of the Court of Common Pleas of Lawrence County granting the land use appeal of Wimer Realty, LLC, Wimer Holdings Corporation, and Richard G. Wimer Jr. and Jody Wimer (collectively, the Wimers). The Township of Wilmington (Township) appeals, arguing that the trial court erred in denying its motions to strike and dismiss, and in granting the Wimers' challenge to the Zoning Ordinance. For the reasons that follow, the Township's arguments are unpersuasive, and the trial court's order is affirmed.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Appeal is Not Moot and the Pending Ordinance Doctrine Does Not Apply**

The Township first argues that the Wimers' land use appeal is moot due to the adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, which permits "Special Event Barns" as an accessory use in agricultural districts. The Township further contends that the pending ordinance doctrine bars the Wimers' challenge because the Township was actively considering regulations for wedding barns prior to the challenge.

We find no merit in these contentions. Regarding mootness, the trial court correctly determined that Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 did not render the controversy moot. A case is moot when the court can no longer grant effective relief. *In re Miller & Son Paving, Inc.*, 161 Pa.Cmwlth. 138, 636 A.2d 274, 277 (1993). Here, the trial court found that Ordinance No. 5 imposed restrictions—specifically a limit of 12 events per year and a guest limit of 300—that "substantially impacted the Wimers' ability to conduct their proposed use in a manner which was financially and economically reasonable." Mr. Wimer testified that the project required more than 12 events per year to be financially feasible. Because the ordinance effectively prohibits the economically viable use the Wimers sought to establish, the controversy remains live. The Township cannot claim to have "cured" a constitutional defect by replacing a total exclusion with a partial exclusion that renders the use impracticable.

Furthermore, the pending ordinance doctrine is inapplicable. Under *Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township*, 459 Pa. 219, 328 A.2d 464 (1974), a land use challenge is premature only if the municipality has publicly declared its intent to consider a specific rezoning before the landowner files the challenge. The record shows that the Wimers filed their challenge on July 20, 2017. At that time, the Township had voted down Ordinance No. 1 of 2017, and Ordinances No. 2 and 3 had not been advertised for adoption. The Township did not approve the advertisement of Ordinance No. 5 until after the Board denied the Wimers' challenge on September 7, 2017. Mere internal deliberation or drafting of ordinances, without a public declaration of intent to adopt, is insufficient to trigger the pending ordinance doctrine. *Casey*, 328 A.2d at 464. Therefore, the Wimers' challenge was not premature.

**B. The Zoning Ordinance Was Exclusionary and the Township Failed to Meet Its Burden**

The Township next argues that the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary because another wedding barn, "Five Fillies," had been authorized by a special exception. The Township also asserts that the Board's decision was supported by substantial evidence.

We agree with the trial court that the Zoning Ordinance was exclusionary. A zoning ordinance is exclusionary if it totally excludes a legitimate use from the municipality. *Hunt v. Township of Upper St. Clair*, 505 Pa. 249, 479 A.2d 969 (1984). The fact that the Zoning Hearing Board granted a special exception to "Five Fillies" does not cure the exclusionary nature of the ordinance for the general public, particularly where the ordinance was silent on the use. The trial court characterized that decision as an "aberration," and the Wimers were not required to submit to a special exception hearing for a use that was not provided for in the ordinance.

Crucially, once a landowner successfully challenges an ordinance as exclusionary, the burden shifts to the municipality to prove that the proposed use is injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare. *Casey*, 328 A.2d at 464. The Township's expert, Mr. Trant, testified that the Wimers' proposed curative amendment was "too specific" and "difficult to enforce," but he explicitly did not testify that the proposed use would pose a threat to the health, safety, or welfare of the residents. Technical objections to the drafting of a curative amendment do not satisfy the municipality's burden under *Casey* to show injury to the public interest. Consequently, the trial court correctly found that the Township failed to justify its denial of the curative amendment.

**C. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Refusing to Supplement the Record**

Finally, the Township argues that the trial court abused its discretion by refusing to permit the Township to supplement the record with additional evidence regarding Ordinance No. 5 of 2017.

The trial court permitted the Township to enter the certified copy of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 into the record but denied the request to present additional testimony. The trial court reasoned that the Township had the opportunity to be fully heard at the September 7, 2017 hearing and failed to indicate that evidence was excluded or that it intended to present more. A trial court has discretion to allow the introduction of additional evidence on appeal, but such discretion is not unlimited. *Shaffer v. City of Philadelphia*, 94 Pa.Cmwlth. 280, 503 A.2d 1050 (1986). Given that the Township's expert had already testified, and the core issue was the legal effect of the ordinance rather than a lack of factual evidence, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in limiting the supplementation to the ordinance itself.

Based on the foregoing, the Township of Wilmington has failed to demonstrate that the trial court committed an error of law or an abuse of discretion. The trial court correctly determined that the appeal was not moot, that the pending ordinance doctrine did not apply, and that the Township failed to prove that the Wimers' proposed use was injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare.

## II. THE APPEAL IS MOOT BECAUSE ORDINANCE NO. 5 OF 2017 CURES ANY ALLEGED EXCLUSIONARY DEFECT

The Wimers contend that Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 does not render this appeal moot because it imposes restrictions making their proposed use "economically unviable." This argument fundamentally misconstrues the nature of a curative amendment and the Township's legitimate authority to regulate land uses.

Pennsylvania law recognizes that municipalities possess broad police power to regulate land uses in the public interest, including imposing reasonable restrictions on commercial activities in agricultural zones. The Wimers' position—that any regulation rendering a proposed use economically unviable constitutes a constitutional violation—would eviscerate this well-established principle. As the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania recognized in Wilson v. Township of Plum, 527 Pa. 155, 589 A.2d 1139 (1991), zoning regulations need not accommodate every conceivable use of property; rather, they must simply provide for reasonable uses within each zoning classification.

The trial court erred in finding that the 12-event annual limit and 300-guest cap rendered Ordinance No. 5 "effectively prohibitive." This finding ignores the Township's legitimate interest in preserving the agricultural character of the district while permitting limited commercial accessory uses. The restrictions in Ordinance No. 5 represent a reasonable balancing of the Wimers' entrepreneurial interests against the Township's responsibility to maintain orderly land use patterns and protect neighboring properties from excessive commercial activity.

Furthermore, the Wimers' argument that their business model "requires" more than 12 events annually does not establish a constitutional entitlement to operate without restriction. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court has consistently held that economic hardship alone does not trigger relief under the curative amendment statute. See Lord v. The same principle applies with equal force to curative amendment challenges.

The Township's adoption of Ordinance No. 5 demonstrates good-faith effort to address the alleged deficiency in the Zoning Ordinance by providing a mechanism for special event barns in agricultural districts. That the Wimers find the regulatory framework unprofitable does not render the ordinance exclusionary or the appeal non-moot.

## III. THE PENDING ORDINANCE DOCTRINE SHOULD HAVE BARRED THE CHALLENGE

The Wimers argue that the pending ordinance doctrine does not apply because the Township had not publicly advertised its intent to adopt Ordinance No. 5 when they filed their challenge in July 2017. This argument ignores the temporal relationship between the Wimers' challenge and ongoing legislative proceedings.

The doctrine articulated in Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township, 459 Pa. 219, 328 A.2d 464 (1974), provides that a land use challenge is premature when the municipality has manifested a clear intent to address the specific zoning issue through pending legislation. Here, the record establishes that:

1. In September 2016, the Township's Planning Commission drafted a proposed amendment specifically addressing wedding barns;
2. In November 2016, the Board considered this amendment;
3. In January 2017, the Township sent three draft ordinances (Ordinance Nos. 1, 2, and 3 of 2017) to the County Planning Commission;
4. In February 2017, public hearings were advertised on Ordinance No. 1;
5. In June 2017, the Township forwarded Ordinances No. 2 and 3 for review.

By July 20, 2017, when the Wimers filed their challenge, the Township had been actively working on wedding barn regulations for approximately ten months. The Wimers' attempt to characterize these sustained legislative efforts as mere "internal deliberations" misrepresents the factual record. The Planning Commission's draft ordinances, the Board meetings, the County Planning Commission reviews, and the public hearings on Ordinance No. 1 collectively constitute the kind of "public declaration of intent" contemplated by Casey.

The Wimers essentially ask this Court to adopt a rule that would allow landowners to file curative amendment challenges whenever legislation is pending, thereby hijacking the legislative process. Such a rule would discourage municipalities from attempting to address zoning deficiencies through democratic means, as any effort at legislative reform would immediately trigger costly and time-consuming legal challenges. The pending ordinance doctrine exists precisely to prevent this outcome.

The trial court erred in finding the doctrine inapplicable based solely on the absence of formal advertisement for Ordinance No. 5. The doctrine should have barred the Wimers' challenge.

## IV. THE ZONING ORDINANCE WAS NOT EXCLUSIONARY

The Wimers' claim that the Zoning Ordinance was exclusionary fails for multiple reasons.

First, the existence of the Five Fillies special exception demonstrates that the ordinance was nottotally exclusionary. In Surrick v. The Five Fillies decision, while the trial court characterized it as an "aberration," nonetheless demonstrates that the Zoning Hearing Board had authority to grant special exceptions for wedding barn uses under appropriate circumstances.

The Wimers argue they should not be required to seek a special exception because the ordinance was "silent" on wedding barns. However, silence does not equal exclusion. Pennsylvania law recognizes that uses not specifically enumerated in a zoning ordinance may be permitted through special exception procedures. See Zoning Hearing Board of Upper Milford Township v. Second, the trial court improperly shifted the burden of proof by requiring the Township to prove that the Wimers' proposed use would not be injurious to the public health, safety, and welfare. Under Pennsylvania law, the burden remains on the challenging landowner to prove that the ordinance is exclusionary. See Delaware River Port Authority v. Pennsylvania Public Utility Commission, 522 Pa. 414, 562 A.2d 297 (1989). The Wimers failed to meet this burden because they did not establish that wedding barns are a legitimate use that the ordinance must accommodate in any zoning district.

Third, even if the ordinance were deemed exclusionary, the remedy is not to grant the specific curative amendment proposed by the landowner. The trial court erred by effectively mandating approval of the Wimers' unlimited-event, 300-guest proposal rather than remand for the Township to fashion an appropriate regulatory scheme.

The Wimers argue that the Board lacked substantial evidence to deny their challenge. This argument misappplies the substantial evidence standard.

The Wimers argue that the Township was required to prove "actual harm" to the public interest. However, the law does not impose such a burden at the curative amendment stage. Once a municipality demonstrates that it has considered the challenger's proposal and determined that alternative regulatory approaches are appropriate, the Board satisfies its obligations under the MPC. The trial court erred in substituting its judgment for that of the Board on questions of ordinance drafting and community impact.

## VI. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN REFUSING TO PERMIT SUPPLEMENTATION OF THE RECORD

The Township sought to supplement the record with evidence regarding Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, including testimony explaining how the ordinance addresses the concerns raised by Mr. Trant. The trial court denied this request, permitting only introduction of the ordinance itself.

Pennsylvania law grants trial courts broad discretion in managing the evidentiary record on land use appeals. However, this discretion must be exercised in light of the substantive rights of the parties. In Shaffer v. City of Philadelphia, 94 Pa.Cmwlth. 280, 503 A.2d 1050 (1986), this Court held that a trial court may permit supplementation of the record where additional evidence would assist in resolving the issues on appeal.

Here, the trial court's refusal to permit testimony regarding Ordinance No. 5 effectively prevented the Township from demonstrating how the ordinance cures any alleged exclusionary defect. The Wimers were permitted to present testimony regarding their business model and financial requirements; the Township should have been afforded equal opportunity to explain the rationale behind the ordinance's restrictions.

The trial court's ruling created an asymmetric evidentiary record that prejudiced the Township's ability to defend its legislative judgment. This constitutes an abuse of discretion requiring reversal.

**OPINION**

**TOWNSHIP OF WILMINGTON, Appellant**
v.
**WIMER REALTY, LLC, WIMER HOLDINGS CORPORATION, RICHARD G. WIMER JR., AND JODY WIMER, Appellees**

**NO.:** [Docket Number Not Provided]
**DATE:** [Current Date]

**BEFORE:** **JUDGE, P.**

**OPINION**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Township of Wilmington (Township) appeals from the Order of the Court of Common Pleas of Lawrence County (trial court) granting the land use appeal of Wimer Realty, LLC, Wimer Holdings Corporation, and Richard G. Wimer Jr. and Jody Wimer (collectively, the Wimers). The trial court found that the Township's Zoning Ordinance was exclusionary as it related to the Wimers' proposed use of their property as a wedding venue, and it granted the Wimers' proposed curative amendment. The Township argues that the appeal is moot due to the subsequent adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, that the pending ordinance doctrine barred the challenge, that the ordinance was not exclusionary, and that the trial court abused its discretion regarding the record. After careful consideration of the parties' rebuttals, we affirm the trial court's order.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Appeal Is Not Moot**

The Township argues that the adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, which permits "Special Event Barns" as an accessory use in agricultural districts with a limit of 12 events per year and a maximum of 300 guests, rendered the Wimers' challenge moot. The Township relies on *Wilson v. Township of Plum*, 527 Pa. 155, 589 A.2d 1139 (1991), and *Lord v. Zoning Hearing Board of Borough of Quakertown*, 556 Pa. 81, 726 A.2d 1004 (1999), for the proposition that economic hardship does not invalidate a zoning ordinance.

While we agree with the general principle that zoning regulations need not accommodate every conceivable use of property, *Wilson*, 589 A.2d at 1143, the issue here is whether the controversy remains live. A case is moot when the court can no longer grant effective relief. *In re Miller & Son Paving, Inc.*, 161 Pa.Cmwlth. 138, 636 A.2d 274, 277 (1993). The trial court found that the restrictions in Ordinance No. 5—specifically the 12-event limit—would "substantially impact[] the Wimers' ability to conduct their proposed use in a manner which was financially and economically reasonable." Mr. Wimer testified that more than 12 events per year were necessary for the business to be feasible.

The Township's argument conflates the standard for a variance with that of a curative amendment. *Lord*, which deals with unnecessary hardship for a variance, is inapposite to a substantive challenge to the validity of the zoning ordinance itself. If the ordinance permits a use but imposes restrictions so severe that the use cannot reasonably exist, the ordinance may still be exclusionary as applied to that legitimate use. *In re Appeal of Girsh*, 437 Pa. 237, 263 A.2d 395, 399 (1970) (stating that zoning must provide for reasonable use of land). Because Ordinance No. 5 effectively prohibits the economically viable use the Wimers seek to establish, the trial court correctly concluded that the appeal was not moot.

**B. The Pending Ordinance Doctrine Does Not Apply**

The Township contends that the pending ordinance doctrine should have barred the Wimers' challenge because the Township was actively engaged in the legislative process regarding wedding barns for ten months prior to the challenge. The Township relies on *Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township*, 459 Pa. 219, 328 A.2d 464 (1974).

Under *Casey*, a land use challenge is premature only if the municipality has publicly declared its intent to consider a particular zoning scheme before the landowner files the challenge. *Casey*, 328 A.2d at 467. The record shows that the Wimers filed their challenge on July 20, 2017. At that time, the Township had voted down Ordinance No. 1 of 2017, and Ordinances No. 2 and 3 had been forwarded to planning commissions for review but had not been advertised for adoption. The Township did not approve the advertisement of Ordinance No. 5 until after the Board denied the Wimers' challenge on September 7, 2017.

The Township's argument that the "sustained legislative efforts" and "draft ordinances" constituted a public declaration of intent is unavailing. Mere internal deliberation, drafting, or committee review, without a public declaration of intent to adopt a specific ordinance, is insufficient to trigger the pending ordinance doctrine. *In re Zoning Hearing of Zoning Hearing Board of Haverford Township*, 412 Pa. 426, 194 A.2d 697, 699 (1963) (noting that the doctrine applies where the governing body has given formal indication of its intention to enact the legislation). Because the Wimers filed their challenge before the Township took any formal action to advertise the specific ordinance that eventually passed, the trial court properly found the doctrine inapplicable.

**C. The Zoning Ordinance Was Exclusionary**

The Township argues that the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary because the Zoning Hearing Board had previously granted a special exception for "Five Fillies," another wedding barn. The Township cites *Surrick v. Zoning Hearing Board of Upper Providence Township*, 552 Pa. 332, 705 A.2d 841 (1997), for the proposition that a zoning ordinance is not exclusionary if it provides some mechanism for the use.

We are not persuaded. The trial court characterized the "Five Fillies" decision as an "aberration" because the ordinance was silent on wedding barns at the time. A zoning ordinance that is silent regarding a specific use effectively excludes that use unless the silence can be reasonably interpreted to permit it through existing use categories. *Hunt v. Township of Upper St. Clair*, 505 Pa. 249, 479 A.2d 969, 972 (1984). The existence of a single special exception does not establish that the ordinance provides a reasonable mechanism for the general public to utilize the land for that purpose. *Surrick* involved an ordinance that explicitly provided for the use in question, unlike the silence present here. The trial court correctly determined that the Wimers were not required to submit to a special exception hearing for a use that was not provided for in the ordinance.

**D. The Township Failed to Meet Its Burden of Proof**

Once a landowner successfully challenges an ordinance as exclusionary, the burden shifts to the municipality to prove that the proposed use is injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare. *Casey*, 328 A.2d at 464; *In re Appeal of Kit-Mar Builders*, 439 Pa. 466, 268 A.2d 765, 767 (1970). The Township argues that the Board's decision was supported by substantial evidence, pointing to the testimony of its expert, Mr. Trant, regarding the drafting of the curative amendment, and the testimony of residents.

However, Mr. Trant testified only that the curative amendment was "too specific" and "difficult to enforce." He did not testify that the proposed use would pose a threat to the health, safety, or welfare of the residents. Technical objections to the drafting of a curative amendment do not satisfy the municipality's burden under *Casey* to show injury to the public interest. Furthermore, the subjective opinions of residents, without evidence of specific harm to public welfare, are insufficient to meet this burden. The trial court correctly found that the Township failed to demonstrate that the Wimers' use would be injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare.

**E. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Refusing to Supplement the Record**

Finally, the Township argues that the trial court abused its discretion by refusing to permit the Township to supplement the record with additional testimony regarding Ordinance No. 5 of 2017. The Township relies on *Shaffer v. City of Philadelphia*, 94 Pa.Cmwlth. 280, 503 A.2d 1050 (1986).

The trial court permitted the Township to enter the certified copy of Ordinance No. 5 into the record but denied the request to present new testimony. The trial court reasoned that the Township had the opportunity to be fully heard at the September 7, 2017 hearing and failed to indicate that evidence was excluded or that it intended to present more. A trial court has discretion to allow the introduction of additional evidence on appeal, but such discretion is not unlimited. *Shaffer*, 503 A.2d at 1053. Given that the core issue was the legal effect of the ordinance and the Township's failure to prove harm to public welfare—a failure established by the existing record—the trial court did not abuse its discretion in limiting the supplementation to the ordinance itself.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the Township of Wilmington has failed to demonstrate that the trial court committed an error of law or an abuse of discretion. The trial court correctly determined that the appeal was not moot, that the pending ordinance doctrine did not apply, that the Zoning Ordinance was exclusionary, and that the Township failed to prove that the Wimers' proposed use was injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare.

The question is not whether the plaintiff finds the regulations economically convenient, but whether the ordinance now provides for the use at issue. Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 explicitly permits "Special Event Barns" as an accessory use in agricultural zoning districts. This is not a case where the ordinance remains silent or prohibits the use entirely. The plaintiff's reliance on *In re Appeal of Girsh*, 437 Pa. 237, 263 A.2d 395, 399 (1970), is misplaced. That case stands for the proposition that zoning must provide for reasonable use of land, not that every regulation must accommodate a landowner's preferred level of profitability. The 12-event limit and 300-guest cap are classic examples of reasonable zoning conditions designed to balance agricultural character with commercial activity. Such restrictions fall well within the Township's police powers.

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish *Wilson v. Township of Plum*, 527 Pa. 155, 589 A.2d 1139 (1991), and *Lord v. Economic hardship is not a basis for invalidating zoning restrictions. *Wilson*, 589 A.2d at 1143. A landowner's desire to maximize profits does not transform reasonable regulations into exclusionary ones. ## II. THE PENDING ORDINANCE DOCTRINE BARS THE WIMERS' CHALLENGE

The plaintiff argues that the pending ordinance doctrine does not apply because the Township had not formally advertised its intent to adopt a specific ordinance when the Wimers filed their challenge in July 2017. In *Casey v. The doctrine exists to prevent landowners from circumventing the legislative process by filing challenges while the municipality is actively engaged in addressing the very issue.

The record demonstrates that the Township was engaged in a comprehensive legislative effort regarding wedding barns for approximately ten months before the Wimers filed their challenge. The Township's Planning Commission drafted a proposed amendment in September 2016. Ordinance No. 1 of 2017 was considered in March 2017. By July 2017, when the Wimers filed their challenge, Ordinances No. 2 and 3 had been forwarded to planning commissions for review. The Township was clearly working on a solution to the wedding barn issue.

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish *Casey* by arguing that no formal advertisement had occurred. However, this formalistic interpretation contradicts the purpose of the doctrine. The Township's actions were not secret; they were part of the public record. The Planning Commission's work, the Board meetings, and the referral of draft ordinances to planning commissions were all matters of public record. To hold that the doctrine requires advertisement of a specific ordinance before any challenge can be premature would incentivize landowners to file challenges immediately upon learning of any legislative discussion, thereby disrupting the democratic process.

The trial court erred in applying a narrow interpretation of "public declaration" that ignores the reality of the Township's sustained legislative efforts. The Wimers should have been required to allow the legislative process to conclude before challenging the ordinance.

---

## III. THE ZONING ORDINANCE WAS NOT EXCLUSIONARY

The plaintiff claims that the Zoning Ordinance was exclusionary because it was silent regarding wedding barns and because the Five Fillies special exception was an "aberration." This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the existence of the Five Fillies special exception directly refutes the claim of exclusion. In *Surrick v. Zoning Hearing Board of Upper Providence Township*, 552 Pa. 332, 705 A.2d 841, 846 (1997), the Pennsylvania Supreme Court held that a zoning ordinance is not exclusionary if it provides some mechanism—such as special exceptions or conditional uses—for the use at issue. The Zoning Hearing Board granted Five Fillies a special exception to operate a wedding barn, demonstrating that the ordinance provided a mechanism for this use. The plaintiff cannot ignore this mechanism and claim the ordinance was exclusionary.

The trial court's characterization of the Five Fillies decision as an "aberration" is unsupported by the record and contrary to law. The Zoning Hearing Board, not the trial court or this Court, has expertise in zoning matters. Its decision to grant a special exception demonstrates that the ordinance, while silent, was not intended to exclude wedding barns entirely. The proper avenue for the Wimers was to seek a similar special exception, not to challenge the ordinance as exclusionary.

Second, the plaintiff misapplies *Hunt v. Township of Upper St. Clair*, 505 Pa. 249, 479 A.2d 969, 972 (1984). That case stands for the proposition that silence in an ordinance may constitute exclusion when the use cannot be reasonably interpreted through existing categories. However, the Five Fillies special exception demonstrates that wedding barns could be reasonably accommodated through the special exception process. The ordinance was not silent in practice—it provided a mechanism that had been utilized.

The plaintiff attempts to distinguish *Surrick* by noting that it involved an ordinance that explicitly permitted the use. Yet this distinction ignores the practical reality that the Five Fillies decision created a path for wedding barns in the Township. The ordinance's silence was interpreted by the Zoning Hearing Board to permit the use through special exception—a reasonable interpretation that the Wimers could have followed.

---

## IV. THE TOWNSHIP MET ITS BURDEN OF PROOF

The plaintiff claims that the Township failed to meet its burden of proving that the Wimers' proposed use would be injurious to public health, safety, or welfare. This argument misconstrues the burden allocation and the evidence presented.

Once a landowner establishes that an ordinance is exclusionary, the burden shifts to the municipality to prove that the proposed use would be injurious to the public interest. *Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township*, 328 A.2d at 467; *In re Appeal of Kit-Mar Builders*, 439 Pa. 466, 268 A.2d 765, 767 (1970). However, this burden is not met simply by the landowner's showing of exclusion. The municipality must have an opportunity to respond to the landowner's proposed use.

Here, the Township presented testimony from John Trant, a certified land use planning consultant, who testified that the Wimers' proposed curative amendment was "too specific" and "difficult to enforce." While the plaintiff characterizes this as merely "technical objections," this testimony goes to the heart of the Township's concern: ensuring that any approval of wedding barns can be properly regulated and enforced throughout the Township. Mr. Trant's concerns were legitimate land use planning considerations that the Board was entitled to weigh.

Additionally, Township residents testified regarding their concerns about the proposed use. The plaintiff dismisses these concerns as "subjective preferences," but community input is a valid consideration in zoning decisions. The Board is elected to represent the interests of Township residents, and their testimony regarding potential impacts on the agricultural character of the area, noise, traffic, and property values is relevant to the Board's determination.

The trial court erred in requiring the Township to produce "expert testimony on public safety risks, no studies on property values, no data on emergency response." This elevated standard has no basis in Pennsylvania zoning law. The Township's burden is to demonstrate that the proposed use could be injurious to the public welfare—not to produce exhaustive studies proving harm with scientific certainty. The testimony of Mr. Trant and Township residents was sufficient to support the Board's decision to deny the curative amendment.

---

## V. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN REFUSING TO PERMIT SUPPLEMENTATION OF THE RECORD

Finally, the plaintiff defends the trial court's refusal to permit the Township to supplement the record with additional testimony regarding Ordinance No. 5 of 2017. This argument fails because the trial court abused its discretion in a manner that prejudiced the Township's case.

In *Shaffer v. City of Philadelphia*, 94 Pa.Cmwlth. 280, 503 A.2d 1050, 1053 (1986), this Court held that trial courts have discretion to allow the introduction of additional evidence on appeal, but that discretion must be exercised in a manner that ensures fairness to both parties. Here, the Township sought to introduce testimony regarding Ordinance No. 5—which was adopted after the Board hearing—to demonstrate that the ordinance cured any alleged deficiency and to explain the rationale behind the restrictions.

The trial court permitted the Township to enter the certified copy of Ordinance No. 5 into the record but denied the request to present new testimony. This partial restriction was an abuse of discretion. The Township could not have presented evidence regarding an ordinance that had not yet been adopted at the time of the September 2017 hearing. To deny the Township the opportunity to explain the ordinance's provisions and the reasoning behind them prejudiced the Township's ability to defend its position.

The plaintiff's argument that the Township had "ample opportunity" to present evidence at the September 2017 hearing is unavailing. The ordinance at issue did not exist at that time. The Township cannot be faulted for failing to present evidence regarding a future ordinance. The trial court's refusal to permit supplementation effectively prevented the Township from presenting its strongest defense—that Ordinance No. 5 addressed the Wimers' concerns by providing for the use.

---

## CONCLUSION

The Township respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's order and dismiss the Wimers' land use appeal. The appeal is moot because Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 provides for the Wimers' proposed use. The pending ordinance doctrine bars the challenge because the Township was actively engaged in the legislative process. The Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary because the Five Fillies special exception demonstrates a mechanism for the use. The Township met its burden of proving that the proposed use warranted careful consideration. And the trial court abused its discretion in refusing to permit supplementation of the record.

The plaintiff has asked this Court to adopt a rule that would allow landowners to challenge zoning ordinances whenever they find the regulations inconvenient, while simultaneously preventing municipalities from responding with legislative solutions. This would invert the balance of power in Pennsylvania zoning law and undermine the police powers of local governments. The Township's zoning ordinance, as amended by Ordinance No. 5, represents a reasonable exercise of the Township's authority to regulate land use in a manner that balances agricultural preservation with limited commercial activity.

For the foregoing reasons, the Township of Wilmington respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's order and deny the Wimers' land use appeal.

**OPINION**

**TOWNSHIP OF WILMINGTON, Appellant**
v.
**WIMER REALTY, LLC, WIMER HOLDINGS CORPORATION, RICHARD G. WIMER JR., AND JODY WIMER, Appellees**

**NO.:** [Docket Number Not Provided]
**DATE:** [Current Date]

**BEFORE:** **JUDGE, P.**

**OPINION**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The Township of Wilmington (Township) appeals from the Order of the Court of Common Pleas of Lawrence County (trial court) granting the land use appeal of Wimer Realty, LLC, Wimer Holdings Corporation, and Richard G. Wimer Jr. and Jody Wimer (collectively, the Wimers). The trial court found that the Township's Zoning Ordinance was exclusionary and granted the Wimers' proposed curative amendment. The Township argues that the appeal is moot due to the subsequent adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017, that the pending ordinance doctrine bars the challenge, that the ordinance was not exclusionary, that the Township met its burden of proof, and that the trial court abused its discretion regarding the record. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties, we affirm the trial court's order.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Appeal Is Not Moot**

The Township reiterates its argument that the adoption of Ordinance No. 5 of 2017 rendered the Wimers' challenge moot because the ordinance now permits "Special Event Barns." The Township relies on *Wilson v. Township of Plum*, 527 Pa. 155, 589 A.2d 1139 (1991), and *Lord v. Zoning Hearing Board of Borough of Quakertown*, 556 Pa. 81, 726 A.2d 1004 (1999), to assert that economic hardship does not invalidate a zoning ordinance.

We find this argument unpersuasive in the context of a curative amendment challenge. *Wilson* and *Lord* address the standard for variances, where a landowner must prove unnecessary hardship. Here, the Wimers are challenging the substantive validity of the zoning ordinance itself. As the trial court found, Ordinance No. 5 imposes a 12-event limit per year. Mr. Wimer testified that more than 12 events are necessary for the business to be financially feasible. If an ordinance permits a use but imposes restrictions so severe that the use cannot reasonably exist, the ordinance may still be exclusionary as applied to that legitimate use. *In re Appeal of Girsh*, 437 Pa. 237, 263 A.2d 395, 399 (1970) (stating that zoning must provide for reasonable use of land). Because Ordinance No. 5 effectively prohibits the economically viable use the Wimers seek to establish, the controversy remains live, and the appeal is not moot.

**B. The Pending Ordinance Doctrine Does Not Apply**

The Township contends that the pending ordinance doctrine should bar the Wimers' challenge because the Township was engaged in "sustained legislative efforts" for ten months prior to the filing. The Township argues that the public record of these efforts constitutes a "public declaration of intent" under *Casey v. Zoning Hearing Board of Warwick Township*, 459 Pa. 219, 328 A.2d 464 (1974).

We disagree. *Casey* requires that a municipality publicly declare its intent to consider a *particular* zoning scheme before the landowner files the challenge. *Casey*, 328 A.2d at 467. The record shows that the Wimers filed their challenge on July 20, 2017. At that time, the Township had voted down Ordinance No. 1 of 2017, and Ordinances No. 2 and 3 had been forwarded to planning commissions for review but had not been advertised for adoption. The Township did not approve the advertisement of the specific ordinance that eventually became Ordinance No. 5 until after the Board denied the Wimers' challenge on September 7, 2017. Mere internal deliberation, drafting, or committee review, without a public declaration of intent to adopt a specific ordinance, is insufficient to trigger the pending ordinance doctrine. *In re Zoning Hearing of Zoning Hearing Board of Haverford Township*, 412 Pa. 426, 194 A.2d 697, 699 (1963). The Wimers filed their challenge before the Township took any formal action to advertise the specific ordinance that eventually passed, so the doctrine does not apply.

**C. The Zoning Ordinance Was Exclusionary**

The Township argues that the Zoning Ordinance was not exclusionary because the Zoning Hearing Board had previously granted a special exception for "Five Fillies," another wedding barn. The Township cites *Surrick v. Zoning Hearing Board of Upper Providence Township*, 552 Pa. 332, 705 A.2d 841 (1997), for the proposition that a zoning ordinance is not exclusionary if it provides some mechanism for the use.

We are not persuaded. The trial court characterized the "Five Fillies" decision as an "aberration" because the ordinance was silent on wedding barns at the time. A zoning ordinance that is silent regarding a specific use effectively excludes that use unless the silence can be reasonably interpreted to permit it through existing use categories. *Hunt v. Township of Upper St. Clair*, 505 Pa. 249, 479 A.2d 969, 972 (1984). The existence of a single special exception does not establish that the ordinance provides a reasonable mechanism for the general public to utilize the land for that purpose. *Surrick* involved an ordinance that explicitly provided for the use in question, unlike the silence present here. The trial court correctly determined that the Wimers were not required to submit to a special exception hearing for a use that was not provided for in the ordinance.

**D. The Township Failed to Meet Its Burden of Proof**

The Township argues that it met its burden of proof through the testimony of its expert, Mr. Trant, and resident testimony. The Township contends that Mr. Trant's testimony regarding the "difficulty to enforce" the curative amendment and the residents' concerns constitute sufficient evidence of potential injury to public welfare.

Once a landowner successfully challenges an ordinance as exclusionary, the burden shifts to the municipality to prove that the proposed use is injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare. *Casey*, 328 A.2d at 464; *In re Appeal of Kit-Mar Builders*, 439 Pa. 466, 268 A.2d 765, 767 (1970). Mr. Trant testified only that the curative amendment was "too specific" and "difficult to enforce." He did not testify that the proposed use would pose a threat to the health, safety, or welfare of the residents. Technical objections to the drafting of a curative amendment do not satisfy the municipality's burden under *Casey* to show injury to the public interest. Furthermore, the subjective opinions of residents, without evidence of specific harm to public welfare, are insufficient to meet this burden. The trial court correctly found that the Township failed to demonstrate that the Wimers' use would be injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare.

**E. The Trial Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Refusing to Supplement the Record**

Finally, the Township argues that the trial court abused its discretion by refusing to permit the Township to supplement the record with additional testimony regarding Ordinance No. 5 of 2017. The Township relies on *Shaffer v. City of Philadelphia*, 94 Pa.Cmwlth. 280, 503 A.2d 1050 (1986).

The trial court permitted the Township to enter the certified copy of Ordinance No. 5 into the record but denied the request to present new testimony. The trial court reasoned that the Township had the opportunity to be fully heard at the September 7, 2017 hearing and failed to indicate that evidence was excluded or that it intended to present more. A trial court has discretion to allow the introduction of additional evidence on appeal, but such discretion is not unlimited. *Shaffer*, 503 A.2d at 1053. Given that the core issue was the legal effect of the ordinance and the Township's failure to prove harm to public welfare—a failure established by the existing record—the trial court did not abuse its discretion in limiting the supplementation to the ordinance itself. The Township's inability to present testimony on an ordinance that did not exist at the time of the hearing does not constitute an abuse of discretion, as the underlying facts regarding the impact of the use could have been presented at that time.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the Township of Wilmington has failed to demonstrate that the trial court committed an error of law or an abuse of discretion. The trial court correctly determined that the appeal was not moot, that the pending ordinance doctrine did not apply, that the Zoning Ordinance was exclusionary, and that the Township failed to prove that the Wimers' proposed use was injurious to the public health, safety, or welfare.
