## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Court of Appeals Correctly Held That § 25-1329 Does Not Apply to Judgments in Petition in Error Proceedings

The central question is whether Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329, which provides for motions to alter or amend judgments and tolls the appeal period when such motions are filed, applies to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings. The Court of Appeals correctly answered this question in the negative.

#### 1. The Nature of Petition in Error Proceedings

A petition in error is a statutory mechanism by which a district court sits in an appellate capacity to review administrative decisions. See Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 20-1019 through 20-1029 (Reissue 2016). Unlike original civil actions where the district court acts as a trial court making findings of fact and conclusions of law, a petition in error is a proceeding in which the district court reviews the record before an administrative body. The court does not receive evidence; it examines whether the administrative body acted within its authority and whether its findings are supported by sufficient evidence.

This Court has consistently recognized the distinct nature of petition in error proceedings. In *Goodman v. City of Omaha*, 289 Neb. 784, 857 N.W.2d 762 (2015), this Court addressed the applicability of post-trial motions to judgments in error proceedings and held that the district court, sitting in its appellate capacity, was not bound by the same procedural rules applicable to trial court judgments.

Similarly, in *Timmerman v. Neth*, 285 Neb. 366, 826 N.W.2d 927 (2013), this Court explained that when a district court acts as an intermediate appellate court reviewing an administrative decision, the procedures governing original proceedings do not apply. The Court specifically addressed whether a motion to alter or amend a judgment would toll the appeal period and concluded that such motions are not applicable in the error proceeding context.

#### 2. The Plain Language of § 25-1329

The Appellant argues that § 25-1329 applies because it refers to "a judgment" without limitation. This argument ignores the statute's context and purpose.

Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329 provides:

> A motion to alter or amend a judgment shall be filed not later than ten days after the entry of the judgment. If such motion is filed, the time for appeal shall be tolled until the entry of the order overruling the motion.

While the statute refers to "a judgment" in general terms, this Court must interpret the statute in light of the entire statutory scheme. The Nebraska Rules of Civil Procedure distinguish between original proceedings and petition in error proceedings. The procedural rules governing new trial motions, alter-or-amend motions, and similar post-judgment motions are found in the civil procedure code, which applies to "civil proceedings" before the district courts. See Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-101.

Petition in error proceedings are governed by a separate statutory scheme. See Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 20-1019 through 20-1029. The Legislature knew how to make statutes applicable to specific proceedings when it intended to do so. When it enacted § 25-1329, it did not specifically reference petition in error proceedings, nor did it indicate that the statute would apply to judgments entered in appellate capacities.

This Court has repeatedly held that statutes must be interpreted in context. See *State v. Kuehn*, 284 Neb. 598, 822 N.W.2d 437 (2012). The context here is critical: § 25-1329 is part of the Nebraska Rules of Civil Procedure governing original district court proceedings, not appellate review proceedings.

#### 3. The Legislature Did Not Intend § 25-1329 to Apply to Petition in Error Proceedings

The Appellant's argument that the Legislature intended § 25-1329 to apply universally rests on a misinterpretation of legislative intent. The Legislature has enacted a comprehensive scheme for reviewing administrative decisions through petitions in error. This scheme includes specific provisions for seeking rehearing before the administrative body and for seeking further review before this Court. The Legislature did not incorporate § 25-1329 into this scheme.

If the Legislature had intended § 25-1329 to apply to petition in error proceedings, it would have said so explicitly. The absence of any such indication demonstrates that the statute was not meant to apply to judgments entered in error proceedings.

This Court should not add words to a statute that the Legislature did not include. See *Ficke v. Ficke*, 290 Neb.  internal (2016) (courts cannot read meaning into a statute that is not there).

### B. The District Court Correctly Concluded That the Motion Was Not a Motion to Alter or Amend Under § 25-1329

The district court addressed the Appellant's motion on its merits and concluded that it was not a proper motion to alter or amend a judgment. The court explained that because no trial had occurred—it had only reviewed the administrative transcript—a motion for new trial was not proper. Regarding the motion to vacate, the court treated it as a motion for relief from judgment under general principles but concluded that the alleged factual error did not affect the merits.

The Appellant argues that the district court should have treated the motion as one to alter or amend under § 25-1329. However, the district court's characterization of the motion is consistent with established law. A motion to alter or amend is appropriate where the district court, sitting as a trial court, has made an error of law or fact in its original judgment. Here, the district court was sitting in an appellate capacity, reviewing the administrative record. Its "judgment" was not the type of judgment contemplated by § 25-1329.

The district court's own analysis confirms this distinction. The court did not treat the motion as one to alter or amend; it treated it as a motion seeking different relief. The Appellant cannot now argue that the motion should have been treated differently when the court correctly identified its nature.

### C. The Appellant's Notice of Appeal Was Untimely

The procedural timeline demonstrates the untimeliness of the appeal. The district court entered its judgment on March 31, 2017. Under Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1912, a notice of appeal must be filed within thirty days of a final judgment.

The Appellant did not file a notice of appeal within thirty days of March 31, 2017. Instead, he filed a motion to vacate on April 10, 2017—ten days after the judgment. He then waited until after the district court denied that motion on May 25, 2017, to file his notice of appeal.

If § 25-1329 does not apply to petition in error proceedings—as this Court should hold—then the motion to vacate did not toll the appeal period. The notice of appeal, filed more than thirty days after the March 31 judgment, was untimely.

The Court of Appeals correctly dismissed the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. An appellate court lacks jurisdiction to hear an appeal from an untimely notice of appeal. See *Anderson v. Broling*, 258 Neb. 112, 602 N.W.2d 506 (1999).

### D. The Cases Relied Upon by the Appellant Do Not Support His Position

The Appellant relies on *Goodman v. City of Omaha* and *Timmerman v. Neth* for the proposition that motions to alter or amend judgments should apply in petition in error proceedings. However, these cases actually support the opposite conclusion.

In *Goodman*, this Court addressed whether a motion for new trial was proper following a judgment in a petition in error proceeding. The Court held that such a motion was not proper because the district court had not conducted a trial; it had only reviewed the administrative record. The Court explained that the procedural rules governing new trials apply to original proceedings, not to error proceedings.

Similarly, in *Timmerman*, this Court considered whether a motion to alter or amend a judgment would toll the appeal period in an error proceeding. The Court concluded that it would not, specifically because the district court was acting in an appellate capacity.

That case does not address whether a motion to vacate tolls the appeal period in an error proceeding.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for further review following the Court of Appeals’ dismissal of Appellant Dr. Robert McEwen’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The central issue is whether Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329 (Reissue 2016), which provides for motions to alter or amend a judgment and tolls the time for appeal, applies to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings. Based on the arguments presented and the applicable law, the Court finds that the Court of Appeals did not err in dismissing the appeal.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The question of whether an appellate court has jurisdiction is a matter of law, which requires this Court to reach a conclusion independent of the lower court’s decision. *State v. Kuehn*, 284 Neb. 598, 822 N.W.2d 437 (2012). Similarly, the interpretation of a statute is a question of law. *Id.*

**III. ANALYSIS**

Appellant argues that Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329 unambiguously applies to "a judgment," regardless of whether that judgment was entered in an original action or a petition in error proceeding. Appellant contends that the plain language of the statute controls and that the Court of Appeals erred by creating an exception for petitions in error based on precedent that Appellant claims misreads the statute.

Appellee counters that § 25-1329 is part of the civil procedure code governing original proceedings and does not apply to petition in error proceedings, which are governed by a separate statutory scheme. Appellee asserts that the district court, when reviewing a petition in error, acts as an intermediate appellate court reviewing an administrative record, not as a trial court conducting a trial. Therefore, Appellee argues that post-trial mechanisms such as motions to alter or amend are inapplicable.

The Court agrees with Appellee. While the plain language of a statute is the starting point for statutory construction, it must be interpreted in context of the statutory scheme as a whole. *State v. Kuehn*, 284 Neb. 598, 822 N.W.2d 437 (2012). Section 25-1329 is located within the Nebraska Rules of Civil Procedure, which govern original civil actions. In contrast, petitions in error are governed by Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 20-1019 through 20-1029 (Reissue 2016). This distinction is significant.

In *Goodman v. City of Omaha*, 289 Neb. 784, 857 N.W.2d 762 (2015), this Court addressed the applicability of post-trial motions to judgments in error proceedings. The Court held that because the district court sits in an appellate capacity in error proceedings and does not conduct a trial, the procedural rules governing new trials do not apply. *Id.* This reasoning extends to motions to alter or amend a judgment under § 25-1329, which are similarly designed to allow a trial court to correct its own errors in the context of a trial or original action.

Similarly, in *Timmerman v. Neth*, 285 Neb. 366, 826 N.W.2d 927 (2013), the Court considered whether a motion to alter or amend a judgment would toll the appeal period in an error proceeding. The Court concluded that such motions are not applicable in the error proceeding context because the district court is acting as an intermediate appellate court. *Id.* Appellant’s attempt to distinguish these cases by focusing solely on the plain text of § 25-1329 is unpersuasive. The Legislature is presumed to know the existing law and the distinctions between original and appellate proceedings. Had the Legislature intended § 25-1329 to apply to error proceedings, it could have explicitly said so. See *Ficke v. Ficke*, 290 Neb. internal (2016) (courts cannot read meaning into a statute that is not there).

Because § 25-1329 does not apply to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings, Appellant’s motion to vacate—filed pursuant to Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-2001—did not toll the time for appeal from the March 31, 2017 judgment. The notice of appeal, filed within 30 days of the May 25, 2017 order but more than 30 days after the March judgment, was untimely. See *Anderson v. Broling*, 258 Neb. 112, 602 N.W.2d 506 (1999) (notice of appeal must be filed within 30 days of a final judgment). Consequently, the Court of Appeals correctly concluded that it lacked jurisdiction to consider the merits of the March judgment.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court of Appeals did not err in dismissing Appellant’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The motion to vacate did not toll the appeal period because Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329 does not apply to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings.

**UPDATED PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on a petition for further review following the Court of Appeals’ dismissal of Appellant Dr. Robert McEwen’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The issue presented is whether Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329 (Reissue 2016), which governs motions to alter or amend a judgment and tolls the time for appeal, applies to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings. Specifically, we must determine if McEwen’s motion, filed pursuant to Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-2001 (Reissue 2016), operated to toll the appeal period from the district court’s March 31, 2017, judgment. After considering the rebuttals of both parties, we find that the Court of Appeals did not err in dismissing the appeal.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The question of whether an appellate court has jurisdiction is a matter of law, which requires this Court to reach a conclusion independent of the lower court’s decision. *State v. Kuehn*, 284 Neb. 598, 822 N.W.2d 437 (2012). Similarly, the interpretation of a statute is a question of law. *Id.*

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Motion Was Not a Motion to Alter or Amend Under § 25-1329**

McEwen argues that his motion should be treated as a motion to alter or amend under § 25-1329 because the district court addressed the merits of his request to vacate the judgment. However, the record shows that the motion filed on April 10, 2017, was explicitly styled as a "Motion for New Trial or, in the Alternative, for an Order Vacating the March 31, 2017 Judgment." The motion stated that it was "based on Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-2001." It did not cite § 25-1329.

The district court’s May 25, 2017, order analyzed the motion under the authority provided by § 25-2001. The court first determined that a motion for new trial was improper because no trial had been conducted. It then addressed the alternative request to vacate, analyzing whether the alleged factual error was determining. This analysis does not convert a motion filed under § 25-2001 into a motion to alter or amend under § 25-1329. As the Nebraska State College System (NSCS) correctly notes, the court’s engagement with the merits of a vacatur request does not validate the motion under a statute that was neither invoked by the movant nor applied by the court.

**B. § 25-1329 Does Not Apply to Petition in Error Proceedings**

McEwen contends that even if his motion was filed under § 25-2001, the plain language of § 25-1329 applies to "a judgment" without exception, and therefore it should have tolled the appeal period. We disagree.

While the plain language of a statute is the starting point for statutory construction, it must be interpreted in the context of the statutory scheme as a whole. *State v. Kuehn*, 284 Neb. 598, 822 N.W.2d 437 (2012). Section 25-1329 is located within the Nebraska Rules of Civil Procedure, which govern original civil actions. In contrast, petitions in error are governed by Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 20-1019 through 20-1029 (Reissue 2016). This distinction is significant because the district court, when reviewing a petition in error, acts as an intermediate appellate court reviewing an administrative record, not as a trial court conducting a trial.

McEwen attempts to distinguish *Goodman v. City of Omaha*, 289 Neb. 784, 857 N.W.2d 762 (2015), and *Timmerman v. Neth*, 285 Neb. 366, 826 N.W.2d 927 (2013), by arguing they addressed only motions for new trial under § 25-2001. This argument is unpersuasive. In *Timmerman*, this Court considered whether a motion to alter or amend a judgment would toll the appeal period in an error proceeding. The Court concluded that such motions are not applicable in the error proceeding context because the district court is acting as an intermediate appellate court. *Timmerman*, 285 Neb. at 374, 826 N.W.2d at 933. The background facts confirm that McEwen previously conceded, based on *Timmerman*, that his motion to vacate did not act as a motion to alter or amend the judgment.

McEwen’s reliance on *Capitol Construction v. Skinner*, 291 Neb. 85, 864 N.W.2d 220 (2015), is misplaced. *Capitol Construction* involved an original civil action, not a petition in error. The Court’s holding regarding tolling in that context does not override the specific statutory framework governing error proceedings. The Legislature is presumed to know the existing law and the distinctions between original and appellate proceedings. Had the Legislature intended § 25-1329 to apply to error proceedings, it could have explicitly incorporated it into the petition in error statutes. See *Ficke v. Ficke*, 290 Neb. 925, 862 N.W.2d 649 (2015) (courts cannot read meaning into a statute that is not there).

**C. The Notice of Appeal Was Untimely**

Because § 25-1329 does not apply to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings, and because McEwen’s motion was filed under § 25-2001, the motion did not toll the time for appeal from the March 31, 2017, judgment. The notice of appeal was filed within 30 days of the May 25, 2017, order, but more than 30 days after the March judgment. See *Anderson v. Broling*, 258 Neb. 112, 602 N.W.2d 506 (1999) (notice of appeal must be filed within 30 days of a final judgment). Consequently, the notice of appeal was untimely, and the Court of Appeals correctly concluded that it lacked jurisdiction to consider the merits of the March judgment.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on a petition for further review following the Court of Appeals’ dismissal of Appellant Dr. Robert McEwen’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The central issue is whether McEwen’s motion to vacate the district court’s judgment operated to toll the time for filing a notice of appeal. Specifically, we must determine whether Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329 (Reissue 2016), governing motions to alter or amend a judgment, applies to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings, or whether such proceedings are governed exclusively by the statutory scheme provided in Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 20-1019 to 20-1029 (Reissue 2016). After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties and the applicable law, we find that the Court of Appeals did not err in dismissing the appeal.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The question of whether an appellate court has jurisdiction is a matter of law, which requires this Court to reach a conclusion independent of the lower court’s decision. *State v. Kuehn*, 284 Neb. 598, 822 N.W.2d 437 (2012). Similarly, the interpretation of a statute is a question of law. *Id.*

**III. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Distinction Between Original Civil Actions and Petitions in Error**

McEwen argues that the plain language of Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329, which applies to "any judgment," governs post-judgment motions in petition in error proceedings just as it does in original civil actions. He relies on *Capitol Construction v. Skinner*, 291 Neb. 85, 864 N.W.2d 220 (2015), to support the proposition that a motion challenging the correctness of a judgment tolls the appeal period. However, this argument ignores the fundamental structural differences between original civil actions and petitions in error.

As the Nebraska State College System (NSCS) correctly points out, petitions in error are governed by a specific statutory scheme, Neb. Rev. Stat. §§ 20-1019 to 20-1029, which is distinct from the Nebraska Rules of Civil Procedure found in Chapter 25. When a district court reviews a petition in error, it acts as an intermediate appellate court reviewing an administrative record, not as a trial court of original jurisdiction. *Timmerman v. Neth*, 285 Neb. 366, 826 N.W.2d 927 (2013). Because the court’s role is appellate in nature, the procedural mechanisms designed for trial courts—such as motions for new trial or motions to alter or amend a judgment based on the court's own factual findings—are generally inapplicable.

**B. Applicability of § 25-1329 and *Timmerman***

McEwen attempts to distinguish *Timmerman v. Neth*, 285 Neb. 366, 826 N.W.2d 927 (2013), and *Goodman v. City of Omaha*, 289 Neb. 784, 857 N.W.2d 762 (2015), by arguing they addressed only motions for new trial under Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-2001. This distinction is unpersuasive. In *Timmerman*, this Court explicitly considered whether motions to alter or amend a judgment would toll the appeal period in an error proceeding. The Court concluded that such motions are not applicable in that context because the district court is acting as an intermediate appellate court. *Timmerman*, 285 Neb. at 374, 826 N.W.2d at 933.

McEwen’s reliance on *Capitol Construction* is misplaced because that case involved an original civil action where the district court exercised original jurisdiction. The holding in *Capitol Construction* does not override the specific statutory framework governing error proceedings. The Legislature is presumed to know the existing law and the distinctions between original and appellate proceedings. Had the Legislature intended § 25-1329 to apply to error proceedings, it could have explicitly incorporated it into the petition in error statutes. See *Ficke v. Ficke*, 290 Neb. 925, 862 N.W.2d 649 (2015) (courts cannot read meaning into a statute that is not there).

Furthermore, the record reflects that McEwen himself conceded, based on *Timmerman*, that his motion to vacate did not act as a motion to alter or amend the judgment. This concession, made at oral argument and acknowledged in the Court of Appeals proceedings, is binding. He cannot now resurrect an argument that he has previously abandoned.

**C. Substance Over Labels and the Statutory Basis of the Motion**

McEwen contends that even if his motion was styled under § 25-2001, its substance—challenging a factual error the district court admitted to—constitutes a motion to alter or amend under § 25-1329. We disagree. The motion filed on April 10, 2017, was explicitly titled "Motion for New Trial or, in the Alternative, for an Order Vacating the March 31, 2017 Judgment" and stated that it was "based on Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-2001."

While the district court analyzed whether the factual error was "determining," it did so in the context of ruling on a motion to vacate filed under § 25-2001. The court’s engagement with the merits of the vacatur request does not retroactively transform the motion into one filed under § 25-1329. The nature of a motion is determined by its statutory basis and the procedural posture of the case, not by the court’s analysis of the merits. *Timmerman*, 285 Neb. at 374, 826 N.W.2d at 933.

**D. Timeliness of the Notice of Appeal**

Because § 25-1329 does not apply to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings, and because McEwen’s motion was filed under § 25-2001, the motion did not toll the time for appeal from the March 31, 2017, judgment. Under Nebraska law, a notice of appeal must be filed within 30 days of a final judgment. *Anderson v. Broling*, 258 Neb. 112, 602 N.W.2d 506 (1999). McEwen’s notice of appeal was filed within 30 days of the May 25, 2017, order, but more than 30 days after the March judgment. Consequently, the notice of appeal was untimely as to the March judgment.

The Court of Appeals correctly noted that McEwen’s brief on appeal argued only that the district court erred by denying his petition in error in the March judgment. He did not assign error to the May order denying his motion to vacate. Therefore, the Court of Appeals lacked jurisdiction to consider the merits of the March judgment and correctly dismissed the appeal.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we find that the Court of Appeals did not err in dismissing Appellant’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction. The motion to vacate did not toll the appeal period because Neb. Rev. Stat. § 25-1329 does not apply to judgments entered in petition in error proceedings, and the motion filed was not a motion to alter or amend under that statute.

**ORDERED** that the judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed.
