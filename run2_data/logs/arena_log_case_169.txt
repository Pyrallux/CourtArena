Your Honor, the trial court correctly applied the Survival Statute to preserve Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One. The court’s ruling was sound because PBS One’s contractual liability under the Consulting Agreement unequivocally existed prior to its dissolution, and the Survival Statute preserves such obligations regardless of when the breach occurred.  

Illinois law is clear: dissolution of a corporation does not terminate its pre-existing contractual obligations. As the First District held in *In re Marriage of Caffrey*, 272 Ill. App. 3d 1087, 1092 (1st Dist. 1995), “the dissolution of a corporation does not terminate its contractual obligations.” This principle is rooted in the Survival Statute, which ensures that obligations incurred before dissolution remain enforceable. Critically, the statute protects claims based on liabilities that accrued *prior to dissolution*, even if the breach itself manifests later.  

Here, PBS One expressly assumed the Consulting Agreement’s obligations in 1988 through two binding agreements. The Asset Purchase Agreement specifically identified the Consulting Agreement as a “material contract” and required PBS One to assume “all liabilities” of Pielet Corp., including those under the Consulting Agreement. The subsequent Assignment Agreement further confirmed PBS One’s assumption of these obligations, stating it “accept[ed] the foregoing assignment of the Assumed Liabilities and from and after the date hereof, assume[d] and agree[d] to perform all of the covenants, agreements and obligations” under the agreement. Crucially, the Consulting Agreement itself contained a “successors and assigns” clause, binding all parties—including PBS One—to its terms indefinitely.  

At the time of dissolution, PBS One held an active, existing liability under the Consulting Agreement. As the court explained in *In re Estate of Sweeney*, 352 Ill. App. 3d 107, 112 (2d Dist. 2004), the Survival Statute applies to “obligations that existed at the time of dissolution,” not merely to causes of action that accrued before dissolution. PBS One’s duty to pay the Consulting Agreement’s fees was a continuous obligation that arose in 1988 and persisted through dissolution. The cessation of payments in 1998 constituted a breach of this pre-existing duty—a breach that flows directly from the liability PBS One assumed years earlier.  

The 1991 assignment of PBS One’s partnership interest to National Material further underscores this point. That transaction explicitly included “obligations” under the Consulting Agreement, confirming that PBS One’s liability was recognized as ongoing at the time of dissolution. When National Material later assumed these same obligations, it did so with full knowledge that the Consulting Agreement’s terms remained in force.  

Plaintiff’s claim is not about a new obligation created after dissolution—it is about enforcing a duty that PBS One voluntarily assumed in 1988 and carried through its dissolution. As *Caffrey* and *Sweeney* make plain, the Survival Statute exists precisely to prevent corporations from evading pre-dissolution liabilities through dissolution. To hold otherwise would reward PBS One for dissolving while still owing contractual duties, undermining the statute’s purpose.  

For these reasons, the trial court did not err. PBS One’s liability under the Consulting Agreement survived dissolution, and Plaintiff is entitled to pursue his claim.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court erred in denying our cross-motion for summary judgment and that the plaintiff has failed to establish liability under Counts IX, X, and XI. The plaintiff's arguments mischaracterize the contractual relationships, misapply the Survival Statute, and ignore the undisputed evidence demonstrating that PBS One neither remained obligated after dissolution nor had any capacity to incur new liabilities. Additionally, National Material's liability is foreclosed by the express terms of the 1991 transaction, which did not create the successor relationship the plaintiff asserts.

## I. Count XI: PBS One's Liability Did Not Survive Dissolution

The plaintiff contends that the Survival Statute preserves PBS One's liability under the Consulting Agreement because that obligation existed prior to dissolution. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

### A. The Survival Statute Does Not Create New Liabilities

The Survival Statute, 755 ILCS 5/2-14, provides that upon dissolution, a corporation's "rights and liabilities" survive to enable creditors to pursue claims. However, the statute preserves *existing causes of action*—it does not create new obligations or extend liability to breaches that occur after the corporate entity has been lawfully terminated. As the First District explained in *In re Marriage of Caffrey*, 272 Ill. App. 3d 1087, 1092 (1st Dist. 1995), dissolution does not terminate "contractual obligations" that accrued during the corporation's active existence.

The critical distinction is between a claim that *accrued* before dissolution and a breach that *occurs* afterward. Here, the plaintiff does not allege that PBS One breached the Consulting Agreement prior to June 1, 1994. To the contrary, the undisputed facts establish that PBS One made no direct payments under the Consulting Agreement; rather, Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics) performed the payment obligations from 1988 onward. The cessation of payments in July 1998—four years after PBS One's dissolution—cannot be characterized as the breach of an obligation that "existed" at the time of dissolution in any meaningful sense.

In *In re Estate of Sweeney*, 352 Ill. App. 3d 107, 112 (2d Dist. 2004), the court applied the Survival Statute to "obligations that existed at the time of dissolution," but the obligation in that case was a fixed, liquidated debt that had fully accrued before dissolution. Here, by contrast, the Consulting Agreement contemplated ongoing, future payments contingent upon Arthur's continued life. PBS One's "obligation" was merely a contingent, executory promise—not a matured claim. The Sweeney court did not address, nor does Illinois law support, the proposition that a dissolved corporation remains liable for breaches that occur years after its legal termination.

### B. PBS One Transferred All Assets and Liabilities Before Dissolution

The undisputed facts demonstrate that PBS One ceased all business operations and transferred its entire partnership interest to National Material in January 1991—three years before its formal dissolution. Tang's deposition testimony establishes that PBS One "has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything. Just an investment." When PBS One was dissolved in 1994, it was an empty shell with no assets, no ongoing business, and no capacity to incur new liabilities.

The plaintiff cannot circumvent this reality by invoking the Survival Statute. The statute is designed to prevent corporations from evading *creditor claims* through dissolution—not to impose post-dissolution liability on entities that have already lawfully transferred their obligations to successors. Once PBS One assigned its partnership interest (including all associated rights and obligations) to National Material in 1991, PBS One had no further liability to perform under the Consulting Agreement. The subsequent dissolution in 1994 merely formalized an entity that had already ceased to function.

### C. The 1991 Assignment Expressly Transferred Obligations

The December 31, 1990 Assumption Agreement between PBS One and National Material is controlling. Under the "Assignment" section, PBS One assigned "one hundred percent (100%) of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in [Pielet LP]" to National Material. Under the "Assumption" section, National Material "accept[ed] and assume[d] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest."

This language demonstrates that PBS One's obligations under the Consulting Agreement were explicitly transferred to National Material before dissolution. The plaintiff acknowledges this in his own pleading, stating that National Material "expressly assumed" PBS One's obligations. Once this transfer was effected, PBS One no longer retained any obligation to perform under the Consulting Agreement. Its subsequent dissolution was the lawful conclusion of an entity that had already assigned its liabilities.

The plaintiff cannot credibly argue that PBS One remained liable after transferring the very partnership interest that generated the obligation. As the court stated in *Caffrey*, the dissolution of a corporation does not terminate *contractual obligations*—but here, PBS One had already transferred, assigned, and been relieved of those obligations years before dissolution.

### D. No Novation Was Required Because No New Obligation Was Created

The plaintiff suggests that Arthur's rights against PBS One were somehow preserved despite the restructuring. The defense notes that Arthur's attorney, Michael Zavis, expressly testified that no novation was intended, no release was sought, and Arthur was merely "informed" of the transaction for "information purposes." Zavis further testified: "I didn't have any intention of removing them [Pielet Corp.], but I couldn't remove them anyhow unless they agreed."

This testimony establishes that Arthur was aware of the restructuring and did not object, but importantly, no party sought or obtained a novation. The plaintiff cannot impose liability on PBS One for post-dissolution breaches when PBS One had already assigned its obligations and ceased to exist as a legal entity. The Consulting Agreement's "successors and assigns" clause bound the parties' successors—but PBS One's successor was National Material, not PBS One in dissolution.

## II. Count IX: National Material Did Not Assume Direct Liability

### A. The Survival Statute Preserves Liabilities Held at Dissolution, Not Liabilities Previously Transferred

The statute provides that dissolution "does not revoke any change [sic] to affect the disposition of property" but goes on to preserve "all rights and liabilities" for creditor purposes. *In re Estate of Sweeney*, 352 Ill. The critical inquiry is not merely whether an obligation "existed" at some prior time, but whether the dissolved corporation *held* that liability at the moment of dissolution. As this court recognized in its preliminary ruling, the statute "protects creditors from having their claims extinguished by dissolution; it does not create liability for a dissolved entity that had previously transferred its obligations."

The plaintiff cites *In re Marriage of Caffrey*, 272 Ill. App. 3d 1087, 1092 (1st Dist. 1995), for the proposition that dissolution does not terminate contractual obligations. The undisputed facts establish that PBS One transferred "everything" to National Material in January 1991. ## III. Count X: Successor Liability Does Not Apply

The plaintiff invokes the "mere continuation" doctrine to hold National Material liable as PBS One's successor. This argument fails as a matter of law.

### A. The Mere Continuation Doctrine Requires Specific Factual Elements

In *Travis v. Whiter*, 289 Ill. App. 3d 234, 238 (1st Dist. 1997), this court identified three circumstances under which successor liability arises: (1) express assumption of liability; (2) de facto merger or consolidation; and (3) mere continuation. The "mere continuation" doctrine applies where "a corporation is created to take over the assets of another" often "for the purpose of evading liability." Id.

The doctrine requires more than mere continuity of ownership or control. It requires that a new entity be created specifically to take over the assets of the old entity, typically as a device to evade liabilities. This is not the case here.

### B. National Material Was Not Created to Take Over PBS One's Assets

The undisputed facts establish that National Material was an existing entity in 1991 when it acquired PBS One's partnership interest. It was not newly created for this purpose. The plaintiff places great weight on the 1993 Partnership Agreement amendment designating National Material as "successor" to PBS One. However, the legal doctrine of "successor liability" is not created by self-serving designations in partnership documents. The doctrine requires a factual finding that the requirements of *Travis* are met—specifically, that the transaction constituted a de facto merger, consolidation, or that the new entity was created to continue the old entity's business while evading its liabilities.

**A. Count XI: Survival Statute and PBS One’s Liability**

The Defense argues that PBS One’s liability was transferred to National Material in 1991 and therefore did not exist at the time of PBS One’s dissolution in 1994. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive in light of the Prosecution’s rebuttal regarding the necessity of a novation.

The Survival Statute, 755 ILCS 5/2-14, preserves "all rights and liabilities" of a dissolved corporation for the purpose of enabling creditors to pursue claims. *In re Estate of Sweeney*, 352 Ill. App. 3d 107, 112 (2d Dist. 2004), holds that the statute preserves obligations that existed at the time of dissolution, even if the breach occurs later. The Defense contends that because PBS One transferred its interest in 1991, it held no liability at dissolution. However, this position ignores the fundamental principle of contract law cited by the Prosecution: an assignment of contractual duties does not release the assignor from liability to the obligee without the obligee's consent.

The undisputed facts indicate that PBS One transferred its partnership interest to National Material in 1991. While the 1991 Assumption Agreement states that National Material assumed PBS One's obligations "in and to the extent of" that interest, there is no evidence that Arthur, the creditor, consented to a substitution of parties that would release PBS One. Attorney Michael Zavis testified that Arthur never released PBS One from liability and that no novation was sought. *In re Marriage of Caffrey*, 272 Ill. App. 3d 1087, 1092 (1st Dist. 1995), confirms that dissolution does not terminate contractual obligations that accrued during the corporation’s existence. Because PBS One was not released from its obligations under the Consulting Agreement prior to its dissolution, the liability remained with PBS One in 1994. Consequently, the Survival Statute preserves the Plaintiff’s claim against PBS One for the breach that occurred in 1998.

**B. Count IX: National Material’s Assumption of Liability**

The Court affirms its preliminary finding that National Material is not directly liable under the Consulting Agreement. The Prosecution argues that National Material assumed "everything," but the Defense correctly points to the limiting language in the governing contract.

The December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement explicitly states that National Material "accept[ed] and assume[d] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." The phrase "in and to the extent of" is a clear limitation on the scope of the assumption. It restricts National Material’s liability to those obligations inherent in or directly related to the partnership interest itself. The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the business entity, Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics), which made the payments, rather than a direct obligation of the limited partner’s equity interest. The Court also affirms its preliminary finding that National Material is not liable as a successor to PBS One. This matter is before the Court on cross-motions for summary judgment regarding Counts IX, X, and XI. The Court has carefully reviewed the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, the undisputed facts of record, and the applicable Illinois law. The central issue on appeal is whether the transfer of assets and partnership interests effectively discharged the obligations of the original parties under the Consulting Agreement, or whether those obligations survived subsequent corporate restructuring and dissolution.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Count XI: Survival Statute and PBS One’s Liability**

The Defense argues that PBS One’s liability was extinguished by the 1991 transfer to National Material and therefore did not exist at the time of PBS One’s dissolution in 1994. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive as it misstates the effect of an assignment under Illinois contract law.

The Defense contends that the 1991 transaction was a valid assignment that transferred PBS One’s obligations to National Material, and that because no novation was required, PBS One was released from liability. This reasoning is flawed. Under Illinois law, an assignment of contractual duties transfers the obligation to the assignee, but it does not discharge the assignor from liability to the obligee absent the obligee's consent to a novation. *In re Marriage of Caffrey*, 272 Ill. App. 3d 1087, 1092 (1st Dist. 1995), holds that dissolution does not terminate contractual obligations that accrued during the corporation’s existence. The Survival Statute, 755 ILCS 5/2-14, preserves "all rights and liabilities" of a dissolved corporation to enable creditors to pursue claims.

The undisputed facts reveal that Arthur, the obligee, never consented to release PBS One from its obligations. Attorney Michael Zavis testified that Arthur "never gave up any of his rights against the original debtor" and that no release was sought. The 1991 Assumption Agreement between PBS One and National Material was an inter-party transfer that did not bind Arthur. Because PBS One was not released from its obligations prior to dissolution, the liability remained with PBS One in 1994. The fact that the breach occurred in 1998 does not negate this liability; *In re Estate of Sweeney*, 352 Ill. App. 3d 107, 112 (2d Dist. 2004), confirms that the Survival Statute preserves obligations that existed at the time of dissolution, even if the breach occurs later. Consequently, PBS One remains liable for the breach of the Consulting Agreement.

**B. Count IX: National Material’s Assumption of Liability**

The Court affirms its finding that National Material is not directly liable under the Consulting Agreement. The Prosecution argues that National Material assumed "everything," but the plain language of the governing contract limits the scope of the assumption.

The December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement states that National Material "accept[ed] and assume[d] [PBS One's] right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." The phrase "in and to the extent of" is a clear limitation on the scope of the assumption. It restricts National Material’s liability to those obligations inherent in or directly related to the partnership interest itself. The Consulting Agreement was an obligation of the business entity, Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics), which made the payments, rather than a direct obligation of the limited partner’s equity interest. The Prosecution has not provided evidence that National Material expressly agreed to assume the specific personal debt owed to Arthur under the Consulting Agreement, as opposed to the obligations associated with the partnership interest. Therefore, the Defense’s motion for summary judgment on Count IX is proper.

**C. Count X: National Material as Successor**

The Court also affirms its finding that National Material is not liable as a successor to PBS One. The Prosecution asserts that the transaction qualifies as a "mere continuation" under *Travis v. Whiter*, 289 Ill. App. 3d 234, 238 (1st Dist. 1997). However, the facts do not support this doctrine.

The "mere continuation" doctrine typically applies where a new entity is created to take over the assets of another, often to evade liabilities. Here, National Material was an existing entity that purchased PBS One’s partnership interest in 1991. PBS One continued to exist for three years after the transfer. The fact that Tang controlled both entities, or that the 1993 Partnership Amendment designated National Material as a "successor" for partnership purposes, does not establish successor liability to third-party creditors under Illinois law. Common ownership and control alone are insufficient to trigger successor liability. *Travis v. Whiter*, 289 Ill. App. 3d at 238. As there was no merger or consolidation, and National Material was not a new entity created to continue PBS One’s business, the requirements for successor liability are not met.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that PBS One remains liable for the breach of the Consulting Agreement pursuant to the Survival Statute, as its liability was not extinguished by the 1991 transfer due to the lack of a novation. However, National Material’s liability is limited to the obligations expressly assumed in the 1991 agreement, which do not encompass the direct contractual duties to Arthur, and National Material is not a successor liable for PBS One’s obligations.

**RULING**

The Defense’s motion for summary judgment is **DENIED** on Count XI and **GRANTED** on Counts IX and X. The Plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment is **GRANTED** on Count XI and **DENIED** on Counts IX and X.
