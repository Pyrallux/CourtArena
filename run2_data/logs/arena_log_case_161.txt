As the Supreme Court explained in *United States v. ## I. INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals erred in affirming the trial court's denial of the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. ## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Applicable Standard Under Roviaro

The prosecution correctly cites *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), for the proposition that the government's privilege to withhold an informant's identity may be overcome when disclosure is "relevant and helpful to the defense or essential to a fair determination of the cause." However, the prosecution misapplies this standard by arguing that the defendant must demonstrate a "particularized need" exceeding the *Roviaro* standard. The *Roviaro* Court explicitly held that no "fixed rule" governs disclosure and that the informer's privilege must "give way" when it "would further the fair administration of justice." *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. at 61.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. McLain*, 337 S.W.3d 268 (Tex. Crim. App. 2011), is misplaced. In *McLain*, the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals applied the *Roviaro* framework but recognized that disclosure may be required when the informant's testimony would be "relevant and helpful to the defense." Here, the defense did not merely speculate about the informant's relevance; it specifically argued that the informant was present during the transaction, that her belongings were found in the defendant's car, and that she may have "set him up." These are not "mere speculation" but rather specific factual allegations going to the heart of the reliability of the tip and the validity of the search.

### B. The Informant's Identity Was Relevant to the Probable Cause Determination

The prosecution argues that the suppression hearing focused solely on whether evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment and that the informant's reliability was adequately established through testimony. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the defense specifically argued that the informant's identity was necessary to challenge "the reliability in regard to the probable cause." The trial judge acknowledged this argument, responding, "I am not sure of that because I have heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." However, the defense was entitled to test the accuracy of this testimony by learning the informant's identity. As the Supreme Court explained in *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971), an informant's tip may support probable cause when corroborated, but the defense must have the opportunity to challenge the informant's credibility. The informant's identity is often essential to this challenge.

Second, the defense presented "new information" regarding the confidential informant that was not available at the initial hearing. This new information—specifically, that the informant was present with the appellant and that her belongings were found in his car—directly bearing on her reliability and potential bias. The defense was entitled to have this information considered in connection with its request for disclosure.

Third, the prosecution's argument that the informant's reliability was "adequately established" conflates the question of whether probable cause existed with the defendant's right to challenge that probable cause. Even if the trial court believed the informant was reliable based on past tips, the defense had the right to investigate whether this particular tip was reliable, whether the informant had a motive to fabricate or exaggerate, and whether the informant was properly credited. These challenges require disclosure of the informant's identity.

### C. The Defense Was Entitled to Investigate Potential Entrapment and Frame-Up

The prosecution dismisses the defense's entrapment argument as irrelevant to a suppression hearing. This characterization is incorrect. While the prosecution is correct that "a suppression hearing is not a trial on the merits," *State v. Kelly*, 204 S.W.3d 808, 815 (Tex. Crim. App. 2006), the defense was not seeking to litigate entrapment at the suppression hearing. Rather, it sought disclosure of the informant's identity to investigate whether the informant had induced the appellant to commit the offense, which would bear on the informant's reliability and the validity of the search.

The defense cited *Bodin v. State* for the proposition that the identity of a confidential informant must be disclosed when the informant's testimony may be necessary to a fair determination of guilt. While the prosecution argues this case is distinguishable because it involved a trial on the merits, the principle underlying *Bodin*—that the informant's identity may be essential to the defense—applies with equal force to a suppression hearing where the defense is challenging the reliability of the informant's tip.

The defense also cited *Holmes v. South Carolina*, which stands for the proposition that a defendant is entitled to put on a defense. Here, the defense was prevented from investigating whether the confidential informant had framed or set up the appellant because the trial court denied disclosure. This denial violated the defendant's constitutional right to present a complete defense.

### D. The "Dispositive Motion" Agreement Did Not Waive the Right to Disclosure

The prosecution suggests that because the motion to suppress was "dispositive," the defendant waived his right to disclosure of the confidential informant. This argument is without merit.

The record reflects that the agreement regarding the dispositive motion concerned the consequences of the trial court's ruling on the suppression motion—not the scope of issues the court could consider. The prosecutor explained that "if he rules in our favor, the defendant pleads; and if he rules not in the State's favor, that we dismiss the case." This agreement did not preclude the defense from seeking disclosure of the informant's identity; it merely established the consequences of the ruling.

Moreover, defense counsel specifically stated that she was "re-urging the motion" for disclosure based on "new information" received "yesterday." The defense did not waive its right to disclosure by agreeing to proceed on a dispositive motion; it simply reserved the right to seek disclosure if the court ruled against it on the suppression motion.

## IV. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's argument rests on the premise that the defendant failed to demonstrate a "particularized need" for the informant's identity. This premise is wrong. The defense demonstrated that the informant was present with the appellant, that her belongings were found in his car, and that she may have "set him up." These facts establish a particularized need for disclosure because they suggest the informant may have been involved in the crime and may have a motive to fabricate or exaggerate the evidence against the appellant.

The prosecution argues that the informant's reliability was established through testimony about her past tips. However, this testimony did not address whether this particular tip was reliable, whether the informant had any bias or motive to lie, or whether the informant was present at the scene of the crime. The defense was entitled to investigate these questions, which required disclosure of the informant's identity.

The prosecution argues that entrapment and similar defenses are irrelevant to a suppression hearing. This argument mischaracterizes the defense's position. The defense was not seeking to litigate entrapment at the suppression hearing; it was seeking disclosure of the informant's identity to investigate whether the informant had induced the appellant to commit the offense. This investigation is relevant to the reliability of the informant's tip and, consequently, to the validity of the search.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the Court of Appeals' decision and remand the case for further proceedings. The trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity because the defendant demonstrated that the informant's identity was relevant and essential to his defense. The defense is entitled to investigate whether the confidential informant "set him up," whether her tip was reliable, and whether the search was supported by probable cause. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

The Court affirms the decision of the trial court to deny the motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant. The Appellant has failed to demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion in withholding the informant's identity. The defense arguments, while highlighting the presence of the informant and the discovery of her belongings, do not establish the "particularized need" required to override the informer's privilege in the context of a suppression hearing. Furthermore, the agreed-upon "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress limited the scope of the hearing to the legality of the search, precluding the relitigation of guilt or innocence issues such as entrapment.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard for Disclosure of a Confidential Informant**

The privilege of the government to withhold the identity of a confidential informant is well-established. This privilege is not absolute, however, and must yield when the disclosure is "relevant and helpful to the defense or essential to a fair determination of the cause." *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 59–60 (1957). In the context of a motion to suppress evidence, the defendant must demonstrate a "particularized need" for the informant's identity to challenge the legality of the search or the existence of probable cause. *State v. McLain*, 337 S.W.3d 268, 275 (Tex. Crim. App. 2011). Mere speculation that the informant might provide favorable testimony is insufficient to meet this burden. *State v. McLain*, 337 S.W.3d at 275.

**II. The Defense Failed to Show a Particularized Need**

The Appellant argues that the "new information" regarding the confidential informant—specifically, that she was present during the transaction and that her belongings were found in the Appellant's car—establishes a particularized need for disclosure. The defense contends that this information suggests the informant may have "set him up," thereby impacting her reliability and the probable cause for the search.

While these facts are relevant to the defense's theory of entrapment or a "frame-up," they do not necessarily bear on the specific inquiry of the suppression hearing: whether the police had probable cause to believe the Appellant possessed cocaine at the time of the search. The reliability of an informant's tip is generally assessed under the totality-of-the-circumstances test, which includes consideration of the informant's veracity, reliability, and basis of knowledge, as well as the extent of police corroboration. *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583 (1971).

Here, the trial court heard testimony that the informant had provided reliable and accurate tips in the past. The Appellant's assertion that the informant "set him up" does not directly refute the accuracy of the information provided to the police regarding the possession of contraband. Even if the informant was present and induced the Appellant, this goes to the defense of entrapment rather than the validity of the probable cause supporting the search warrant or arrest. The defense has not shown how knowing the informant's identity would specifically disprove the facts supporting probable cause, other than through speculative allegations of bias. Under *State v. McLain*, 337 S.W.3d at 275, such speculation is insufficient to compel disclosure.

**III. Scope of the Dispositive Motion to Suppress**

The procedural posture of this case further supports the denial of the motion to disclose. The parties agreed that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive." This agreement limited the issues before the court to the Fourth Amendment question of whether the evidence was obtained illegally. As noted in *State v. Kelly*, 204 S.W.3d 808, 815 (Tex. Crim. App. 2006), "a suppression hearing is not a trial on the merits; it is limited to determining whether law enforcement acted constitutionally."

The defense attempts to circumvent this limitation by arguing that the informant's potential involvement in a "setup" renders her identity relevant to the probable cause determination. However, the trial court correctly identified that the defense's arguments regarding the informant's presence and belongings "go[ing] to a critical defense of Mr. Bland’s case" are matters of guilt or innocence. By agreeing to a dispositive motion to suppress, the defense effectively waived the right to litigate these factual disputes at that stage. The prosecution correctly notes that if the defense wished to attack the credibility of the informant regarding entrapment or a frame-up, the appropriate forum was a trial on the merits, not the limited hearing on the motion to suppress.

**IV. Conclusion**

The trial court acted within its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The Appellant failed to meet the high burden of showing that the informant's identity was "essential to a fair determination" of the suppression issue. *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. at 59–60. The "new information" provided by the defense does not establish a particularized need to challenge probable cause, but rather raises issues of entrapment and credibility that are outside the scope of a dispositive suppression hearing. *State v. McLain*, 337 S.W.3d at 275; *State v. Kelly*, 204 S.W.3d at 815. Therefore, the ruling of the court of appeals is affirmed.

## II. THE DEFENSE ESTABLISHED A PARTICULARIZED NEED FOR DISCLOSURE

The prosecution argues that the defense failed to demonstrate a particularized need because it offered only "speculation" about the informant's role. This characterization ignores the specific facts presented and misapplies *State v. McLain*, 337 S.W.3d 268 (Tex. Crim. App. 2011).

Under *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 59–60 (1957), disclosure is required when the informant's identity "is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or essential to a fair determination of the cause." The Supreme Court emphasized that the privilege "has limited application" and must yield when the informant's testimony "would be relevant and helpful to the defense." *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 60–61.

The prosecution erroneously argues that the informant's presence and belongings are irrelevant to probable cause because "the focus is on whether the tip's content was corroborated." The defense below specifically argued that the informant was "with appellant that day" and that her belongings were found in his car. When an informant participates in the crime, disclosure is more likely warranted because the informant's identity may reveal bias, motive to fabricate, or alternative explanations for the officers' observations.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. The prosecution argues that "challenges to the informant's credibility based on entrapment, bias, or 'set up' allegations go to the merits of guilt or innocence—not the legality of the search." This argument misapprehends the nature of the probable cause inquiry.

The existence of probable cause depends on whether the informant's tip was reliable. *Gates*, 462 U.S. at 230. If the informant "set up" the defendant—meaning she fabricated the tip or induced the offense—then the tip lacks reliability, and any probable cause based on that tip is defective. This is not a "guilt or innocence" argument; it is a direct challenge to the constitutional foundation of the search.

The prosecution cites *State v. Kelly*, 204 S.W.3d 808, 815 (Tex. Crim. App. 2006), for the proposition that "a suppression hearing is not a trial on the merits." However, *Kelly* does not hold that a defendant cannot challenge the reliability of an informant at a suppression hearing. To the contrary, *Kelly* confirms that suppression hearings determine "whether law enforcement acted constitutionally"—which necessarily includes assessing whether probable cause existed. If the informant's tip was unreliable because she "set up" the defendant, the officers did not act constitutionally in relying on that tip.

Here, the defense is not seeking to introduce evidence at trial—it is seeking to obtain information necessary to challenge the constitutional basis for the search.
