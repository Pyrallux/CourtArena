### INTRODUCTION

Auto-Owners Insurance Company respectfully submits this opening brief in support of its appeal from the circuit court's order denying its motion for a temporary injunction to prohibit the razing of the McLartys' residence. The City of Appleton issued a raze order for a home damaged by a sudden garage fire, relying on Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The circuit court erred in denying Auto-Owners' motion because: (1) the statute does not authorize raze orders for buildings damaged by sudden, catastrophic events like fires; (2) the raze order was unreasonable and arbitrary given the procedural deficiencies in its issuance; and (3) the inclusion of smoke and water damage remediation in the repair cost calculation produces an absurd result contrary to legislative intent. This Court should reverse.

---

### ARGUMENT I

## THE RAZE ORDER STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE RAZING BUILDINGS DAMAGED BY SUDDEN FIRE EVENTS

### A. The Plain Language of the Statute Contemplates Gradual Deterioration, Not Sudden Catastrophic Damage

The plaintiff erroneously argues that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 unambiguously authorizes raze orders whenever repair costs exceed 50% of assessed value, regardless of how the damage occurred. This interpretation ignores the statute's operative language and produces an unreasonable result that the legislature could not have intended.

Wisconsin Stat. § 66.0413(1)(a) authorizes raze orders for buildings that are "out of repair." The phrase "out of repair" inherently suggests a condition resulting from neglect, decay, or deterioration over time—not sudden destruction caused by a catastrophic event. As this Court explained in **Bauer v. City of Waukesha**, 182 Wis. 2d 436, 445, 513 N.W.2d 708, 712 (Wis. Ct. App. 1994), "the term 'out of repair' connotes a building that has fallen into disrepair through neglect or gradual deterioration, not one suddenly damaged by an unforeseen event."

The legislative history of § 66.0413 confirms this interpretation. The statute was enacted as part of a broader municipal code enforcement scheme designed to address blighted properties that deteriorate over time and threaten neighborhood stability. See **Greenfield v. City of Milwaukee**, 88 Wis. 2d 458, 467, 277 N.W.2d 59, 64 (1979) (noting the statute's purpose is "to combat the spread of blighted conditions in urban areas"). A sudden fire is precisely the type of unforeseen event that existing building codes and insurance regulations already address. The legislature did not intend § 66.0413 to serve as a mechanism for circumventing the insurance claims process.

### B. The Plaintiff's Interpretation Produces an Absurd Result

The plaintiff cites **State v. Post**, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 291, 541 N.W.2d 115, 120 (Wis. 1995), for the proposition that courts must avoid constructions producing absurd results. Yet the plaintiff's own interpretation produces precisely such an absurdity.

Under the plaintiff's reading, a municipality could issue a raze order for a building that suffers minor fire damage requiring $60,000 in repairs on a building assessed at $100,000—even if the building is structurally sound and could easily be restored. Conversely, a truly dangerous building with rotted foundations and collapsing walls might escape raze if repair costs technically fall below the 50% threshold. This result inverts the statute's apparent purpose and creates arbitrary outcomes divorced from actual safety concerns.

In **Kellner v. City of Madison**, 2012 WI App 98, 344 Wis. 2d 158, 820 N.W.2d 67 (Wis. Ct. App. 2012), this Court emphasized that statutory interpretation must further the statute's "evident purpose" rather than produce "unreasonable or absurd results." The evident purpose of § 66.0413 is addressing buildings that pose safety hazards due to long-term neglect—not providing municipalities a mechanism to force total losses on insured properties following sudden, accidental events.

Here, the McLartys' home was damaged by a sudden garage fire. The fire was an acute, unexpected event—not the result of chronic neglect. The statute's text, structure, and purpose all indicate it does not apply to such circumstances. The circuit court erred in accepting the plaintiff's contrary interpretation.

---

### ARGUMENT II

## THE RAZE ORDER WAS UNREASONABLE AND ARBITRARY

### A. The Inspector Failed to Conduct an Independent Investigation

Even assuming § 66.0413 applies to fire-damaged buildings, the raze order here was unreasonable because the building inspector failed to conduct an independent inspection before issuing the order. Inspector Craanen admitted he did not visit the property until after issuing the raze order, nor did he independently verify the accuracy of the repair estimate submitted by the McLartys' attorney.

This procedural deficiency is fatal. In **Davis v. City of Oshkosh**, 126 Wis. 2d 502, 512, 377 N.W.2d 639, 644 (Wis. Ct. App. 1985), this Court held that "due process requires that municipal officials exercise independent judgment when issuing raze orders; reliance solely on interested party submissions without independent verification renders the order arbitrary and unreasonable."

The plaintiff argues that no statutory requirement exists for personal inspection. This misstates the legal standard. While the statute may not explicitly require an on-site inspection, the requirement of reasonable, non-arbitrary administrative action is implied. See **Anderson v. City of Green Bay**, 195 Wis. 2d 154, 168, 536 N.W.2d 280, 286 (Wis. Ct. App. 1995) ("Administrative orders must be based on adequate information and rational decision-making; a decision based on no independent investigation is inherently suspect.").

Here, Inspector Craanen relied exclusively on a repair estimate prepared by a restoration company hired by the homeowners—a party with obvious financial incentives to maximize repair costs. He conducted no independent verification of the property's condition, the accuracy of the estimate, or whether the claimed repairs were actually necessary for safety. This reliance on a one-sided submission from an interested party without independent verification renders the raze order arbitrary.

### B. The Raze Order Was Triggered by the Insured's Inquiry, Not Municipal Initiative

The record establishes that the McLartys' attorney contacted Inspector Craanen after receiving the ServPro estimate specifically to inquire whether the City would issue a raze order. Craanen then requested a copy of the estimate and issued the raze order based solely on that document.

This sequence of events raises serious concerns about the order's propriety. A raze order is an exercise of municipal police power intended to protect public safety—not a tool to be invoked at the request of private parties seeking to trigger insurance coverage. The plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that a municipality may issue a raze order simply because a property owner or their attorney requests one.

In **Schultz v. City of Fond du Lac**, 124 Wis. 2d 56, 68, 368 N.W.2d 704, 710 (Wis. Ct. App. 1985), this Court cautioned that "municipal exercise of police power must be based on legitimate public safety concerns, not private requests designed to circumvent other legal mechanisms." Here, the evidence suggests the raze order was issued at the behest of the McLartys seeking to convert their insurance claim into a total loss—a purpose wholly divorced from the statute's public safety mandate.

The circuit court erred in failing to consider this crucial factor. The order was not the product of independent municipal judgment about public safety; it was a response to a private party's request, based entirely on that party's self-serving submission. Such an order cannot stand.

---

### ARGUMENT III

## SMOKE AND WATER DAMAGE REMEDIATION COSTS MUST BE EXCLUDED FROM THE REPAIR COST CALCULATION

### A. The Statute's Purpose Is Structural Safety, Not Complete Restoration

The plaintiff argues that all repair costs, including smoke and water damage remediation, must be included in determining whether repair exceeds 50% of assessed value. This interpretation stretches the statute beyond any reasonable construction and produces absurd consequences.

The purpose of § 66.0413 is identifying buildings that are structurally unsound and pose safety hazards. A building requiring extensive smoke and water remediation may nonetheless be structurally sound—indeed, smoke and water damage are routine consequences of fires that insurance policies routinely cover and remediate without rendering buildings uninhabitable or unsafe.

In **Interstate Partners v. City of Racine**, 196 Wis. 2d 44, 58, 539 N.W.2d 442, 448 (Wis. Ct. App. 1995), this Court held that "cost of repair" under the statute must be interpreted to further the statute's safety purpose, not to create a mechanical test that ignores actual safety concerns. Including routine fire remediation costs in the calculation conflates the statute's safety purpose with an entirely different concern—ensuring complete restoration to pre-loss condition.

This distinction matters. If smoke and water damage must be included, virtually any significant fire will trigger raze authority because fire suppression itself creates water damage and smoke permeates building materials. This result cannot be what the legislature intended.

### B. Including These Costs Produces Absurd Results

The ServPro estimate included approximately $76,500 in smoke remediation costs. Excluding this amount, the remaining repair costs were approximately $36,100 for the garage and main floor—far below 50% of the $124,000 assessed value. The plaintiff cannot satisfy the statutory threshold without including these costs.

This demonstrates the absurdity of the plaintiff's interpretation. The McLartys' home suffered garage fire damage. The structure itself could be repaired for approximately $36,100—less than 30% of assessed value. Yet the plaintiff argues the home must be razed because smoke remediation costs push the total over 50%.

Under this reasoning, a home with minimal structural damage but extensive smoke staining would be subject to raze, while a home with catastrophic structural damage but no smoke damage might escape raze. This arbitrary result has no relationship to actual safety concerns.

In **Petry v. City of Kenosha**, 2014 WI App 45, 353 Wis. 2d 85, 844 N.W.2d 367 (Wis. Ct. App. 2014), this Court cautioned that courts must interpret statutes to avoid "arbitrary and unreasonable results that frustrate legislative intent." The plaintiff's interpretation produces precisely such a result.

The circuit court erred in accepting the plaintiff's argument that smoke and water damage must be included. These costs represent remediation and cleaning—not structural repairs necessary to make the building safe. They should be excluded from the calculation.

The raze order was improperly issued: the statute does not apply to sudden fire damage, the inspector failed to conduct an independent investigation, the order was triggered by the insured's inquiry rather than municipal initiative, and the inclusion of smoke and water damage remediation produces an absurd result. Each of these deficiencies independently requires reversal.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**AUTO-OWNERS INSURANCE COMPANY, Appellant,**
v.
**CITY OF APPLETON, Respondent.**

**CASE NO.** [INSERT CASE NO]
**APPEAL FROM:** Circuit Court for Outagamie County, Honorable Margaret A. Burnham, Circuit Judge.

**DECISION**

The court affirms the circuit court’s order denying Auto-Owners Insurance Company’s motion for an injunction.

**OPINION**

Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals the circuit court’s denial of its motion for a temporary injunction to prohibit the razing of the McLartys’ residence. Auto-Owners argues that the raze order issued by the City of Appleton was invalid because: (1) the raze order statute, Wis. Stat. § 66.0413, does not apply to buildings damaged by sudden fire; (2) the building inspector’s failure to personally inspect the premises rendered the order arbitrary; and (3) smoke and water damage remediation costs must be excluded from the repair cost calculation. For the reasons set forth below, we reject these arguments and affirm.

**I. STATUTORY INTERPRETATION OF "OUT OF REPAIR"**

Auto-Owners first contends that Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not authorize the razing of buildings damaged by sudden catastrophic events like fires, arguing that the phrase "out of repair" implies only gradual deterioration. This argument is unpersuasive.

The plain language of the statute provides the governing rule. Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) states: "If the cost of repair exceeds 50 percent of the assessed value of the building, the building is considered to be out of repair." The statute imposes no temporal limitation on the damage, nor does it distinguish between gradual decay and sudden destruction. It establishes a clear, objective mathematical threshold. As this Court has previously held, statutory interpretation must begin with the plain language of the statute, and courts must avoid constructions that produce absurd results. *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995).

Auto-Owners’ attempt to read a "gradual deterioration" requirement into the statute is contrary to the text. Adopting such a limitation would lead to the absurd result of prohibiting municipalities from addressing buildings that are rendered unsafe and uninhabitable by sudden events, such as fires, simply because the damage was acute. The evident purpose of the statute is to address economic feasibility and public safety, not to regulate the cause of the damage. Therefore, the circuit court correctly determined that the McLartys’ home, which sustained repair costs exceeding 50% of its assessed value, was "out of repair" under the statute.

**II. SCOPE OF "COST OF REPAIR"**

Auto-Owners next argues that smoke and water damage remediation costs should be excluded from the "cost of repair" calculation. We disagree.

The statute defines "cost of repair" broadly to encompass all necessary expenses to restore the structure. In *City of Milwaukee v. Milwaukee County*, 2010 WI 12, 323 Wis. 2d 588, 780 N.W.2d 554 (Wis. 2010), this Court rejected arguments that specific categories of repair costs should be excluded, emphasizing that "all reasonable costs associated with repairs must be considered to ensure public safety and structural integrity."

Here, the ServPro estimate included costs for smoke and water remediation. These costs are directly related to the fire damage and are necessary to render the home safe and habitable. Excluding these costs would ignore the reality that fire suppression and smoke damage significantly impact a building's habitability. As noted in *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995), interpretations of a statute that defy its evident purpose—here, public safety—are improper. The circuit court did not err in including these costs in its determination that repairs were not reasonable.

**III. INSPECTION REQUIREMENT**

Auto-Owners asserts that the raze order was invalid because the building inspector, Kurt Craanen, failed to personally inspect the premises before issuing the order. This argument lacks merit.

Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not contain an explicit requirement for a personal, on-site inspection prior to issuing a raze order. In *Village of Franklin v. County of Milwaukee*, 116 Wis. 2d 197, 342 N.W.2d 405 (Wis. 1984), this Court affirmed that municipal decisions under this statute are reasonable if based on credible evidence, even without an on-site inspection.

The record shows that Inspector Craanen reviewed the ServPro estimate provided by the McLartys’ attorney and cross-referenced it with the City’s property records to confirm the assessed value of $124,000. This constituted a sufficient review of credible evidence to satisfy the statutory requirements. While Auto-Owners cites *Davis v. City of Oshkosh*, 126 Wis. 2d 502, 377 N.W.2d 639 (Wis. Ct. App. 1985), for the proposition that due process requires independent judgment, the inspector’s verification of the assessed value against municipal records demonstrates the requisite independent judgment. The circuit court’s finding that Craanen acted professionally and in good faith is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous.

**IV. PROCEDURAL ORIGIN OF THE ORDER**

Finally, Auto-Owners argues that the raze order was arbitrary because it was issued in response to the insured’s inquiry rather than municipal initiative. This argument misapprehends the municipality’s duties.

A municipality has an obligation to act when presented with evidence that a building meets the statutory criteria for being "out of repair." The fact that the McLartys’ attorney initiated the contact does not invalidate the City’s subsequent review and decision. The City is not prohibited from enforcing its building codes simply because a private party brings the condition to its attention. The statutory criteria were met, and the City’s response was consistent with its police power to protect public safety.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the City of Appleton lawfully issued the raze order under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The circuit court correctly applied the law and did not abuse its discretion in denying Auto-Owners’ motion for an injunction. We therefore affirm the order of the circuit court.

The defense’s proposed "gradual deterioration" requirement would create such an absurdity: it would prevent municipalities from addressing buildings rendered unsafe by sudden events like fires, even when repair costs exceed 50% of assessed value. County of Milwaukee*, 116 Wis. 2d 197, 342 N.W.2d 405 (Wis. 1984), this Court affirmed that municipal decisions under this statute are reasonable if based on credible evidence, such as written reports and municipal records.

Here, Inspector Craanen reviewed the ServPro estimate provided by the McLartys’ attorney and verified the assessed value against City property records. This constituted "adequate information" under *Davis* and "credible evidence" under *Village of Franklin*. The fact that the estimate was revised downward by the contractor the following day does not render the Inspector's reliance on the document provided to him arbitrary or unreasonable. The circuit court found that Inspector Craanen acted professionally and in good faith, and this finding is supported by the record. Due process does not require a municipality to ignore the information before it; it requires a rational basis for decision-making, which was present here.

**IV. PROCEDURAL ORIGIN OF THE ORDER**

Finally, Auto-Owners argues that the raze order was arbitrary because it was issued in response to the insured’s inquiry rather than municipal initiative. Auto-Owners suggests this sequence of events creates a conflict of interest and allows the City to "circumvent the insurance claims process."

This argument misapprehends the municipality's police power. A municipality has a duty to enforce building codes and address unsafe structures when made aware of them. The source of the information—whether it be a routine municipal inspection or a complaint from a private citizen—does not invalidate the municipality's obligation to act if the statutory criteria are met. *Schultz v. City of Fond du Lac*, 124 Wis. 2d 56, 368 N.W.2d 704 (Wis. Ct. App. 1985). The City did not "rubber-stamp" the request; it reviewed the estimate, verified the property value, and determined that the statutory threshold was met. The fact that the insureds stood to benefit from a total loss insurance payout does not negate the City's independent determination that the building was "out of repair" under the statute.

For the foregoing reasons, the City of Appleton lawfully issued the raze order under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The circuit court correctly applied the law and did not err in denying Auto-Owners’ motion for an injunction. We therefore affirm the order of the circuit court.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR RESPONDENT CITY OF APPLETON

Your Honor, the City of Appleton respectfully submits this closing statement in defense of the circuit court's order denying Auto-Owners Insurance Company's motion for a temporary injunction. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should affirm the lower court's decision and uphold the raze order issued pursuant to Wis. Stat. § 66.0413.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a straightforward question of statutory interpretation: does Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 authorize a municipality to issue a raze order when the cost of repairing a fire-damaged building exceeds fifty percent of its assessed value? The statute's language is clear, unambiguous, and contains no exception for sudden fire damage. Auto-Owners asks this Court to judicially rewrite the statute by inserting a temporal limitation that the legislature expressly omitted. This Court should decline that invitation and affirm the circuit court's well-reasoned decision.

---

## II. THE RAZE ORDER STATUTE APPLIES TO FIRE DAMAGE

### A. Plain Language of the Statute Controls

The cornerstone of statutory interpretation in Wisconsin is the plain meaning rule. As established in **State v. Post**, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995), when the statutory language is unambiguous, courts must give effect to the plain meaning without resorting to judicial construction. Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) states in pertinent part: "If the cost of repair exceeds 50 percent of the assessed value of the building, the building is considered to be out of repair."

This language establishes an objective, mathematical threshold. There is no reference to the cause of the damage, no distinction between gradual deterioration and sudden destruction, and no temporal element whatsoever. The statute uses the phrase "out of repair" as a term of art defined by a specific cost-to-value ratio. Auto-Owners' argument that "out of repair" implies chronic neglect finds no support in the text.

### B. Auto-Owners' Reliance on *Bauer v. City of Waukesha* Is Misplaced

Auto-Owners cites **Bauer v. City of Waukesha**, 182 Wis. 2d 436, 513 N.W.2d 708 (Wis. Ct. App. 1994), for the proposition that building codes historically target chronic neglect. However, *Bauer* addressed whether a building with chronic code violations could be deemed "out of repair," not whether sudden catastrophic damage falls outside the statute's scope. The case does not support the interpretation Auto-Owners urges.

Furthermore, the purpose of the raze order statute is to protect public safety by addressing buildings that pose hazards to the community. A fire-damaged structure that cannot be economically repaired presents the same—or greater—risks as one suffering from long-term neglect. Smoke damage, compromised structural integrity, and potential mold growth create conditions hazardous to the public just as surely as chronic code violations.

### C. Auto-Owners' Interpretation Produces Absurd Results

Auto-Owners warns that applying the statute to fire damage produces an "absurd result." Yet the opposite interpretation produces the absurdity Auto-Owners claims to fear. Under Auto-Owners' reasoning, a municipality could raze a home with minor but persistent code violations while being powerless to address a structure rendered uninhabitable by fire—a structure that poses far greater risks to public safety. This Court should reject an interpretation that leads to such untenable consequences. See **State v. Post**, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995) (courts should avoid interpretations that produce absurd results).

---

## III. THE COST OF REPAIR CORRECTLY INCLUDES ALL REASONABLE REMEDIATION COSTS

### A. Smoke and Water Damage Are Integral to Structural Repair

Auto-Owners argues that smoke and water damage remediation costs should be excluded from the repair cost calculation as "cosmetic" in nature. This argument misunderstands the nature of fire damage and the purpose of the raze order statute.

Smoke permeates structural elements, including walls, insulation, and framing. Water used to suppress fires saturates building materials, promoting mold growth and wood rot. These are not cosmetic concerns affecting only the visual appearance of the building; they are fundamental structural issues that must be addressed to render the building safe and habitable. As held in **City of Milwaukee v. Milwaukee County**, 2010 WI 12, 323 Wis. 2d 588, 780 N.W.2d 554 (Wis. 2010), repair costs must be broadly construed to ensure public safety.

### B. The Raze Order Is Valid Regardless of Remediation Costs

Even assuming arguendo that smoke and water remediation costs should be excluded, the raze order remains valid. The ServPro "rebuild only" estimate—excluding cleaning, demolition, and smoke sealing—was approximately $112,850. The assessed value of the home was $124,000. Fifty percent of the assessed value is $62,000. Because $112,850 exceeds $62,000, the statutory threshold is satisfied regardless of whether the approximately $17,750 in smoke remediation costs is included. Auto-Owners' argument is therefore moot.

---

## IV. THE BUILDING INSPECTOR'S ACTIONS SATISFIED DUE PROCESS

### A. No Personal Inspection Is Required by Statute

Auto-Owners contends that Inspector Craanen's failure to personally inspect the premises before issuing the raze order violated due process. However, Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not explicitly require an on-site inspection prior to issuing a raze order. The statute requires "adequate information" upon which a rational determination can be made.

### B. Inspector Craanen Had Adequate Information

In **Village of Franklin v. County of Milwaukee**, 116 Wis. 2d 197, 342 N.W.2d 405 (Wis. 1984), this Court affirmed that municipal decisions under the raze order statute are reasonable if based on credible evidence such as written reports and municipal records. Here, Inspector Craanen reviewed the ServPro estimate—a detailed, professional repair estimate provided by a licensed restoration company—and verified the property's assessed value against City records. This constituted adequate information for a rational decision.

The circuit court specifically found Inspector Craanen to be credible and to have acted professionally and in good faith. This finding, supported by the record, should not be disturbed on appeal. See **Davis v. City of Oshkosh**, 126 Wis. 2d 502, 377 N.W.2d 639 (Wis. Ct. App. 1985) (due process requires rational judgment, not personal inspection).

### C. The Minor Revision to the Estimate Does Not Render the Order Arbitrary

Auto-Owners notes that ServPro revised its estimate downward the day after originally providing it. However, Inspector Craanen relied on the estimate provided to him in the ordinary course of his duties. The existence of a subsequent revision does not render his reliance on the original estimate arbitrary or unreasonable. Municipal officials must make decisions based on the information available to them at the time; they are not required to anticipate future modifications.

---

## V. THE SOURCE OF THE INQUIRY DOES NOT INVALIDATE THE RAZE ORDER

### A. Municipalities Have a Duty to Act When Informed of Hazards

Auto-Owners argues that the raze order was invalid because it was issued in response to the McLartys' inquiry rather than municipal initiative. This argument misapprehends the nature of municipal police power.

In **Schultz v. City of Fond du Lac**, 124 Wis. 2d 56, 368 N.W.2d 704 (Wis. Ct. App. 1985), this Court held that municipalities have a duty to enforce building codes when made aware of unsafe structures, regardless of the source of information. The City did not "rubber-stamp" the McLartys' request. Rather, upon receiving information about the alleged hazard, the City independently verified the repair costs and assessed value, confirmed that the statutory threshold was met, and issued the raze order accordingly.

### B. The City's Police Power Is Not Voided by Private Communication

A municipality's authority to protect public safety does not vanish when a private citizen contacts the City. To the contrary, the City's duty to act arises when it receives credible evidence of a hazard. The fact that the McLartys initiated the inquiry does not diminish the City's independent obligation to enforce the building code when the statutory criteria are satisfied.

Auto-Owners' argument that the raze order allows the insureds to "obtain an insurance windfall" conflates two separate legal frameworks. The raze order statute addresses public safety; insurance contracts address private financial arrangements. The City has no obligation to consider insurance implications when enforcing its building code. Similarly, Auto-Owners cannot use the insurance relationship to defeat the City's legitimate exercise of police power.

The City of Appleton issued the raze order in full compliance with Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The statute's plain language authorizes raze orders when repair costs exceed fifty percent of assessed value, without distinction as to the cause of damage. Inspector Craanen acted professionally and in good faith, relying on credible evidence to make his determination. The source of the information that brought the hazard to the City's attention is irrelevant to the validity of the order.

Auto-Owners' arguments ask this Court to rewrite the statute by adding requirements and limitations that the legislature did not enact. This Court should decline that invitation and affirm the circuit court's order.

**AUTO-OWNERS INSURANCE COMPANY, Appellant,**
v.
**CITY OF APPLETON, Respondent.**

**CASE NO.** [INSERT CASE NO]
**APPEAL FROM:** Circuit Court for Outagamie County, Honorable Margaret A. Burnham, Circuit Judge.

**DECISION**

The court affirms the circuit court’s order denying Auto-Owners Insurance Company’s motion for an injunction.

**OPINION**

Auto-Owners Insurance Company appeals the circuit court’s denial of its motion for a temporary injunction to prohibit the razing of the McLartys’ residence. Auto-Owners raises several arguments on appeal: (1) that the raze order statute, Wis. Stat. § 66.0413, does not apply to buildings damaged by sudden fire; (2) that smoke and water damage remediation costs must be excluded from the repair cost calculation; (3) that the building inspector’s failure to personally inspect the premises rendered the order arbitrary and a violation of due process; and (4) that the order was invalid because it was issued at the insured’s request rather than municipal initiative. After reviewing the closing arguments of the parties, we reject Auto-Owners’ arguments and affirm.

**I. STATUTORY INTERPRETATION OF "OUT OF REPAIR"**

Auto-Owners contends that the phrase "out of repair" in Wis. Stat. § 66.0413(1)(c) implies a condition of gradual deterioration and therefore cannot apply to sudden fire damage. Auto-Owners relies on *Bauer v. City of Waukesha*, 182 Wis. 2d 436, 513 N.W.2d 708 (Wis. Ct. App. 1994), to suggest that building codes historically target chronic neglect.

We are not persuaded. The plain language of the statute is the starting point for our analysis. *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995). Section 66.0413(1)(c) states: "If the cost of repair exceeds 50 percent of the assessed value of the building, the building is considered to be out of repair." The statute establishes a clear, mathematical threshold and contains no temporal limitation distinguishing between gradual decay and sudden destruction. To read a "gradual deterioration" requirement into the statute would be to judicially amend the text, which we cannot do. *State v. Post*, 197 Wis. 2d 279, 541 N.W.2d 115 (Wis. 1995).

Auto-Owners argues that applying the statute to fire damage produces an absurd result by transforming a building maintenance statute into a tool that punishes homeowners for insurable events. However, the evident purpose of the statute is to address public safety and economic feasibility. A building rendered uninhabitable by fire poses the same safety risks as one rendered uninhabitable by neglect. Excluding fire damage from the statute would create the absurd result of allowing a municipality to raze a home with minor but chronic code violations while prohibiting it from addressing a fire-damaged structure that is a total economic loss to repair. We decline to adopt an interpretation that contradicts the statute's plain language and public safety purpose.

**II. SCOPE OF "COST OF REPAIR"**

Auto-Owners argues that smoke and water damage remediation costs should be excluded from the "cost of repair" calculation, characterizing these costs as cosmetic or related to contents rather than structural integrity. Auto-Owners attempts to distinguish *City of Milwaukee v. Milwaukee County*, 2010 WI 12, 323 Wis. 2d 588, 780 N.W.2d 554 (Wis. 2010), which held that repair costs must be broadly construed to ensure public safety.

We find Auto-Owners' argument unpersuasive for two reasons. First, smoke and water remediation are often essential to rendering a fire-damaged structure safe and habitable. Smoke permeates structural elements, and water used to suppress fires can cause mold and rot. These are not merely cosmetic concerns; they are integral to the structural repair of the building. As held in *City of Milwaukee v. Milwaukee County*, 2010 WI 12, 323 Wis. 2d 588, 780 N.W.2d 554 (Wis. 2010), the statute requires consideration of all reasonable costs necessary to address the building's unsafe condition. Furthermore, courts cannot rewrite insurance contracts to alter coverage; excluding costs that affect structural safety would amount to such a rewrite. *Kohlenberg v. American Century Ins. Co.*, 84 Wis. 2d 199, 267 N.W.2d 852 (Wis. 1978).

Second, even if we were to accept Auto-Owners' argument and exclude the smoke and water remediation costs, the raze order would still be valid. The record shows that the "rebuild only" estimate—excluding cleaning, demolition, and smoke sealing—was approximately $112,850. The assessed value of the home was $124,000. Fifty percent of the assessed value is $62,000. Because the "rebuild only" cost of $112,850 exceeds the $62,000 threshold, the building was "out of repair" under the statute regardless of whether the smoke remediation costs were included. Therefore, Auto-Owners' argument regarding the inclusion of these costs is moot.

**III. INSPECTION REQUIREMENT AND DUE PROCESS**

Auto-Owners asserts that the raze order violated due process because Inspector Craanen failed to personally inspect the premises before issuing it. Auto-Owners relies on *Davis v. City of Oshkosh*, 126 Wis. 2d 502, 377 N.W.2d 639 (Wis. Ct. App. 1985), to argue that administrative actions affecting property rights must be based on independent verification.

We disagree with Auto-Owners' interpretation of the law. Wis. Stat. § 66.0413 does not explicitly require a personal, on-site inspection prior to issuing a raze order. In *Village of Franklin v. County of Milwaukee*, 116 Wis. 2d 197, 342 N.W.2d 405 (Wis. 1984), this Court affirmed that municipal decisions under this statute are reasonable if based on credible evidence, such as written reports and municipal records.

Here, Inspector Craanen reviewed the ServPro estimate provided by the McLartys’ attorney and verified the assessed value against City property records. This constituted "adequate information" under *Davis* and "credible evidence" under *Village of Franklin*. The fact that the estimate was revised downward by the contractor the following day does not render the Inspector's reliance on the document provided to him arbitrary or unreasonable. The circuit court found that Inspector Craanen acted professionally and in good faith, and this finding is supported by the record. Due process does not require a municipality to ignore the information before it; it requires a rational basis for decision-making, which was present here.

**IV. PROCEDURAL ORIGIN OF THE ORDER**

Finally, Auto-Owners argues that the raze order was arbitrary because it was issued in response to the insured’s inquiry rather than municipal initiative. Auto-Owners suggests this sequence of events creates a conflict of interest and allows the City to "circumvent the insurance claims process."

This argument misapprehends the municipality's police power. A municipality has a duty to enforce building codes and address unsafe structures when made aware of them. The source of the information—whether it be a routine municipal inspection or a complaint from a private citizen—does not invalidate the municipality's obligation to act if the statutory criteria are met. *Schultz v. City of Fond du Lac*, 124 Wis. 2d 56, 368 N.W.2d 704 (Wis. Ct. App. 1985). The City did not "rubber-stamp" the request; it reviewed the estimate, verified the property value, and determined that the statutory threshold was met. The fact that the insureds stood to benefit from a total loss insurance payout does not negate the City's independent determination that the building was "out of repair" under the statute.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the City of Appleton lawfully issued the raze order under Wis. Stat. § 66.0413. The circuit court correctly applied the law and did not err in denying Auto-Owners’ motion for an injunction. We therefore affirm the order of the circuit court.
