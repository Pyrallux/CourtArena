The Supreme Court has long held that disclosure of an informant’s identity is required only when the informant is a "material witness" to the defendant’s guilt or innocence—a standard that applies at trial, not during a suppression hearing. As the Court explained in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957), the "informant’s privilege" must yield only when "the informer’s testimony is relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." But a suppression hearing is not a "fair determination of guilt or innocence"; it is a threshold procedural inquiry into the legality of evidence collection. The informant’s identity has no bearing on whether an officer reasonably believed probable cause existed at the time of the search. *See* id. at 62 ("The privilege is not a matter of constitutional dimension but a rule of evidence designed to protect the public interest in effective law enforcement.").

This principle was reinforced in *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583–84 (1971), where the Court squarely rejected the notion that an informant’s identity must be disclosed to establish probable cause for a search. The Court held that "the reliability of the information provided by an informant may be established without revealing the informant’s identity," so long as the government demonstrates "the informant’s credibility or the reliability of his information" through corroborating details. Here, the trial court properly considered the informant’s past reliability—evidenced by "the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them"—to satisfy the probable cause standard. The defense’s argument that disclosure was necessary for "reliability" misapprehends the law: reliability for suppression purposes is assessed based on the substance of the information, not the informant’s personal identity. *See* United States v. Here, the defense specifically argued that "if she set him up, it goes to her reliability." **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court erred in denying the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant during a hearing on a dispositive motion to suppress. The Appellant argues that the informant’s identity was necessary to challenge the reliability of the information forming the basis for the probable cause determination and because the informant was a material witness to the alleged crime. The State contends that the scope of a suppression hearing is limited to the legality of the search and that probable cause can be established without revealing the informant’s identity.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The public’s interest in protecting the flow of information to law enforcement is balanced against the accused’s right to prepare a defense. The Supreme Court has held that the government’s privilege to withhold the identity of an informant is not absolute. In *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957), the Court stated that the privilege "must give way" where "the informer's testimony may be relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." However, this standard is not without limits. The Court in *Roviaro* also noted that the privilege is a rule of evidence, not a constitutional mandate, designed to protect effective law enforcement. *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 62.

Specifically, in the context of establishing probable cause for a search or arrest, the identity of an informant need not be disclosed. In *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583–84 (1971), the Supreme Court held that "the reliability of the information provided by an informant may be established without revealing the informant's identity," provided the government demonstrates the informant's credibility or the reliability of the information through other means, such as corroboration or a track record of accuracy. This principle is echoed in *United States v. Williams*, 504 F.2d 759, 763 (5th Cir. 1974), which affirmed that "the government need not disclose the identity of an informant to establish probable cause for a search; it is sufficient to show that the information provided by the informant was reliable."

**III. ANALYSIS**

The trial court in this case conducted a hearing on a "dispositive" motion to suppress. The parties agreed that the sole purpose of the hearing was to determine the legality of the search and seizure; if the court ruled in favor of the State, the Appellant would plead guilty, and if the court ruled against the State, the case would be dismissed. This procedural posture is critical. The defense sought to use the motion to disclose to introduce arguments regarding entrapment and the informant's potential role in "setting up" the Appellant—issues that go to the guilt or innocence of the defendant rather than the legality of the officer's actions at the time of the search.

The trial court denied the motion to disclose after finding that the informant was reliable based on "the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." This finding is consistent with the holdings of *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583–84 (1971), and *United States v. Williams*, 504 F.2d 759, 763 (5th Cir. 1974). Under these authorities, the State satisfied its burden of establishing probable cause without revealing the informant's identity. The defense’s assertion that the informant "set him up" relates to the defense of entrapment or the informant's motive, which are matters for trial on the merits, not for a suppression hearing focused on the objective reasonableness of the officer's reliance on the tip.

While the defense relies on *Bodin v. State* and *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), to argue that the informant was a material witness, the facts provided do not demonstrate that the informant's identity was "essential to a fair determination" of the probable cause issue. The defense argues that the informant's belongings were found in the car and that she was present, implying bias. However, the reliability of the tip for probable cause purposes was independently verified by the trial court through the informant's history of providing accurate information. The defense's need to cross-examine the informant regarding potential bias or entrapment does not override the informant's privilege in the context of a suppression hearing where the State has otherwise met its burden. *See Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 62.

The prosecution correctly argues that the defense is attempting to litigate the issue of guilt or innocence—specifically entrapment—under the guise of a suppression hearing. The "dispositive" nature of the agreement underscores that the court was not conducting a trial on the merits. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that the informant's identity was not material to the specific issue of probable cause.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing, the trial court properly applied the law in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The State established the reliability of the informant sufficient to support a finding of probable cause without disclosure, as permitted by *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573 (1971), and *United States v. Williams*, 504 F.2d 759 (5th Cir. 1974). The defense's arguments regarding entrapment and the informant's status as a material witness are more appropriately addressed at trial, not in a suppression hearing. Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling is affirmed.

The prosecution's argument that *Roviaro* does not compel disclosure here because the informant's testimony was not "essential to a fair determination" of the suppression issue reflects a misreading of the privilege analysis. **UPDATED RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the trial court’s denial of the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant. The Appellant contends that the trial court erred in refusing to order disclosure or, at a minimum, an in camera review of the informant’s identity, given specific evidence suggesting the informant’s bias and involvement in the alleged offense. The State argues that the informant’s reliability was sufficiently established by a track record of past tips, rendering disclosure unnecessary for the probable cause determination. After reviewing the rebuttals of both parties and the applicable legal standards, the Court finds that the trial court abused its discretion by failing to conduct a meaningful balancing test under *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), and by not considering an in camera review in light of the specific evidence of the informant’s potential unreliability.

**II. LEGAL STANDARD**

The government’s privilege to withhold the identity of a confidential informant is not absolute. As established in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957), this privilege "must give way" where "the informer's testimony may be relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." The determination of whether disclosure is required involves a balancing of the public’s interest in protecting the flow of information against the accused’s right to prepare a defense. *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 62.

In the context of probable cause, the Supreme Court has held that the reliability of an informant’s tip is assessed under the "totality of the circumstances." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 230 (1983). This analysis includes consideration of the informant’s veracity, reliability, and basis of knowledge. *Gates*, 462 U.S. at 230. While the government may often establish probable cause without revealing the informant’s identity by demonstrating the tip’s reliability through corroboration or a history of accurate tips, *see United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583–84 (1971); *United States v. Williams*, 504 F.2d 759, 763 (5th Cir. 1974), this general rule is not without exception. When a defendant presents specific evidence challenging the informant’s credibility or suggesting bias, the trial court must conduct a careful *Roviaro* analysis. In such instances, an in camera review of the informant’s identity or statement may be an appropriate intermediate step to protect the government’s interest while ensuring a fair determination of probable cause. *See United States v. Marshall*, 488 F.2d 1169, 1175–76 (9th Cir. 1973).

**III. ANALYSIS**

The trial court denied the motion to disclose based on its finding that the informant was reliable "because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." The State relies on *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573 (1971), and *United States v. Williams*, 504 F.2d 759 (5th Cir. 1974), to argue that this finding alone was sufficient to uphold the privilege. However, the State’s reliance on these precedents is misplaced in light of the specific factual context presented by the defense.

Unlike the circumstances in *Harris* and *Williams*, where the defendant’s challenge to the informant’s reliability was general, the Appellant here presented specific evidence that the informant was present during the transaction, that her belongings were found in the Appellant’s car, and that she may have "set him up." Under *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 230 (1983), the informant’s veracity and basis of knowledge are central to the probable cause analysis. Evidence that the informant was a participant in the offense, rather than a mere observer, directly implicates her reliability and motivation for providing information. These are not merely matters of guilt or innocence; they are material to the validity of the probable cause determination itself.

The trial court failed to account for this specific evidence when it relied exclusively on the informant’s past track record. By ignoring the allegations of bias and involvement, the court neglected the balancing test mandated by *Roviaro*. The *Roviaro* Court explicitly stated that the privilege must yield when the informant’s testimony is relevant to a fair determination of the cause. *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 60–61. Here, the informant’s potential bias is highly relevant to the "fair determination" of whether probable cause existed. If the informant fabricated the tip to shift blame or secure an advantage, the foundation for the search and seizure is compromised.

Furthermore, the trial court failed to consider the procedural safeguard identified in *United States v. Marshall*, 488 F.2d 1169, 1175–76 (9th Cir. 1973). *Marshall* holds that when a defendant raises specific concerns about an informant’s reliability or bias, the trial court should conduct an in camera review to determine whether disclosure is warranted. The Appellant’s request for disclosure was supported by concrete allegations regarding the informant’s belongings and presence. The trial court’s outright denial, without first conducting an in camera review to assess the credibility of these allegations, constituted an abuse of discretion.

The State’s argument that the "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress forecloses disclosure is unpersuasive. The fact that the hearing was dispositive arguably heightens the need for a rigorous scrutiny of the probable cause basis, as the Appellant was agreeing to forego a trial on the merits. The State cannot rely on the dispositive agreement to shield the informant from scrutiny while simultaneously using that same agreement to prevent the Appellant from challenging the very evidence that formed the basis of the State’s case.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred by denying the motion to disclose without conducting the required *Roviaro* balancing test and without considering an in camera review of the informant’s identity. The Appellant presented specific evidence of the informant’s potential bias and involvement that distinguished this case from the general rule established in *Harris* and *Williams*. Because the informant’s reliability was a material issue to the probable cause determination, and the trial court failed to address the specific challenges to that reliability, the judgment of the trial court is reversed. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion, including an in camera review by the trial court to determine whether the informant’s identity must be disclosed.

## II. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

### A. The "Dispositive" Nature of the Motion Does Not Foreclose Disclosure

The prosecution repeatedly emphasizes that the motion to suppress was "dispositive" and that the defense agreed to this characterization. However, this argument proves too much. The defense never waived its right to challenge the probable cause basis for the search—it merely agreed that the resolution of the suppression motion would determine the outcome of the case. As the appellate court correctly noted, the dispositive nature of the hearing arguably *heightens* the need for rigorous scrutiny of the probable cause foundation, not diminishes it.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Salinas*, 387 F.3d 550 (7th Cir. 2004), is misplaced. In *Salinas*, the court held that when a suppression hearing is dispositive, the trial court's role is limited to determining whether evidence should be suppressed. This does not mean, however, that the court may ignore fundamental defects in the probable cause analysis. The very purpose of a dispositive suppression hearing is to determine whether the evidence was lawfully obtained. If the probable cause determination is flawed because the informant's reliability was improperly assessed, the agreement to make the motion dispositive cannot insulate that flaw from review.

The prosecution's statement that "if they wanted to have the confidential informant disclosed and then have that attacked, they should have had a trial" reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the defense's position. The defense is not seeking disclosure to prove guilt or innocence at a trial—it seeks disclosure to challenge the validity of the probable cause that formed the sole basis for the search. This distinction was recognized by the Supreme Court in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957), which held that the informer's privilege "must give way" where the informer's testimony may be relevant to "a fair determination of a cause." The "cause" here is the suppression motion, and the informant's potential bias is directly relevant to whether probable cause existed.

### B. The Defense's Claims Go to Probable Cause, Not Merely Guilt or Innocence

The prosecution attempts to dismiss the defense's allegations about the informant "setting up" the defendant as irrelevant guilt-or-innocence matters. This characterization is incorrect and ignores the specific facts presented to the trial court.

The defense presented evidence that the confidential informant was present during the transaction, that her belongings were found in the defendant's car when it was released from evidence, and that she may have "set him up." These are not generalized claims about the informant's character or motives unrelated to the search. Under the totality-of-the-circumstances analysis mandated by *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 230 (1983), the informant's veracity, reliability, and basis of knowledge are central to the probable cause determination. Evidence that the informant was a participant in the offense, rather than a mere observer, directly implicates her reliability and motivation for providing information.

The prosecution argues that the defense's allegations "do not challenge the accuracy of the tip regarding the cocaine's location." This argument misses the point. The question under *Gates* is not merely whether the tip was accurate, but whether the informant was credible and reliable. If the informant fabricated the tip to shift blame or secure an advantage, the tip's "accuracy" becomes irrelevant—the information was not lawfully obtained and cannot form the basis for probable cause.

The prosecution cites *United States v. Jones*, 959 F.2d 156 (10th Cir. 1992), for the proposition that disclosure is not required when the defendant's challenge goes to guilt or innocence. However, *Jones* is distinguishable. In *Jones*, the defendant generally challenged the informant's credibility without presenting specific evidence of bias or involvement. Here, the defense presented specific evidence—the informant's presence, her belongings in the car, and the allegation that she "set him up"—that directly challenges the informant's reliability for probable cause purposes. This distinction was recognized by the appellate court and properly forms the basis for requiring disclosure or an in camera review.

### C. The Trial Court Failed to Conduct the Required *Roviaro* Balancing Test

The prosecution defends the trial court's reliance on the informant's "past tips" and "accuracy" to establish reliability. While this factor may be sufficient in some cases, the Supreme Court in *Roviaro* explicitly held that the informer's privilege is not absolute and must give way when the informer's testimony may be relevant and helpful to the defense or essential to a fair determination of the cause. 353 U.S. at 60–61.

The trial court failed to conduct any balancing of the competing interests. It simply found that the informant was reliable "because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them" and denied the motion. This approach ignores the specific evidence presented by the defense about the informant's potential bias and involvement. As the appellate court correctly found, when a defendant presents specific evidence challenging the informant's credibility or suggesting bias, the trial court must conduct a careful *Roviaro* analysis. The trial court failed to do so here.

The prosecution argues that *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573 (1971), and *United States v. Williams*, 504 F.2d 759 (5th Cir. 1974), establish that past tips alone are sufficient to establish probable cause. However, these cases did not involve the specific evidence of bias and involvement presented here. As the appellate court correctly noted, the State's reliance on these precedents is "misplaced in light of the specific factual context presented by the defense."

### D. An In Camera Review Was Warranted

The prosecution argues that *United States v. Marshall*, 488 F.2d 1169 (9th Cir. 1973), does not require an in camera review here because the defense's allegations do not directly undermine the tip's reliability. This argument fails for the same reason discussed above—the defense's specific allegations about the informant's presence, her belongings in the car, and her alleged role in "setting up" the defendant directly implicate her reliability and motivation.

In *Marshall*, the Ninth Circuit held that when a defendant raises specific concerns about an informant's reliability or bias, the trial court should conduct an in camera review to determine whether disclosure is warranted. 488 F.2d at 1175–76. The defense's request for disclosure was supported by concrete allegations regarding the informant's belongings and presence. The trial court's outright denial, without first conducting an in camera review to assess the credibility of these allegations, constituted an abuse of discretion.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Hines*, 717 F.2d 1481 (D.C. Cir. 1983), is unavailing. However, the defense's allegations here are not merely generalized claims of entrapment. The defense did not waive its right to challenge the probable cause basis for the search. It merely agreed that the resolution of the suppression motion would determine the outcome of the case. If the probable cause determination is flawed, the agreement to make the motion dispositive cannot cure that flaw. The defense preserved its right to challenge the denial of the motion to disclose, and the appellate court properly exercised its review authority.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from the trial court’s denial of the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant. The Appellant contends that the trial court abused its discretion by refusing to order disclosure or, at a minimum, an *in camera* review of the informant’s identity, given specific evidence suggesting the informant’s bias and involvement in the alleged offense. The State argues that the informant’s reliability was sufficiently established by a track record of past tips and that the "dispositive" nature of the suppression hearing precluded the defense from relitigating issues of guilt or innocence. After reviewing the closing arguments of both parties and the applicable legal standards, the Court affirms the appellate court's decision to reverse the trial court.

**II. THE DISPOSITIVE NATURE OF THE MOTION TO SUPPRESS**

The prosecution argues vigorously that the defense waived its right to disclosure by agreeing to a "dispositive" motion to suppress. The State relies on *United States v. Salinas*, 387 F.3d 550, 555 (7th Cir. 2004), for the proposition that when a suppression hearing is dispositive, the trial court’s role is limited to determining whether the evidence should be suppressed, not to resolving factual disputes about guilt or innocence. The State further contends that the defense’s allegations—that the informant "set him up" or that her belongings were found in the car—are classic entrapment or bias issues relevant only to guilt or innocence, not to the probable cause determination.

While the Court agrees that the scope of a dispositive hearing is limited to the suppression issue, this limitation does not absolve the trial court of its duty to conduct a thorough inquiry into the validity of the probable cause supporting the search. The defense did not waive its right to challenge the *foundation* of the State’s evidence; it merely agreed that the outcome of that challenge would determine the case’s final disposition. As the defense correctly notes, the "cause" referenced in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957), is the suppression motion itself. If the informant’s identity is necessary to a fair determination of whether probable cause existed, the dispositive nature of the agreement does not strip the defendant of the right to seek that information.

The prosecution’s reliance on *United States v. Jones*, 959 F.2d 156, 161 (10th Cir. 1992), is unpersuasive in this specific context. *Jones* holds that disclosure is unnecessary when the challenge to the informant’s reliability is untethered to the probable cause facts. Here, however, the defense’s challenge is inextricably linked to the probable cause analysis. The allegation that the informant "set him up" is not merely a claim of entrapment; it is a direct challenge to the informant’s veracity and basis of knowledge. If the informant was a participant in the offense who provided information to shift blame or secure an advantage, the tip’s reliability is fundamentally compromised. Therefore, the dispositive agreement does not bar the defense from seeking disclosure to test the informant’s credibility.

**III. THE NECESSITY OF A ROVIARO BALANCING TEST**

The trial court denied the motion to disclose based solely on its finding that the informant was reliable "because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." The State argues that this finding is sufficient under *United States v. Harris*, 403 U.S. 573, 583–84 (1971), and *United States v. Williams*, 504 F.2d 759, 763 (5th Cir. 1974), which hold that the government may establish probable cause without revealing an informant’s identity by demonstrating a history of accurate tips.

However, the State’s reliance on *Harris* and *Williams* is misplaced where, as here, the defendant presents specific evidence challenging the informant’s credibility. The Supreme Court in *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 230 (1983), established that probable cause is based on the "totality of the circumstances," which includes the informant’s veracity, reliability, and basis of knowledge. While a history of accurate tips is a strong indicator of reliability, it is not dispositive when contradicted by evidence of the informant’s potential involvement in the crime.

The defense presented specific evidence that the informant was present during the transaction, that her belongings were found in the Appellant’s car, and that she may have "set him up." These facts, if true, suggest that the informant was not a neutral bystander but a participant whose motivation for providing information may have been self-serving. Under *Roviaro*, the government’s privilege "must give way" where the informer's testimony is "essential to a fair determination of a cause." *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 62. The trial court failed to balance the public’s interest in protecting the informant against the Appellant’s specific need for disclosure to challenge this alleged participation. By relying exclusively on past tips and ignoring the specific allegations of bias, the trial court failed to perform the required *Roviaro* balancing test.

**IV. THE REQUIREMENT OF AN IN CAMERA REVIEW**

The prosecution argues that *United States v. Marshall*, 488 F.2d 1169, 1175–76 (9th Cir. 1973), does not require an *in camera* review because the defense’s allegations do not directly undermine the tip’s reliability. The State characterizes the defense’s claims as "speculation" about bias, which it argues is insufficient to overcome the informant privilege under *United States v. Hines*, 717 F.2d 1481, 1485 (D.C. Cir. 1983).

The Court disagrees. The defense did not rely on mere speculation; defense counsel stated that "documents had been received yesterday" regarding the informant’s belongings and presence. This constitutes specific evidence triggering the defendant’s right to have the trial court assess the credibility of these claims. *Marshall* holds that when a defendant raises specific concerns about an informant’s reliability or bias, the trial court should conduct an *in camera* review to determine whether disclosure is warranted. *Marshall*, 488 F.2d at 1175–76.

The trial court’s outright denial of the motion, without first conducting an *in camera* review to assess the new evidence presented by the defense, constituted an abuse of discretion. The prosecution’s assertion that these issues are "outside the scope" of a suppression hearing ignores the reality that the informant’s potential participation goes to the heart of the probable cause determination. If the informant was the source of the contraband or the instigator of the transaction, the tip’s accuracy is suspect, and the State cannot rely solely on the informant’s uncorroborated history.

**V. CONCLUSION**

The trial court erred by denying the motion to disclose without conducting the required *Roviaro* balancing test and without considering an *in camera* review of the informant’s identity. The Appellant presented specific evidence of the informant’s potential bias and involvement that distinguished this case from the general rule established in *Harris* and *Williams*. The "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress did not waive the Appellant’s right to challenge the reliability of the informant, which was a material issue to the probable cause determination. Because the trial court failed to address the specific challenges to the informant’s reliability, the judgment of the trial court is reversed. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion, including an *in camera* review by the trial court to determine whether the informant’s identity must be disclosed.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
