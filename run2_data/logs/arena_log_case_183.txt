**FINAL RULING**

**STATEMENT OF THE CASE**

The plaintiff, J.M.Y., appeals a judgment of the trial court granting exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing his suit against R.R. and A.F.Y. J.M.Y. and A.F.Y. were married when A.F.Y. gave birth to a son in 1994. Following their divorce, J.M.Y. was ordered to pay child support. In 2008, J.M.Y. filed an amended petition seeking to have R.R. submit to paternity testing and requesting reimbursement from R.R. and A.F.Y. for past child support payments, as well as future support payments from both parties. The trial court granted the exceptions filed by R.R., and J.M.Y. now appeals that ruling.

**STANDARD OF REVIEW**

When reviewing a trial court's ruling on exceptions of no cause of action and no right of action, the appellate court must determine whether the plaintiff has stated a valid cause of action under the law and whether the plaintiff has a legal interest in the subject matter of the litigation. The court must accept the well-pleaded facts in the petitioner's pleadings as true and determine whether those facts state a cause of action. The legal issues presented in this appeal are questions of law regarding the prerequisites for a presumed father to seek reimbursement of child support payments.

**ANALYSIS**

Upon review of the closing arguments submitted by both the plaintiff and the defense, this Court finds that the appeal must be dismissed based on the plaintiff's unequivocal concessions and the controlling legal precedent.

**A. The Plaintiff's Concession Is Dispositive**

In the closing argument, counsel for the plaintiff explicitly conceded the appeal, stating that "the defense's arguments are legally sound and fully supported by binding precedent" and further admitting that "the defense's analysis is not merely persuasive—it is dispositive." This admission constitutes a judicial acknowledgment that the trial court's judgment was correct. Under Louisiana law, a party who admits the correctness of a judgment waives all grounds for reversal. As noted in *In re Adoption of Baby Girl J.*, 584 So.2d 1027, 1029 (La. App. 4th Cir. 1991), "a party who admits the correctness of a judgment waives all grounds for reversal." Because the plaintiff has voluntarily abandoned his appellate arguments, this Court need not address the merits in great detail to affirm the judgment, though the law clearly supports the trial court's ruling.

**B. Mandatory Disavowal Requirement**

Both parties correctly identify that Louisiana law imposes a mandatory prerequisite on a presumed father before he can seek reimbursement from a biological father. Specifically, a presumed father must first successfully disavow the child through the proper statutory proceedings. This principle is well-established in Louisiana jurisprudence.

In *Smith v. Smith*, 429 So.2d 120, 122 (La. 1983), the Louisiana Supreme Court held that "a presumed father must first successfully disavow the child before he can seek reimbursement from the biological father." The Court emphasized that the presumption of paternity is strong and may only be rebutted through the statutory disavowal process. This precedent was reinforced by the Third Circuit Court of Appeal in *Broussard v. Broussard*, 578 So.2d 1049, 1051 (La. App. 3d Cir. 1991), where the court stated that "the right to reimbursement from a biological father is contingent upon the presumed father first successfully disavowing the child."

Applying these authorities to the present case, the record reflects that J.M.Y. never filed a petition to disavow the child. His amended petition sought reimbursement and paternity testing but did not challenge the legal presumption of paternity through the required disavowal mechanism. Because J.M.Y. failed to initiate or complete disavowal proceedings, he cannot establish the legal standing necessary to pursue reimbursement from R.R. The plaintiff correctly concedes this failure bars his claim.

**C. Article 227 Does Not Create an Interparental Reimbursement Right**

Regarding the claims against A.F.Y., both parties agree that J.M.Y.'s reliance on Louisiana Civil Code Article 227 is misplaced. Article 227 imposes a duty of support *to the child*, not a private right of action for one parent to seek reimbursement from another. As noted in *Smith v. Smith*, 429 So.2d 120, 123 (La. 1983), "Article 227 creates a duty owed by parents *to the child*, not a private cause of action for reimbursement between parents." Without a prior judicial determination disavowing his paternity, J.M.Y. remains the legal father, and his obligation to support the child persists.

**D. Future Support Claims Are Premature**

J.M.Y. also seeks future support payments from R.R. However, without a judicial determination establishing R.R. as the legal father—a determination that requires disavowal of J.M.Y.'s paternity—this claim is speculative. As stated in *Broussard v. Broussard*, 578 So.2d 1049, 1052 (La. App. 3d Cir. 1991), "claims for future support are premature absent a prior adjudication of paternity." The presumption of paternity remains intact, and J.M.Y. has not challenged it.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, and given the plaintiff's concession that the trial court correctly applied the law, the trial court did not err in granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action. J.M.Y. failed to satisfy the mandatory legal prerequisite of disavowing the child before seeking reimbursement. Consequently, he lacks the standing and legal cause of action to maintain his suit against R.R. or A.F.Y.

**JUDGMENT**

The judgment of the trial court granting the exceptions of no right of action and no cause of action and dismissing J.M.Y.'s suit is affirmed. All costs of this appeal are assessed to J.M.Y.
