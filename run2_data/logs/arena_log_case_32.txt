COURT ARENA LOG - Case 32
FACTS:
DECISION AND JOURNAL ENTRY
HENSAL, Presiding Judge.
{¶ 1} Defendant-Appellant, Dana Kittle, appeals her conviction for illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs from the Summit County Court of Common Pleas. This Court affirms.
I.
{¶ 2} This case involves the discovery of a methamphetamine lab and Ms. Kittle's involvement with same. Officer Lemonier from the Akron Police Department testified that he and another officer conducted surveillance on an apartment where they believed illegal activity was occurring. The officers observed Ms. Kittle and another woman, J.S., leave the apartment and get into Ms. Kittle's car, with J.S. taking the driver's seat. Shortly thereafter, the officers performed a traffic stop on the vehicle. During the stop, the officers discovered that J.S. did not have a driver's license and that she had an outstanding felony warrant. As a result, they placed her under arrest and searched her, finding three bags of methamphetamine in her purse. The officers did not arrest or search Ms. Kittle, but did request that she empty her pockets, which revealed no illegal substances.
{¶ 3} The officers then returned to the apartment and knocked on the door. After about twenty minutes of waiting outside, one of the occupants allowed the officers inside. Upon entering, Officer Lemonier immediately smelled the distinct odor associated with the production of methamphetamine. The occupant then consented to a search of the apartment, which revealed a number of items associated with the production of methamphetamine, as well as several small bags containing the finished product.
{¶ 4} Through his investigation of the matter, including an interview with J.S., Officer Lemonier discovered information that implicated Ms. Kittle. Another officer located Ms. Kittle and brought her to the Akron Police Department for questioning. Officer Lemonier conducted the interview, wherein Ms. Kittle admitted to buying pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine.
*1243{¶ 5} A Grand Jury indicted Ms. Kittle on the following three counts: (1) illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs in violation of Revised Code Section 2925.041(A) ; (2) illegal manufacture of drugs in violation of Section 2925.04(A); and (3) aggravated possession of drugs in violation of 2925.11(A),(C)(1). The State dismissed the latter two counts prior to trial.
{¶ 6} The case proceeded to a bench trial. The State presented testimony from one witness: Officer Lemonier. The State also played portions of Officer Lemonier's interview with Ms. Kittle, and introduced records from The National Precursor Log Exchange ("NPLEX"), which is a system that electronically tracks purchases of pseudoephedrine. Ms. Kittle did not present any evidence.
{¶ 7} The trial court found Ms. Kittle guilty, and subsequently sentenced her to five years of imprisonment. She now appeals, raising three assignments of error for our review.
II.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I
THE TRIAL COURT ERRED WHEN IT ADMITTED THE NPLEX RECORDS INTO EVIDENCE AND ALLOWED A POLICE OFFICER WHO WAS NOT A QUALIFIED WITNESS TO LAY THE FOUNDATION FOR INTRODUCING THE NPLEX RECORDS INTO EVIDENCE, IN VIOLATION OF MS. KITTLE'S CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS PURSUANT TO ARTICLE I, SECTION 10, OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE CONFRONTATION CLAUSE OF THE SIXTH AMENDMENT OF THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 8} In her first assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court erred by allowing Officer Lemonier to lay the foundation for the NPLEX records because he was not the custodian of those records, nor was he otherwise qualified to do so. Ms. Kittle asserts that a de novo standard of review applies because the trial court's error in this regard implicated her rights under the Confrontation Clause.
{¶ 9} We will begin by addressing the standard of review. While Ms. Kittle's assignment of error suggests that she believes NPLEX records are testimonial in nature, thus implicating the Confrontation Clause, she does not develop an argument in that regard. See App.R. 16(A)(7). Rather, she cites Ohio Supreme Court precedent for the proposition that business records are nontestimonial, and states: "[a]ssuming that the NPLEX records of the pharmacy admitted into evidence in [this] case were truly nontestimonial in nature, the foundation for their proper authentication as 'business records' under the meaning of Evid.R. 803(6) could not have been established by [Officer Lemonier]." Ms. Kittle does, however, develop such an argument in her reply brief, arguing that NPLEX records are testimonial because the primary purpose of that system is to "assist in the interdiction of methamphetamine creation and use[.]"
{¶ 10} As an initial matter, we note that "[a]ppellate courts generally will not consider a new issue presented for the first time in a reply brief." State v. Quarterman , 140 Ohio St.3d 464, 2014-Ohio-4034, 19 N.E.3d 900, ¶ 18. Notwithstanding, we are unpersuaded by Ms. Kittle's argument that NPLEX records are testimonial in nature, thus implicating the Confrontation Clause. This is because "[t]estimonial statements are those created or *1244given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." State v. Garcia , 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208, ¶ 58, quoting Ohio v. Clark , --- U.S. ----, 135 S.Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L.Ed.2d 306 (2015). The purpose of the NPLEX system, however, is to track purchases of pseudoephedrine. See R.C. 3715.05 ; State v. Coleman , 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, ¶ 35 ("The purpose of the NPLEx system is to monitor suspicious purchases of pseudoephedrine tablets."). We cannot say that NPLEX records are "created or given with the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony." Garcia at id. ; United States v. Collins , 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir.2015) (holding that a similar "MethCheck" record was "not clearly testimonial in nature," and stating that "it is improbable that a pharmacy employee running a standard identification check of a customer would have anticipated that the records of that transaction would later be used against these particular defendants at trial."). We, therefore, reject Ms. Kittle's contentions that her rights under the Confrontation Clause were implicated, and that a de novo standard of review applies.
{¶ 11} We now turn to Ms. Kittle's argument that the trial court erred by allowing Officer Lemonier to lay the foundation for the NPLEX records. This Court reviews a trial court's decision to admit a business record into evidence for an abuse of discretion. State v. Baker , 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 9 ; Coleman at ¶ 35-42 (acknowledging that a NPLEX record is a business record). "A trial court is deemed to abuse its discretion where it admits a business record when the party lays an inadequate foundation to establish its admissibility in accordance with Evid.R. 803(6)." Baker at ¶ 9.
{¶ 12} Evidence Rule 803(6) provides that a business record is excepted from the hearsay rule if it is "kept in the course of a regularly conducted business activity, and if it was the regular practice of that business activity to make the * * * record * * *, all as shown by the testimony of the custodian or other qualified witness or as provided by Rule 901(B)(10)[.]" "A 'qualified witness' for this purpose would be someone with 'enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business.' " (Citation omitted.) State v. Hood , 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, quoting 5 McLaughlin, Weinstein's Federal Evidence , Section 803.08[8][a] (2d Ed.2009). Notably, "[t]he witness whose testimony establishes the foundation for a business record need not have personal knowledge of the exact circumstances of preparation and production of the document." Baker at ¶ 11, citing Evid.R. 803(6). The witness, however, must "demonstrate that he or she is sufficiently familiar with the operation of the business and with the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record in order to reasonably testify on the basis of this knowledge that the record is what it purports to be, and was made in the ordinary course of business." Id. , quoting Keeva J. Kekst Architects, Inc. v. George Dev. Group , 8th Dist. Cuyahoga No. 70835, 1997 WL 253171, *5 (May 15, 1997).
{¶ 13} Here, Officer Lemonier testified that, through his training as a law enforcement officer, he has personal knowledge as to how pharmacies use the NPLEX system to track purchases of pseudoephedrine. He testified that he applied for-and was granted-a license to access the system, which requires a username and password. He indicated that he accesses the NPLEX system every day, and that he *1245receives real-time alerts for pseudoephedrine purchases.
{¶ 14} Regarding the process for electronically tracking purchases, Officer Lemonier explained that a person attempting to buy pseudoephedrine must provide an ID, which is then swiped. As soon as the ID is swiped, the information contained thereon is immediately transmitted to the NPLEX database. Within a few minutes, more specific information is transmitted, including the brand name, pill count, grams of pseudoephedrine contained in the package, and whether the purchase was accepted or denied. He stated that a purchase will be denied if the individual attempts to purchase more than nine grams of pseudoephedrine during a 30-day period.
{¶ 15} Officer Lemonier testified that he personally obtained the NPLEX record for Ms. Kittle, which was introduced as an exhibit, and that the record is kept in the ordinary course of business. To obtain the record, he entered Ms. Kittle's name, date of birth, and driver's license number into the NPLEX system, which then generated a list of pseudoephedrine purchases associated with that information. Officer Lemonier then testified as to the purchases of pseudoephedrine reflected in the NPLEX record.
{¶ 16} While not every police officer may have familiarity with the NPLEX system or the ability to explain how those records came into existence in the ordinary course of business, the record reflects that Officer Lemonier did, in fact, have this knowledge. He, therefore, was a "qualified witness" for purposes of Evidence Rule 803(6). Thus, we cannot say that the trial court abused its discretion when it admitted the NPLEX records into evidence. Accordingly, Ms. Kittle's first assignment of error is overruled.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II
THE TRIAL COURT ERRED WHEN IT ABDICATED ITS DUTIES AS A NEUTRAL AND IMPARTIAL FINDER OF FACT BY DIRECTING THE PROSECUTION TO SUPPLEMENT ITS EVIDENCE AGAINST MS. KITTLE MID-TRIAL, IN VIOLATION OF HER CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS TO A FAIR TRIAL AND DUE PROCESS UNDER ARTICLE 1, SECTION 10 OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE FIFTH, SIXTH AND FOURTEENTH AMENDMENTS TO THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 17} In her second assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court abdicated its duties as a neutral and impartial finder of fact by directing the State to supplement its evidence against her at trial. Similar to her first assignment of error, she contends that a de novo standard of review applies, arguing that the trial court's actions deprived her of a fair trial. We disagree that a de novo standard of review applies, and otherwise find no merit in her argument.
{¶ 18} Evidence Rule 614(B) provides that "[t]he court may interrogate witnesses, in an impartial manner, whether called by itself or by a party." As this Court has stated, " '[t]he evident purpose of Evid. R. 614(B) is to prevent the trial judge, in questioning the witness, from conveying to a jury the judge's impression as to the credibility or lack of credibility of a witness.' " State v. Grad , 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44, quoting State v. Armstrong , 2d Dist. Montgomery No. 13498, 1993 WL 294834, *5 (Aug. 6, 1993). When, as is the case here, the matter is proceeding as a bench trial, the trial judge is afforded greater flexibility in questioning a witness because there is no risk of prejudicially *1246influencing a jury. Id. " 'In absence of any showing of bias, prejudice, or prodding of a witness to elicit partisan testimony, it will be presumed that the trial court acted with impartiality [in propounding to the witness questions from the bench] in attempting to ascertain a material fact or to develop the truth.' " (Alterations sic.) State v. Baston , 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), quoting Jenkins v. Clark , 7 Ohio App.3d 93, 98, 454 N.E.2d 541 (2d Dist.1982). "In reviewing any alleged prejudicial errors resulting from the court's own interrogation of a witness, a reviewing court will examine the questions in light of the entire record and, based upon the totality of the circumstances, determine whether there has been a manifest abuse of discretion." Grad at ¶ 43, quoting State v. Hoover , 9th Dist. Medina No. 1549, 1987 WL 12247, *2 (June 3, 1987).
{¶ 19} Here, when cross-examining Officer Lemonier, Ms. Kittle's counsel pointed out that the NPLEX record did not contain certain information, such as the form of identification used to purchase the pseudoephedrine. Officer Lemonier indicated that if he logged into the NPLEX system and clicked on a certain field, that information would populate onto the record, which he could then print. He further indicated that it was not his typical practice to print that information, and that it had never been an issue in prior trials that he has been involved in.
{¶ 20} The trial court noted that it was "engaged in a matter to find the truth[,]" and did not fault the State for failing to provide an expanded version of the NPLEX record. The trial court then allowed Officer Lemonier to print an expanded version, which included the missing information. Despite the trial court's willingness to allow Ms. Kittle's counsel a few days to review the record and prepare accordingly, her counsel elected to proceed as scheduled and chose not to cross-examine Officer Lemonier on the expanded record.
{¶ 21} Based upon the totality of the circumstances present in this case, we cannot say that the trial court's actions resulted in a manifest abuse of discretion. Id. at ¶ 43. Accordingly, Ms. Kittle's second assignment of error is overruled.
ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III
THE TRIAL COURT COMMITTED REVERSIBLE ERROR WHEN IT AMENDED THE INDICTMENT ON THE MORNING OF TRIAL TO EXPAND THE SINGLE DATE DETERMINED BY THE GRAND JURY TO INCLUDE THE ENTIRE PREVIOUS WEEK, IN VIOLATION OF HER CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS TO DUE PROCESS AND INDICTMENT BY A GRAND JURY UNDER ARTICLE 1, SECTION 10 OF THE OHIO CONSTITUTION AND THE FIFTH, SIXTH, AND FOURTEENTH AMENDMENTS OF THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.
{¶ 22} In her third assignment of error, Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court erred by allowing the State to amend the timeframe of the indictment to include the previous week. We disagree.
{¶ 23} "We review a trial court's decision to allow the amendment of an indictment for an abuse of discretion." State v. Sauto , 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, ¶ 10. An abuse of discretion implies that the court's attitude is unreasonable, arbitrary or unconscionable. Blakemore v. Blakemore , 5 Ohio St.3d 217, 219, 450 N.E.2d 1140 (1983). To constitute reversible error, the defendant must show that: (1) the trial court abused its discretion; and (2) the amendment prejudiced her defense.
*1247State v. Dudukovich , 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16.
{¶ 24} Here, the indictment listed the date of the illegal-assembly-or-possession-of-chemicals-for-the-manufacture-of-drugs offense as on or about December 2, 2014 (the date the officers discovered the methamphetamine lab). On the morning of trial, the trial court allowed the State to extend the timeframe of the indictment to include the preceding seven days. Despite allowing the State to amend the indictment, the trial court ultimately determined that the evidence indicated that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine on November 30, 2014, that she gave those pills to J.S. so that J.S. could make methamphetamine, and that methamphetamine was made on December 2, 2014. The trial court's journal entry indicates that it found Ms. Kittle guilty based upon the December 2, 2014, date only. Thus, even assuming without deciding that the trial court abused its discretion, Ms. Kittle cannot establish that she suffered prejudice and, consequently, cannot establish reversible error. Id. at ¶ 16. Ms. Kittle's third assignment of error is overruled.
III.
{¶ 25} Ms. Kittle's assignments of error are overruled. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.
Judgment affirmed.

### I. INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Appellant, Dana Kittle, respectfully submits this defense opening response in opposition to the prosecution's arguments and in support of affirming the appellate court's reversal of her conviction. The prosecution has failed to rebut the substantial constitutional and evidentiary errors committed below, and this Court should find that the trial court committed reversible error in (1) admitting NPLEX records through an unqualified witness in violation of the Confrontation Clause, (2) abdicating its neutral role as finder of fact by directing the prosecution to supplement evidence, and (3) allowing amendment of the indictment in violation of Ms. Kittle's right to due process and grand jury indictment.

### II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

The facts as established in the record reveal a fundamentally flawed prosecution that relied on circumstantial evidence and improperly obtained evidence to secure a conviction.

On the evening in question, Officer Lemonier and fellow officers conducted surveillance on an apartment believed to be involved in illegal activity. The officers observed Ms. Kittle and J.S. leave the apartment and enter Ms. Kittle's vehicle, with J.S. driving. Following a traffic stop, officers discovered J.S. lacked a driver's license and had an outstanding felony warrant. J.S. was arrested, and officers found three bags of methamphetamine in her purse. Critically, **Ms. Kittle was neither arrested nor searched**, and a search of her pockets revealed no illegal substances.

Officers then returned to the apartment, waited approximately twenty minutes, and were eventually admitted inside. Upon entry, Officer Lemonier smelled the odor associated with methamphetamine production. The occupant consented to a search, revealing methamphetamine production materials and finished product.

Through investigation including an interview with J.S., officers implicated Ms. Kittle. Ms. Kittle was brought to the police station for questioning, where she **admitted** to buying pseudoephedrine for J.S. in exchange for methamphetamine.

The Grand Jury indicted Ms. Kittle on three counts: (1) illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs; (2) illegal manufacture of drugs; and (3) aggravated possession of drugs. The State dismissed counts two and three prior to trial.

The case proceeded as a bench trial where the State presented only Officer Lemonier as a witness, played portions of Ms. Kittle's interview, and introduced NPLEX records. **Ms. Kittle presented no evidence.**

---

### III. ARGUMENT

#### A. THE ADMISSION OF NPLEX RECORDS VIOLATED MS. KITTLE'S CONFRONTATION CLAUSE RIGHTS

The prosecution argues that NPLEX records are nontestimonial business records not subject to Confrontation Clause protections. This Court should reject this argument because the prosecution has misapplied the relevant precedent and ignored the unique circumstances of this case.

In **Ohio v. Clark**, 576 U.S. 237, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 2183, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015), the United States Supreme Court held that "testimonial statements are those made under circumstances which would lead an objective witness reasonably to believe that the statement would be available for use at a later trial." The prosecution cites **State v. Garcia**, 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208 (¶ 58), for the proposition that testimonial statements require "the primary purpose of creating an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony."

However, the prosecution's reliance on **United States v. Collins**, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), is misplaced. In Collins, the Sixth Circuit acknowledged that the MethCheck system at issue was "not clearly testimonial in nature," but this Court should note that the Collins court explicitly stated it was "improbable that a pharmacy employee running a standard identification check of a customer would have anticipated that the records of that transaction would later be used against these particular defendants at trial." This language suggests a case-by-case analysis is required.

Here, the NPLEX system operates under Ohio Revised Code Section 3715.05, which was enacted specifically to combat methamphetamine production. The statutory purpose is explicitly "to monitor suspicious purchases of pseudoephedrine tablets" for law enforcement purposes. See **State v. Coleman**, 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526 (¶ 35). Unlike the routine business records in Collins, NPLEX records are generated specifically because law enforcement has a known interest in tracking precursor chemicals used in illegal drug manufacturing. An objective observer would reasonably understand that these records are created specifically for use in criminal prosecutions.

Furthermore, even assuming arguendo that NPLEX records are business records, **Officer Lemonier was not a qualified witness to lay the foundation for their admission** under Evidence Rule 803(6).

In **State v. Hood**, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057 (¶ 40), the Ohio Supreme Court held that a qualified witness must have "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business." The prosecution argues Officer Lemonier satisfied this requirement because he "accessed the system daily with a licensed account."

This argument fails for several reasons. First, **accessing a database is not equivalent to understanding how the business creates and maintains records**. Officer Lemonier testified that he "applied for and was granted a license to access the system, which requires a username and password." This establishes only that he was authorized to view records—not that he understood the business practices of the pharmacies that input data into the system.

Second, in **State v. Baker**, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697 (¶ 11), this Court held that a witness must "demonstrate that he or she is sufficiently familiar with the operation of the business and with the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record." Officer Lemonier's testimony established only that he understood how to retrieve records from NPLEX—not how pharmacies input data, not how the electronic transmission occurs from pharmacy to database, and not what quality control measures ensure accuracy.

Third, the prosecution conflates law enforcement knowledge with business knowledge. Officer Lemonier's testimony established his familiarity with using NPLEX as an investigative tool, not his familiarity with the record-keeping practices of pharmacies. The witness must demonstrate familiarity with the **business's** record-keeping system, not the police department's access to that system.

Therefore, this Court should find that the trial court abused its discretion in admitting the NPLEX records, and that such admission violated Ms. Kittle's rights under the Confrontation Clause.

---

#### B. THE TRIAL COURT ABDICATED ITS ROLE AS NEUTRAL FINDER OF FACT

The prosecution attempts to minimize the trial court's actions by characterizing them as permissible judicial questioning under Evidence Rule 614(B). However, the prosecution ignores the fundamental principle that a trial court must remain neutral and impartial, particularly when directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence.

In **State v. Baston**, 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), the Ohio Supreme Court stated that it will be presumed the trial court acted with impartiality "in absence of any showing of bias, prejudice, or prodding of a witness to elicit partisan testimony." However, the Baston presumption applies only when the court's questioning is directed at "ascertaining a material fact or to develop the truth."

Here, the trial court's actions went far beyond neutral fact-finding. When Ms. Kittle's counsel pointed out during cross-examination that the NPLEX record lacked certain information, the trial court effectively **directed the prosecution to cure a deficiency in its case**. This is not neutral questioning to develop the truth; this is the trial court assuming an adversarial role and assisting the prosecution in meeting its burden of proof.

The prosecution argues that the trial court's flexibility in a bench trial permits such questioning. However, this argument conflates flexibility with impartiality. Even in a bench trial, **the trial court cannot become an advocate for either party**. See **State v. Grad**, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215 (¶ 44), quoting **State v. Armstrong**, 2d Dist. Montgomery No. 13498, 1993 WL 294834, *5 (Aug. 6, 1993): "The evident purpose of Evid.R. 614(B) is to prevent the trial judge, in questioning the witness, from conveying to a jury the judge's impression as to the credibility or lack of credibility of a witness."

While no jury was present, the principle remains vital. By directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence, the trial court communicated its belief that the prosecution's case was incomplete—a function that should be performed by the defense, not the court.

The prosecution notes that Ms. Kittle's counsel was given the opportunity to review the expanded records and cross-examine Officer Lemonier, but elected not to do so. This does not cure the error. The damage was already done when the trial court directed the prosecution to supplement its evidence. The defense should not be required to demonstrate prejudice from an error that fundamentally altered the nature of the proceedings.

This Court should find that the trial court committed reversible error by abdicating its neutral role as finder of fact.

---

#### C. THE AMENDMENT OF THE INDICTMENT VIOLATED MS. KITTLE'S DUE PROCESS RIGHTS

The prosecution argues that any error in amending the indictment was harmless because the trial court ultimately relied only on the original date. This argument ignores the fundamental constitutional violation that occurred.

Article I, Section 10 of the Ohio Constitution guarantees that no person shall be held to answer for a capital or otherwise infamous crime unless presentment or indictment by a grand jury. Similarly, the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution provides that no person shall be held to answer for a capital or otherwise infamous crime unless on a presentment or indictment of a grand jury.

In **State v. Dudukovich**, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102 (¶ 16), this Court held that to establish reversible error regarding amendment of an indictment, a defendant must show both (1) abuse of discretion by the trial court and (2) prejudice to the defense.

The prosecution argues that because the trial court's journal entry indicates it found Ms. Kittle guilty based only on the December 2, 2014 date, she cannot establish prejudice. This argument is fundamentally flawed.

First, the prosecution's argument assumes the trial court's written findings are accurate and complete. However, the trial court's oral findings at sentencing, combined with its written journal entry, suggest it considered the entire timeframe covered by the amended indictment.

Second, and more fundamentally, **the prejudice in an illegal amendment of an indictment is inherent**. When a grand jury indicts a defendant for conduct occurring on a specific date, the defendant prepares her defense for that specific date. When the prosecution is permitted to amend the indictment to cover an entire week, the defendant must now defend against conduct occurring on any day during that week—a fundamentally different defense.

The prosecution cites **State v. Sauto**, 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922 (¶ 10), for the proposition that amendment of an indictment is reviewed for abuse of discretion. However, Sauto involved amendments to correct typographical errors, not expansions of the temporal scope of the offense.

Here, the indictment originally listed the offense date as "on or about December 2, 2014." The amendment expanded this to include "the preceding seven days"—meaning November 25 through December 2, 2014. This is not a correction of error; it is a substantive expansion of the charge.

Ms. Kittle was charged with illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs under Revised Code Section 2925.041(A). This offense requires the state to prove that Ms. Kittle "possess[ed] any chemical any compound, any mixture, any preparation, any device, or any combination thereof" that may be used in the manufacture of methamphetamine.

If the indictment charged only December 2, 2014, the defense could focus on demonstrating that Ms. Kittle was not present at the apartment on that date and did not participate in the manufacturing process. By expanding the timeframe to seven days, the defense was forced to account for Ms. Kittle's whereabouts on multiple dates—a fundamentally different evidentiary burden.

This Court should find that the trial court abused its discretion in allowing the amendment and that Ms. Kittle was prejudiced thereby.

The defense argues that the NPLEX system is distinguishable from the system at issue in *United States v. The defense relies on *State v. Coleman*, 5th Dist. Richland No. 14-CA-82, 2015-Ohio-3907, 2015 WL 5608526, ¶ 35, to assert that the system is designed to "monitor suspicious purchases."

The prosecution correctly responds that the "primary purpose" test articulated in *Ohio v. Clark*, 576 U.S. 237, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015), and *State v. Garcia*, 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208, ¶ 58, focuses on whether the record was created to serve as a substitute for trial testimony. As held in *United States v. Collins*, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), it is improbable that a pharmacy employee entering data into a tracking system anticipates that the record will be used against a specific defendant in a future trial. The fact that the system aids law enforcement does not transform a routine retail transaction record into a testimonial statement. The defense’s emphasis on the system's secondary utility to law enforcement does not alter the primary purpose of the record's creation at the point of sale. Therefore, the records are nontestimonial and the Confrontation Clause was not violated.

**B. Business Record Foundation**
The defense contends that Officer Lemonier was not a "qualified witness" under Evid.R. 803(6) because he lacked knowledge of the pharmacies' internal record-keeping procedures, distinguishing him from a custodian of records. The defense relies on *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, and *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11.

The prosecution counters that Officer Lemonier demonstrated sufficient familiarity with the NPLEX system itself, which is the relevant record-keeping system for these electronic logs. Under *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, a qualified witness need not be the custodian but must have "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business." Officer Lemonier testified regarding the mechanics of the data entry (ID swipe), the electronic transmission to the database, and the system's automatic denial protocols. This testimony established how the record was generated and maintained in the ordinary course of the NPLEX system's operation. The defense's argument that he must know the specific internal procedures of every pharmacy is too restrictive; his familiarity with the electronic system that generates the record satisfies Evid.R. 803(6). The prosecution argues that in a bench trial, the trial judge has greater flexibility to interrogate witnesses to ascertain the truth, provided there is no bias. As stated in *State v. Grad*, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44, a judge is afforded greater latitude in a bench trial because there is no jury to influence.

The record reflects that the defense counsel identified a deficiency in the record, and Officer Lemonier stated he could access the missing information. Here, the court’s actions were aimed at clarifying the evidence, not assisting the prosecution. The defense has not demonstrated that the court’s conduct was partial or that it resulted in prejudice. The trial court did not commit a manifest abuse of discretion.

Accordingly, Assignment of Error II is overruled.

Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, asserting that the amendment required her to defend against a broader range of conduct.

The prosecution responds that to establish reversible error, the defendant must show both an abuse of discretion and prejudice. *State v. Dudukovich*, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16. The prosecution notes that the trial court’s journal entry explicitly states the conviction was based on the original date of December 2, 2014.

The defense argues that the court’s finding that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine on November 30, 2014, demonstrates prejudice because that date was outside the original indictment. However, the charge was illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs. The evidence of the purchase on November 30 was relevant to the offense committed on December 2, regardless of the precise timeframe listed in the indictment. The trial court’s finding of guilt was predicated on the events of December 2, 2014, as charged in the original indictment. The defense has not shown how the amendment prevented them from preparing a defense to the charge as it stood on December 2. See *State v. Sauto*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, ¶ 10. Without a showing of specific prejudice to the defense of the charged offense, the amendment does not constitute reversible error.

Accordingly, Assignment of Error III is overruled.

Ms. Kittle’s assignments of error are overruled. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.

This Court should find that the NPLEX records are testimonial in nature and that their admission violated Ms. Kittle's rights under the Confrontation Clause.

## II. REPLY TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT ON OFFICER LEMONIER AS A QUALIFIED WITNESS

The prosecution claims that Officer Lemonier was a "qualified witness" under Evidence Rule 803(6) because he demonstrated familiarity with the NPLEX system. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the prosecution conflates two distinct concepts: (1) familiarity with how to access and use the NPLEX system as an end-user, and (2) knowledge of the record-keeping procedures of the business that creates the records. As this Court recognized in *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, a qualified witness must have "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business."

Officer Lemonier's testimony established only that he knows how to log into NPLEX and retrieve records. He never testified that he has knowledge of how pharmacies maintain their records, how the electronic data is transmitted from individual pharmacies to the NPLEX central database, or the internal procedures of the businesses that create these records. This is insufficient under *Hood*.

In *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11, this Court held that a qualified witness must demonstrate "sufficient familiarity with the operation of the business and with the circumstances of the preparation, maintenance, and retrieval of the record." Officer Lemonier testified only about the retrieval of records from NPLEX—not about the preparation, maintenance, or retrieval procedures of the pharmacies that generate the underlying data.

The prosecution's argument that Officer Lemonier explained "how the record was generated and maintained in the ordinary course of the NPLEX system's operation" misstates the record. The "business" at issue is not NPLEX—it is the pharmacies that sell pseudoephedrine and create the initial records. Officer Lemonier never testified about how pharmacies input data, how they maintain their records, or their internal procedures. He testified only about what happens after data is transmitted to NPLEX.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Hood* is particularly unavailing. In *Hood*, the Ohio Supreme Court required that a qualified witness have knowledge of "the record-keeping system of the business in question." Here, the "business in question" is the pharmacy business that sells pseudoephedrine—not the NPLEX system itself. Officer Lemonier has no knowledge of how individual pharmacies maintain their records.

This Court should find that Officer Lemonier was not a qualified witness and that the trial court abused its discretion in admitting the NPLEX records.

## III. REPLY TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT ON TRIAL COURT CONDUCT

The prosecution attempts to justify the trial court's actions by arguing that in a bench trial, the judge has greater flexibility to "ascertain the truth." However, the prosecution ignores the specific holding in *State v. Grad*, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, which states that even in a bench trial, the court must avoid "bias, prejudice, or prodding of a witness to elicit partisan testimony."

The facts show that the trial court did exactly what *Grad* prohibits. When defense counsel pointed out that the NPLEX record was missing certain information, the trial court did not merely permit Officer Lemonier to answer questions about what the record contained. Rather, the court "directed the prosecution to supplement its evidence" by having Officer Lemonier print an expanded version of the record. This goes beyond clarifying testimony—it is the court actively assisting the prosecution in building its case.

In *State v. Baston*, 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), the Ohio Supreme Court held that reversible error occurs when the trial court's questioning conveys "the judge's impression as to the credibility or lack of credibility of a witness." Here, by directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence, the trial court implicitly conveyed that it believed the prosecution's case was incomplete and needed strengthening.

The prosecution argues that the court "merely allowed" Officer Lemonier to print the expanded record, but the record demonstrates otherwise. The trial court noted that it was "engaged in a matter to find the truth" and specifically directed that the expanded record be printed. This is precisely the kind of judicial activism that Evidence Rule 614(B) and the cases interpreting it were designed to prevent.

The prosecution's argument that the defense was not prejudiced because counsel was offered a continuance is unavailing. The defense was put in the position of having to either request a continuance (which would have delayed the proceedings and potentially prejudiced Ms. Kittle) or proceed with incomplete preparation. This is not a meaningful alternative—it is a Hobson's choice that does not cure the underlying constitutional violation.

This Court should find that the trial court committed reversible error by abdicating its neutral role and assisting the prosecution.

## IV. REPLY TO PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENT ON INDICTMENT AMENDMENT

The prosecution argues that the amendment of the indictment was harmless because the trial court's journal entry states the conviction was based on December 2, 2014. This argument ignores the actual findings of the trial court.

The trial court found that "Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine on November 30, 2014, that she gave those pills to J.S. so that J.S. could make methamphetamine, and that methamphetamine was made on December 2, 2014." The prosecution is correct that the journal entry states the conviction was based on December 2, 2014. However, the trial court's factual findings make clear that the November 30 purchase was essential to the conviction.

Under *State v. Dudukovich*, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16, the defendant must show both an abuse of discretion and prejudice. Here, the amendment expanded the timeframe from a single date to seven days. This expansion allowed the prosecution to introduce evidence of the November 30 purchase—which was not part of the original indictment—and to argue that this evidence supported the charge.

The prosecution's argument that the November 30 purchase was "merely contextual evidence" ignores the fact that the charge is illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs. The assembly of chemicals occurs when the chemicals are acquired. If the trial court found that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine on November 30 and that this purchase was part of the manufacturing process, then the November 30 date was not contextual—it was an essential element of the offense.

The prosecution cites *State v. Sauto*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, for the proposition that expanding the timeframe does not prejudice the defense. However, in *Sauto*, the amendment was to the "date" of the offense, not to the timeframe for evidence. Here, the amendment allowed the prosecution to introduce evidence of conduct that occurred outside the original charging period.

This Court should find that the amendment prejudiced Ms. Kittle's defense and that the trial court committed reversible error.

**FINAL RULING**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense regarding Defendant-Appellant Dana Kittle’s three assignments of error. After careful consideration of the arguments presented, the applicable law, and the record provided, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.

**I. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR I: ADMISSION OF NPLEX RECORDS**

Ms. Kittle argues that the NPLEX records are testimonial and that Officer Lemonier was not a qualified witness to lay the foundation for their admission.

**A. Confrontation Clause**
The defense contends that the NPLEX system is distinguishable from the system at issue in *United States v. Collins*, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), because NPLEX’s primary purpose is law enforcement interdiction, evidenced by its ability to generate real-time alerts for officers. The defense asserts that because officers have licenses to access the system and receive alerts, the records are created for the primary purpose of aiding criminal investigations.

The prosecution correctly responds that the "primary purpose" test articulated in *Ohio v. Clark*, 576 U.S. 237, 135 S. Ct. 2173, 192 L. Ed. 2d 306 (2015), and applied in *State v. Garcia*, 2016-Ohio-4667, 66 N.E.3d 1208, ¶ 58, focuses on whether the record was created to serve as a substitute for trial testimony. The critical inquiry is the purpose of the *record-keeper* at the time the record was made. As held in *United States v. Collins*, 799 F.3d 554, 586 (6th Cir. 2015), it is improbable that a pharmacy employee entering data into a tracking system anticipates that the record will be used against a specific defendant in a future trial. The fact that the system aids law enforcement through real-time alerts does not transform a routine retail transaction record into a testimonial statement. The records are created in the ordinary course of business by pharmacies to facilitate the sale of pseudoephedrine in compliance with statutory regulations. See R.C. 3715.05. The defense’s emphasis on the system's secondary utility to law enforcement does not alter the primary purpose of the record's creation at the point of sale. Therefore, the records are nontestimonial and the Confrontation Clause was not violated.

**B. Business Record Foundation**
The defense contends that Officer Lemonier was not a "qualified witness" under Evid.R. 803(6) because he lacked knowledge of the pharmacies' internal record-keeping procedures, distinguishing him from a custodian of records. The defense argues that Officer Lemonier only testified about accessing the system, not how the pharmacies create or maintain the records.

The prosecution counters that Officer Lemonier demonstrated sufficient familiarity with the NPLEX system itself, which is the relevant record-keeping system for these electronic logs. Under *State v. Hood*, 135 Ohio St.3d 137, 2012-Ohio-6208, 984 N.E.2d 1057, ¶ 40, a qualified witness need not be the custodian but must have "enough familiarity with the record-keeping system of the business in question to explain how the record came into existence in the ordinary course of business." Officer Lemonier testified regarding the mechanics of the data entry (ID swipe), the electronic transmission to the database, and the system's automatic denial protocols. This testimony established how the record was generated and maintained in the ordinary course of the NPLEX system's operation. The defense's argument that he must know the specific internal procedures of every pharmacy is too restrictive; his familiarity with the electronic system that generates the record satisfies Evid.R. 803(6). See *State v. Baker*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 21414, 2003-Ohio-4637, 2003 WL 22047697, ¶ 11. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the records.

Accordingly, Assignment of Error I is overruled.

**II. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR II: TRIAL COURT’S CONDUCT**

Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court abdicated its neutrality by directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence, specifically by having Officer Lemonier print an expanded NPLEX record. The defense characterizes the court's action as "directing the prosecution to supplement its evidence" and claims this violated the court's duty to remain neutral, citing *State v. Grad*, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, and *State v. Baston*, 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999). The defense further argues that the offer of a continuance was a "Hobson's choice" that did not cure the error.

The prosecution argues that in a bench trial, the trial judge has greater flexibility to interrogate witnesses to ascertain the truth, provided there is no bias. As stated in *State v. Grad*, 9th Dist. Medina No. 10CA0003-M, 2012-Ohio-1358, 2012 WL 1076215, ¶ 44, a judge is afforded greater latitude in a bench trial because there is no jury to influence.

The record reflects that the defense counsel identified a deficiency in the record, and Officer Lemonier stated he could access the missing information. The trial court allowed this to ensure a complete factual record. Crucially, the court offered the defense a continuance to review the expanded record, which defense counsel declined. Under *State v. Baston*, 85 Ohio St.3d 418, 426, 709 N.E.2d 128 (1999), reversible error requires a showing of bias or the "prodding of a witness to elicit partisan testimony." Here, the court’s actions were aimed at clarifying the evidence, not assisting the prosecution. The defense has not demonstrated that the court’s conduct was partial or that it resulted in prejudice, particularly given the opportunity to continue the trial which was rejected. The trial court did not commit a manifest abuse of discretion.

Accordingly, Assignment of Error II is overruled.

**III. ASSIGNMENT OF ERROR III: AMENDMENT OF INDICTMENT**

Ms. Kittle argues that the trial court’s amendment of the indictment to expand the timeframe from a single date to a seven-day period prejudiced her defense. The defense relies on *State v. Dudukovich*, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16, and *State v. Sauto*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, asserting that the amendment required her to defend against a broader range of conduct. The defense specifically argues that the trial court's finding that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine on November 30, 2014, demonstrates prejudice because that date was outside the original indictment and constituted an essential element of the offense.

The prosecution responds that to establish reversible error, the defendant must show both an abuse of discretion and prejudice. *State v. Dudukovich*, 9th Dist. Lorain No. 05CA008729, 2006-Ohio-1309, 2006 WL 709102, ¶ 16. The prosecution notes that the trial court’s journal entry explicitly states the conviction was based on the original date of December 2, 2014.

The Court agrees with the prosecution. While the trial court found that Ms. Kittle purchased pseudoephedrine on November 30, 2014, this finding was evidence of the "assembly or possession of chemicals" leading up to the manufacture of drugs. The charge was illegal assembly or possession of chemicals for the manufacture of drugs. The evidence of the purchase on November 30 was relevant to the offense committed on December 2, regardless of the precise timeframe listed in the indictment. The trial court’s finding of guilt was predicated on the events of December 2, 2014, as charged in the original indictment. The defense has not shown how the amendment prevented them from preparing a defense to the charge as it stood on December 2. See *State v. Sauto*, 9th Dist. Summit No. 26404, 2013-Ohio-1320, 2013 WL 1324922, ¶ 10. Without a showing of specific prejudice to the defense of the charged offense, the amendment does not constitute reversible error.

Accordingly, Assignment of Error III is overruled.

**CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court finds that the trial court did not err in admitting the NPLEX records, did not act with partiality, and did not prejudice the defendant by amending the indictment. Ms. Kittle’s assignments of error are overruled. The judgment of the Summit County Court of Common Pleas is affirmed.
