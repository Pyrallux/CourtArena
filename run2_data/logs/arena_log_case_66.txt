# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's argument rests on a fundamentally flawed interpretation of the applicable law. While they correctly note that suppression hearings differ from trials on the merits, they incorrectly conclude that the confidential informant's identity is never relevant to a suppression motion. This Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals because the trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The informant's identity was material to the critical issue of probable cause, the defense raised a legitimate basis for disclosure related to the search's validity, and the prosecution's reliance on alternative evidence of reliability does not negate the defendant's right to challenge that reliability through disclosure.

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

On the day in question, Appellant was charged with possession of a controlled substance (cocaine). The charges arose from information provided by a confidential informant. Appellant filed a motion to suppress the evidence on the basis that it was illegally obtained, and separately filed a motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant.

The motion to disclose the informant's identity was based on two grounds: first, that the informant's identity was needed to show that the officers who conducted the arrest and search did not have probable cause based upon a reliable and credible informant, and second, that the informant's identity was necessary because the informant was a material witness to the defendant's guilt or innocence and upon the possible defense of entrapment.

Defense counsel represented to the court that the confidential informant was with Appellant on the day in question, that the informant's belongings were found in Appellant's car when it was released from evidence, and that the informant may have set Appellant up—matters critical to Appellant's defense. Defense counsel further represented that new information regarding the confidential informant had been received "yesterday" and that this new information warranted reconsideration of the disclosure motion.

During the hearing, the trial judge acknowledged that he had "heard testimony that she was reliable because of the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." However, defense counsel argued that the informant's reliability was still at issue even on a motion to suppress because if the informant set Appellant up, that goes to her reliability and thus to the validity of the probable cause.

The trial court denied both the motion to suppress and the motion for disclosure of the confidential informant's identity. Appellant subsequently pled guilty and received a sentence of sixteen years in accordance with the plea agreement.

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Confidential Informant's Identity Was Material to the Probable Cause Determination

The prosecution incorrectly argues that Roviaro v. United States, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), establishes that disclosure is required only when the informant is a material witness to guilt or innocence. However, this reading of Roviaro is too narrow. The Supreme Court in Roviaro recognized that the informant's privilege "has a limited application," requiring disclosure "when the informant's testimony may be necessary to a fair determination of the cause." 353 U.S. at 60–61. The prosecution's argument conflates "fair determination of guilt" with "fair determination of the cause," but these are distinct concepts.

A suppression hearing determines whether evidence was lawfully obtained—a "cause" that is fundamentally separate from guilt or innocence. If the informant's identity is necessary to challenge the reliability of the information that formed the basis of probable cause, then disclosure is required even though the hearing does not address ultimate guilt. The prosecution's position would render the informant's identity immune from scrutiny whenever the defense cannot prove that the informant would testify favorably on the issue of guilt—a circular result that defeats the purpose of the Fourth Amendment's protection against unreasonable searches.

The prosecution cites State v. Williams, 839 N.W.2d 779 (Iowa 2013), for the proposition that when the prosecution presents evidence of reliability through other means, disclosure is not required. However, this Court should not adopt such a categorical rule. The Williams holding addresses one circumstance in which disclosure may be unnecessary, but it does not stand for the proposition that alternative evidence of reliability automatically defeats a defendant's right to disclosure. The defendant must have the opportunity to test the prosecution's evidence of reliability, and where the informant's identity is the only means by which the defense can meaningfully challenge that reliability, disclosure is required.

Here, the trial court heard testimony about the informant's "past accurate tips," but defense counsel was never afforded the opportunity to test this testimony by learning the informant's identity. The officer's conclusory assertion of reliability is not a substitute for the defense's right to investigate whether that reliability actually existed. As defense counsel argued below, the informant's alleged role in "setting up" Appellant directly bears on her reliability. Without disclosure, the defense cannot determine whether the informant was a credible source or whether her information was merely a fabrication.

### B. The Defense Raised a Legitimate Basis for Disclosure Distinct From Guilt or Innocence

The prosecution's argument erroneously suggests that any issue related to the suppression motion is automatically divorced from the informant's identity. This is incorrect. The very purpose of the motion to suppress was to challenge whether the officers had probable cause to conduct the search. The informant's identity was directly relevant to this challenge because it would allow the defense to investigate whether the informant was, in fact, reliable.

The prosecution argues that suppression hearings are "narrowly focused on constitutional violations in evidence collection, not on the defendant's ultimate culpability." This is true, but it actually supports the defense's position. The Fourth Amendment inquiry is whether the search was reasonable. If the probable cause was based on an unreliable informant, the search was unreasonable. The informant's identity is therefore material to the Fourth Amendment inquiry, even if it is not material to the question of guilt.

The prosecution's reliance on United States v. Russell, 411 U.S. 423 (1973), for the proposition that entrapment is irrelevant to a suppression hearing is a misapplication of that case. Russell held that entrapment is not a defense to the admissibility of evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment. 411 U.S. at 428. However, this does not mean that evidence of entrapment is irrelevant to the question of whether probable cause existed. If the informant induced the defendant to commit the crime, this bears on whether the informant's information was reliable and whether the officers reasonably relied on it.

### C. The Defense Was Entitled to Put On a Complete Defense

Defense counsel below cited Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), for the proposition that the defense was entitled to put on a true defense. In Holmes, the Supreme Court held that a state rule excluding evidence of third-party guilt if the prosecution has a forensic DNA exclusion violates the defendant's right to present a complete defense. 547 U.S. at 331. While Holmes addressed third-party guilt evidence, its reasoning applies more broadly: defendants have a constitutional right to present defenses that are relevant to their guilt or innocence.

Here, the informant's identity was relevant to multiple defenses. First, it was relevant to the probable cause determination. Second, it was relevant to the entrapment defense, which, although not a defense to the admissibility of evidence, is a defense to guilt that the defendant is entitled to raise at trial. The prosecution cannot simultaneously force the defendant to resolve all suppression issues before trial while also denying the defendant the evidence needed to prepare a complete defense.

The prosecution's argument that the "dispositive" nature of the motion to suppress somehow bars the disclosure motion is without merit. The agreement between the parties, as described at the hearing, was that the motion to suppress would be dispositive in the sense that if the court ruled in the prosecution's favor, the defendant would plead. However, defense counsel represented that new information regarding the confidential informant had been received after this agreement was made. The trial court should have allowed the defense to present this new information rather than summarily denying the disclosure motion.

## IV. REBUTTAL OF PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's argument fails for several additional reasons.

First, the prosecution incorrectly characterizes the suppression hearing as a proceeding in which the informant's identity is categorically irrelevant. The very purpose of a suppression hearing is to determine whether the search was lawful. If the search was based on information from an unreliable informant, the search was unlawful, and the evidence must be suppressed. The informant's identity is therefore directly relevant to the suppression inquiry.

Second, the prosecution's reliance on State v. Williams is misplaced. In Williams, the court held that disclosure was not required because the prosecution presented evidence of reliability through other means. 839 N.W.2d at 785. However, the court did not hold that such evidence automatically suffices to defeat a disclosure motion. The defendant in Williams was able to cross-examine the officer about the informant's reliability. Here, by contrast, the defense was denied any opportunity to investigate the informant's identity or to test the officer's assertions about her reliability through cross-examination or independent investigation.

Third, the prosecution's argument that the informant's identity is only required when the informant is a "material witness to guilt or innocence" misreads Roviaro. The Supreme Court in Roviaro held that disclosure is required when the informant's testimony may be necessary to a fair determination of the cause. 353 U.S. at 60–61. The "cause" in this case is the lawfulness of the search. The informant's identity is necessary to a fair determination of whether probable cause existed.

Finally, the prosecution's argument that the defendant waived his right to disclosure by agreeing to a dispositive motion to suppress is without support in the record. Defense counsel represented that new information had been received after the agreement was made. The trial court should have allowed the defense to present this information rather than denying the disclosure motion outright.

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and remand the case to the trial court with instructions to grant the motion to disclose the confidential informant's identity. The informant's identity was material to the probable cause determination, and the defense was entitled to investigate the informant's reliability. The prosecution has not met its burden of proving that disclosure was unnecessary, and the trial court erred in denying the motion.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the conviction and remand for further proceedings consistent with this brief.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND PROCEDURAL POSTURE**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court erred in denying the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant was charged with possession of a controlled substance and filed a motion to suppress evidence, arguing it was illegally obtained. He also filed a motion to disclose the CI’s identity, asserting it was necessary to challenge the CI’s reliability regarding probable cause and to support a potential entrapment defense. The hearing on the motion to suppress was designated as "dispositive," pursuant to an agreement where a ruling in favor of the State would result in a guilty plea and a ruling in favor of the defense would result in a dismissal. The trial court denied both motions, and the Appellant subsequently pled guilty.

**II. STANDARD FOR DISCLOSURE**

The privilege of the State to withhold the identity of a confidential informant is not absolute. The Supreme Court in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957), established that disclosure is proper when the informant’s identity is relevant to the defense of the accused and is necessary to a fair determination of the cause. However, this standard must be applied within the specific context of the proceeding. Here, the proceeding was a suppression hearing focused on the legality of the search, not a trial on the merits of guilt or innocence.

**III. APPLICATION TO THE SUPPRESSION HEARING**

The prosecution correctly argues that the suppression hearing was narrowly confined to the question of whether the police had probable cause to conduct the search. The "dispositive" nature of the agreement further limited the scope of the hearing, precluding it from serving as a forum to adjudicate defenses like entrapment that pertain to guilt.

The trial court denied the motion to disclose based on evidence that the CI had provided reliable information in the past. As the prosecution noted, the officer testified to the "effectiveness of her past tips" and their "accuracy." Under the precedent of *State v. Williams*, 839 N.W.2d 779, 785 (Iowa 2013), when the prosecution presents evidence of an informant's reliability through means other than the informant's direct testimony, disclosure of the informant's identity is not required. The trial court found this evidence sufficient to establish probable cause. The defense's claim of "new information" received the day before the hearing does not overcome the State's showing of reliability, nor does it demonstrate that the CI's identity was the sole means to challenge the probable cause. The judge’s determination that the past tips established reliability was a factual finding supported by the record.

**IV. DISTINCTION BETWEEN ENTRAPMENT AND PROBABLE CAUSE**

The defense contends that the CI "set him up" and that this fact is critical to the CI’s reliability. However, the defense’s description of the CI being with the Appellant and her belongings being found in his car suggests a scenario of entrapment or inducement, rather than a fabrication of the tip provided to law enforcement.

As held in *United States v. Russell*, 411 U.S. 423, 428 (1973), the defense of entrapment is not relevant to the question of whether evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment. Entrapment is a defense to the crime itself, not to the legality of the search or seizure. Because the hearing was limited to the dispositive issue of probable cause, the trial court properly excluded this line of inquiry. The defense’s reliance on *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), regarding the right to present a complete defense, is inapplicable here; the right to present a defense does not transform a suppression hearing into a trial on the merits where the informant's identity must be revealed to explore entrapment theories.

**V. CONCLUSION**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The State met its burden of establishing the informant's reliability through evidence of past tips, satisfying the standard set forth in *State v. Williams*, 839 N.W.2d 779 (Iowa 2013). The defense's arguments regarding entrapment are irrelevant to the Fourth Amendment inquiry at the suppression hearing, consistent with *United States v. Russell*, 411 U.S. 423 (1973). Therefore, the trial court’s ruling is affirmed.

**RULING**

The motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant was properly denied. The trial court correctly determined that the State had established the informant's reliability through evidence of past accurate tips, and the defense's entrapment theory was not relevant to the probable cause determination at the suppression hearing. The judgment of the trial court is affirmed.

### I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF POSITION

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's arguments and in support of reversing the trial court's denial of the motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant. The prosecution's arguments conflate distinct legal standards and ask this Court to adopt an interpretation of the privilege that would render it absolute—a result explicitly rejected by the United States Supreme Court in *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957).

---

### II. THE INFORMANT'S IDENTITY WAS RELEVANT AND NECESSARY TO CHALLENGE PROBABLE CAUSE

The prosecution correctly acknowledges that *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), establishes that disclosure is required when the informant's identity is relevant to the defense and necessary to a fair determination of the cause. However, the prosecution erroneously argues that the "cause" before the trial court was narrowly confined to the legality of the search under the Fourth Amendment. This argument ignores the fundamental reality that the very question of legality—whether the search was supported by probable cause—depends entirely upon the reliability of the confidential informant's tip.

The defense presented the motion to disclose precisely because the informant's identity was necessary to challenge the probable cause determination. As defense counsel explained during the hearing, "the confidential informant's reliability is important even on a Motion to Suppress, even on a Motion to Suppress." The prosecution's attempt to silo the probable cause inquiry from the reliability of the informant is logically circular: probable cause cannot be assessed without examining the basis for the informant's tip, and the informant's reliability cannot be assessed without knowing who the informant is.

The prosecution cites *United States v. Russell*, 411 U.S. 423 (1973), for the proposition that entrapment defenses are irrelevant to Fourth Amendment analysis. This is a misapplication of *Russell*. In *Russell*, the Supreme Court held that evidence of government participation in a crime is not relevant to a motion to suppress under the Fourth Amendment because the Fourth Amendment concerns the legality of the search, not the defendant's criminal intent. *Russell*, 411 U.S. at 428. However, the defense in this case does not seek to introduce the informant's conduct to establish an entrapment defense at trial. Rather, the defense seeks to disclose the informant's identity to challenge the reliability of the tip that established probable cause for the search. These are distinct inquiries.

The defense argued below that "the CI was with him that day, her belongings were found in his car when it was released from evidence." This evidence goes directly to the informant's credibility and potential bias—not as a substantive defense to the charge, but as evidence bearing on whether the tip was worthy of belief. The prosecution's argument that this is "entrapment" mischaracterizes the defense's position. The question is not whether the informant induced the defendant to commit a crime; the question is whether the informant's tip was reliable enough to support probable cause. An informant who has personal involvement in the case, whose belongings are found in the defendant's possession, and who may have "set him up" has a motive to provide inaccurate information to law enforcement. This directly impacts the reliability assessment.

---

### III. THE STATE FAILED TO MEET ITS BURDEN OF ESTABLISHING RELIABILITY

The prosecution contends that officer testimony regarding the informant's "past accurate tips" was sufficient to establish reliability under *State v. Williams*, 839 N.W.2d 779 (Iowa 2013). The defense submits that this characterization overstates the record and misapplies the legal standard.

The trial court's ruling states that the testimony established reliability because of "the effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." However, as the defense argued below, this testimony was "notably thin" and generalized. The prosecution has not identified any specific instances of past reliability, any successful investigations resulting from the informant's tips, or any corroborating evidence supporting the officer's conclusory assertions.

In *State v. Williams*, the Iowa Supreme Court held that "when the prosecution presents evidence of an informant's reliability through means other than the informant's direct testimony, disclosure is not required." 839 N.W.2d at 785. However, *Williams* does not stand for the proposition that bare, unsubstantiated assertions of reliability are sufficient. The prosecution bears the burden of establishing probable cause, and when the sole basis for probable cause is an informant's tip, the reliability of that tip must be supported by more than generalized assertions of past performance.

Here, the defense sought to probe the informant's reliability by examining her identity and potential bias. The prosecution cannot satisfy its burden of establishing probable cause through conclusory testimony while simultaneously preventing the defense from testing the accuracy of that testimony. The "new information" regarding the informant's belongings in the defendant's car and her presence during the alleged crime directly undermines the assertion of reliability. If the informant was personally involved in the case, her "past tips" become less relevant to assessing the reliability of the tip in this specific case.

### IV. THE "DISPOSITIVE" NATURE OF THE HEARING DID NOT LIMIT THE DEFENSE'S RIGHTS

The prosecution places significant weight on the agreement that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive." The defense acknowledges this agreement but submits that it does not operate to extinguish constitutional protections.

The purpose of a "dispositive" motion is to resolve the case efficiently based on a single legal issue. However, the agreement did not—and could not—contract away the defense's right to challenge the foundational basis for the search. As defense counsel explained, "the confidential informant's reliability is important even on a Motion to Suppress." The defense was not seeking to litigate guilt or innocence; it was seeking to establish that the search itself was unlawful because it was not supported by probable cause.

The prosecution's argument that the defense should have proceeded to trial if it wished to challenge the informant's identity fundamentally misunderstands the nature of the suppression hearing. If the evidence obtained through the search is suppressed, there may be no trial at all. The defense has every incentive to challenge the legality of the search before trial, and the prosecution cannot bootstrap a "dispositive" agreement into a blanket privilege against disclosure.

### V. THE DEFENSE'S RELIANCE ON *HOLMES V. SOUTH CAROLINA* IS APPROPRIATE

The prosecution dismisses the defense's reliance on *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), as misplaced. However, the principles underlying *Holmes* support the defense's position.

In *Holmes*, the Supreme Court held that a defendant has a constitutional right to present a complete defense, including evidence that another person committed the crime. *Holmes*, 547 U.S. at 324. While *Holmes* addressed third-party guilt evidence at trial, the underlying principle—that the defense must have the opportunity to challenge the State's evidence—is equally applicable to suppression hearings.

Here, the defense sought to challenge the reliability of the informant's tip, which was the sole basis for probable cause. The informant's potential involvement in the case, her presence with the defendant, and the discovery of her belongings in his car are directly relevant to assessing whether the tip was reliable. The defense cannot present this evidence without knowing the informant's identity.

The prosecution argues that suppression hearings are "limited proceedings" that do not require the same breadth of inquiry as trials. This is true as to the scope of the hearing—suppression hearings address the legality of the search, not the defendant's guilt. However, within that scope, the defense has the right to fully challenge the State's evidence. The prosecution cannot limit the defense's ability to challenge probable cause while simultaneously relying on that probable cause to support the search.

The "dispositive" nature of the hearing does not operate to shield the informant's identity from scrutiny, and the prosecution's reliance on *Russell* is misplaced because the defense is not raising an entrapment defense—it's challenging the foundational basis for the search.

The informant's identity is relevant and necessary to the defense's challenge to probable cause. The trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose, and this Court should reverse that ruling and remand for further proceedings.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION AND PROCEDURAL POSTURE**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from the trial court’s denial of the Appellant’s motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant was charged with possession of a controlled substance and filed a motion to suppress evidence, arguing it was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment. He also moved to disclose the CI’s identity, asserting it was necessary to challenge the CI’s reliability regarding probable cause and to support a potential defense of entrapment. The hearing on the motion to suppress was conducted pursuant to a "dispositive" agreement, wherein a ruling for the State would result in a guilty plea and a ruling for the defense would result in a dismissal. The trial court denied both motions, and the Appellant subsequently pled guilty. Having reviewed the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms the trial court’s ruling.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The decision to withhold the identity of a confidential informant rests within the sound discretion of the trial court. *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60–61 (1957). An appellate court will not reverse such a decision absent a clear abuse of that discretion. The privilege of the State to withhold the informant's identity is not absolute; however, it prevails unless the disclosure is "relevant and helpful to the defense of an accused, or is essential to a fair determination of a cause." *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 60–61.

**III. SUFFICIENCY OF THE STATE’S EVIDENCE OF RELIABILITY**

The central issue is whether the trial court abused its discretion in finding that the State established the CI’s reliability without disclosing the CI’s identity. The defense argues that the State’s evidence was "notably thin" and that the officer’s testimony regarding the CI’s "effectiveness" and "accuracy" was too generalized to satisfy the legal standard. The defense relies on *State v. Williams*, 839 N.W.2d 779 (Iowa 2013), to argue that the State must provide specific instances of reliability.

However, the prosecution correctly applies *State v. Williams*, which holds that when the State presents evidence of an informant's reliability through means other than the informant's direct testimony, disclosure is not required. 839 N.W.2d at 785. The trial court heard testimony regarding the CI’s history of providing accurate tips and found this sufficient to establish probable cause. The defense’s contention that this testimony was "thin" is essentially a request for this Court to re-weigh the evidence, which is not the role of an appellate court reviewing a discretionary ruling. The trial court’s finding that the CI’s track record established reliability is supported by the record and is not an abuse of discretion.

Furthermore, the defense’s "new information"—that the CI’s belongings were found in the Appellant’s car and that she was with him on the day in question—does not undermine the *historical* reliability of the CI’s past tips. While this information may suggest the CI’s involvement or bias in this specific instance, it does not contradict the officer’s testimony regarding the CI’s past performance. Because probable cause can be based on the totality of the circumstances, including an informant’s history of reliability, the State met its burden under *State v. Williams*.

**IV. DISTINCTION BETWEEN ENTRAPMENT AND PROBABLE CAUSE**

The defense argues that the CI "set him up" and that this fact is critical to assessing the CI’s reliability, rather than merely constituting an entrapment defense. The prosecution correctly counters that this argument is barred by *United States v. Russell*, 411 U.S. 423 (1973). In *Russell*, the Supreme Court held that the defense of entrapment is irrelevant to the question of whether evidence was obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment. 411 U.S. at 428.

The defense attempts to frame the "set up" as a credibility issue affecting probable cause. However, the factual basis for this argument—the CI’s presence and her belongings in the car—primarily suggests participation in the crime or inducement. Even if this conduct theoretically impacts the CI’s credibility, the trial court properly excluded this line of inquiry because the "dispositive" hearing was limited to the legality of the search. The prosecution correctly argues that the defense cannot bootstrap an entrapment argument into a probable cause hearing by relabeling it as a reliability issue. The reliability of a tip for probable cause purposes is generally assessed by the informant's history with law enforcement, not by their conduct during the specific investigation, unless that conduct directly falsifies the information provided. Here, the defense has not shown that the CI's presence falsified the tip that led to the search.

**V. INAPPLICABILITY OF *HOLMES V. SOUTH CAROLINA***

The defense relies on *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), to argue that the "dispositive" nature of the hearing did not extinguish the right to present a complete defense. The prosecution correctly distinguishes *Holmes*, which addresses the admissibility of third-party guilt evidence at trial, not the disclosure of informants at a pre-trial suppression hearing. *Holmes*, 547 U.S. at 324. The right to present a complete defense at trial does not override the State’s privilege to protect the flow of information by withholding an informant’s identity when, as here, the informant’s testimony is not essential to the determination of probable cause. The "dispositive" agreement properly limited the scope of the hearing to the Fourth Amendment question, and *Holmes* does not expand that scope to include substantive defenses.

**VI. CONCLUSION**

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The State met its burden of establishing the informant's reliability through evidence of past tips, satisfying the standard set forth in *State v. Williams*, 839 N.W.2d 779 (Iowa 2013). The defense's arguments regarding the CI's participation and the "new information" regarding her belongings are irrelevant to the Fourth Amendment inquiry at the suppression hearing and are properly characterized as entrapment issues, which are inapplicable under *United States v. Russell*, 411 U.S. 423 (1973). The "dispositive" nature of the motion further limited the scope of the hearing, precluding the broad inquiry into the CI's identity requested by the defense. Therefore, the trial court’s ruling is affirmed.

**RULING**

The judgment of the trial court is affirmed. The motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant was properly denied.
