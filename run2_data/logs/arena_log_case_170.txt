# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction and Statement of the Case

The prosecution's opening argument mischaracterizes the facts and ignores critical distinctions that compel a ruling in favor of the admissibility of the evidence. This was not a case of an officer illegally detaining a motorist and then obtaining coerced consent. Rather, the evidence demonstrates that the defendant was engaged in a *consensual encounter* with law enforcement, during which he voluntarily consented to a search of his vehicle after being explicitly informed that he was free to leave. The prosecution's attempt to frame this as an illegal seizure fails under established Fourth Amendment jurisprudence.

## I. The Encounter Was a Consensual Police Encounter, Not a Seizure

The prosecution erroneously treats the entire interaction as a seizure from its inception. However, the facts establish that the defendant was not seized within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment at the critical moment when he consented to the search.

Under *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544 (1980), a seizure occurs only when, considering all the circumstances, a reasonable person would not feel free to leave. Here, the officer explicitly told the defendant he was "free to go" (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10) after completing all paperwork related to the traffic citation. The defendant's girlfriend was en route to pick him up, and he was waiting on the shoulder of the road for her arrival. The officer testified that the defendant "was not intending to drive anywhere" (Suppression Tr. 19:12-18). At this point, a reasonable person would have felt free to leave.

The prosecution argues that the presence of a backup officer and the activation of lights transformed this into a seizure. However, in *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1 (1989), the Supreme Court recognized that the presence of police officers does not automatically convert a consensual encounter into a seizure. The backup officer arrived as a matter of "normal protocol, especially at night" (Suppression Tr. 20:7-15), and the officer who initiated the contact had not requested additional assistance. The rotating lights served the dual purpose of protecting the officer from oncoming traffic and activating the dashboard camera—a standard procedure unrelated to any attempt to detain the defendant.

Critically, the defendant *did not* attempt to leave after being told he was free to go. Instead, he re-engaged the officer in conversation, asking again about the license seizure form. This voluntary re-engagement further demonstrates that the defendant did not perceive himself as being detained. See *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991) (holding that a consensual encounter does not become a seizure merely because police ask questions or are present).

## II. The Prosecution Fails to Establish an Illegal Pat-Down

The prosecution's central claim—that the officer conducted an unconstitutional pat-down without reasonable suspicion of weapons—is factually unsupported and legally irrelevant to the search that followed.

The facts establish that the officer asked the defendant if he "could ask him a question" (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The defendant agreed. The officer then made a statement about problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia and asked if defendant "had anything that would concern me" (Suppression Tr. 22:23-25). In response, "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle" (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2).

The prosecution conflates this moment with an alleged pat-down. However, the facts do not establish that any pat-down of the defendant's person occurred. The officer's testimony indicates that the defendant *himself* placed his hands on the vehicle, apparently in a gesture indicating willingness to be searched. The officer did not testify that he conducted a pat-down of the defendant's person. The court below did not make a finding that a pat-down occurred, and the prosecution's argument rests on a factual assertion not supported by the record.

Even assuming *arguendo* that a pat-down occurred, the prosecution's reliance on *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), is misplaced. The prosecution argues that the officer had no reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. However, the prosecution conflates the standard for a pat-down with the standard for the search that actually occurred—the search of the vehicle pursuant to consent.

The critical distinction is this: the defendant was not subjected to a Terry frisk based on the officer's unilateral determination. Rather, the defendant *volunteered* to be searched by placing his hands on the vehicle and stating "I can search him" (Suppression Tr. 23:24-24:2). This is not a case where an officer physically searched a detainee without consent. This is a case where a suspect voluntarily offered to be searched.

## III. The Consent to Search the Vehicle Was Voluntary

The prosecution's argument that "consent obtained during the illegal detention was involuntary" fails because there was no illegal detention at the time consent was given.

In *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973), the Supreme Court held that voluntariness of consent must be determined from the totality of the circumstances. The prosecution bears the burden of proving that consent was voluntary. Here, the evidence overwhelmingly establishes voluntariness.

The defendant was explicitly told he was "free to go" (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). His girlfriend was coming to pick him up. He was not under arrest. He had not been physically restrained. The officer's weapon was not drawn (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5), and the officer acknowledged he had "no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4). The defendant was described throughout the encounter as "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative" (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19).

When the officer asked if he could ask a question, the defendant turned around and came back, stating "yes, I can — he would answer my questions" (Suppression Tr. 21:22-25). The officer described the defendant's demeanor in giving consent as "agreeable" (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18). The defendant then voluntarily placed his hands on the side of his vehicle and offered to be searched.

The prosecution relies on *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), for the proposition that "consent to search obtained during an illegal detention is not voluntary." However, Royer is distinguishable. In that case, the defendant was detained in a small room with two detectives, his airline ticket and luggage were in the detectives' possession, and he was not told he was free to leave. Here, by contrast, the defendant was told explicitly that he was free to go, his vehicle was not impounded, and he was waiting for a ride. The prosecution cannot rely on Royer when the factual predicate—an illegal detention—does not exist.

Similarly, the prosecution's citation to *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544 (1980), is unavailing. In Mendenhall, the defendant was confronted by federal agents in an airport terminal, her airline ticket was in the agents' possession, and she was led to a small room. The Court held that the encounter constituted a seizure. Here, the defendant was on the shoulder of a road, his vehicle was present, he had been told he was free to go, and his ride was en route. The factual matrix is entirely different.

## IV. The Prosecution Misapplies United States v. Sharpe

The prosecution invokes *United States v. Sharpe*, 470 U.S. 675 (1985), for the proposition that "a traffic stop must last no longer than necessary to effectuate the purpose of the stop." This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, this was not a traffic stop in the traditional sense. The defendant pulled his vehicle over on his own initiative. The officer testified that "regardless of any observations of the motorist's driving, the officer did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, the car pulled over on its own" (Suppression Tr. 13, 26, 30, 46, 59-60). The officer did not activate his lights until *after* the defendant had already pulled over. The prosecution cannot rely on traffic stop jurisprudence when no traffic stop occurred.

Second, even assuming *arguendo* that the initial encounter constituted a detention, that detention had fully concluded before the consent to search was requested. The officer had completed the license check, written the citation, explained the license forfeiture procedures, and told the defendant he was free to go. The defendant was waiting for his girlfriend. At that point, any investigative purpose had been fulfilled.

The prosecution argues that the officer "had no reasonable suspicion to prolong the detention for further questioning." However, the prosecution conflates the conclusion of one encounter with the beginning of another. Once the defendant was told he was free to go, the detention ended. The subsequent conversation—including the request to ask a question and the request for consent to search—occurred during a new, consensual encounter.

In *United States v. Sullivan*, 138 F.3d 126 (4th Cir. 1998), the Fourth Circuit held that once the purpose of a traffic stop is completed, the officer cannot continue to detain the driver absent additional reasonable suspicion. However, the court also recognized that an officer may approach a driver after a stop has concluded and engage in a consensual encounter. Here, the officer did exactly that. He asked if he could "ask a question," the defendant agreed, and the conversation evolved into a request for consent to search.

## V. The Prosecution's Characterization of the Facts Is Incorrect

The prosecution states that "the officer's search violated the Fourth Amendment" and that "the officer conducted an unconstitutional pat-down of the defendant." These assertions are not supported by the facts.

The officer testified that he had "no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4). He did not conduct a pat-down based on suspicion of weapons. He asked a question, and the defendant offered to be searched. The prosecution cannot manufacture a constitutional violation where none occurred.

The prosecution argues that "aside from [the officer] not believing the story that he is lost, there's no other suspicious activity" (Suppression Tr. 33:16-19). This is true. But the prosecution conflates the absence of suspicious activity with the presence of an illegal search. The defendant was not searched because of suspicious activity. He was searched because he voluntarily consented to a search.

The prosecution also argues that "the officer had no probable cause to search the vehicle without consent." This is correct—but irrelevant. The officer did not search the vehicle without consent. The defendant consented. The prosecution's argument that "any consent to search the vehicle obtained during this illegal detention was involuntary" rests on the false premise that an illegal detention occurred. As demonstrated above, no such detention occurred.

## Conclusion

The prosecution has failed to establish a Fourth Amendment violation. The evidence demonstrates that the defendant was engaged in a consensual encounter with law enforcement after being explicitly told he was free to go. He voluntarily consented to a search of his vehicle. The search was lawful, and the evidence discovered therein is admissible.

The prosecution's argument rests on a mischaracterization of the facts—treating a consensual encounter as an illegal detention, conflating a voluntary offer to be searched with an unconstitutional pat-down, and ignoring the explicit testimony that the defendant was free to leave.

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should deny the motion to suppress and admit the evidence recovered from the defendant's vehicle.

**REBUTTAL TO DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT**  

The defense mischaracterizes the legal framework governing this encounter and ignores critical precedent that renders the vehicle search unconstitutional. The core error in the defense’s argument is its assertion that the officer’s request for consent occurred during a consensual encounter. This is factually and legally untenable.  

### I. The Officer’s Detention of the Defendant Extended Beyond the Scope of a Lawful Traffic Stop  

The defense claims the encounter was consensual because the officer told the defendant he was “free to go.” But this ignores *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002), which clarifies that “a seizure occurs when the officer, by means of physical force or show of authority, has in some way restrained the liberty of a citizen.” Here, the officer’s conduct—activating rotating lights, summoning backup, and initiating a traffic stop despite no traffic violation—created a coercive environment. The officer testified that he “activated his lights, front and back, so the driver would know it was a police officer and as protection in case anyone would come up from behind” (Suppression Tr. 13). The presence of a second patrol car with rotating lights, as confirmed by the officer (Suppression Tr. 48:19-21), further reinforces the objective show of authority.  

Under *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), a person is not free to leave when police “restrain[] his freedom of movement” through “show of authority.” The Supreme Court in *Royer* held that consent obtained during such a restraint is invalid. Here, the officer’s conduct—activating lights, summoning backup, and maintaining control of the defendant’s vehicle—created an environment where a reasonable person would not feel free to leave. This is not a consensual encounter but an illegal detention.  

The defense cites *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1 (1989), but *Sokolow* is inapposite. In *Sokolow*, the Court found no seizure where officers approached a passenger in an airport terminal, asked for identification, and inquired about travel plans. There was no show of force, no physical restraint, and no indication the passenger was not free to leave. Here, the officer initiated contact by activating lights behind a stopped vehicle, summoned backup, and maintained control of the scene. The defendant was not free to leave; he was detained for the purpose of investigating a traffic violation (driving with a suspended license) and potential criminal activity.  

### II. The Officer’s Pat-Down Was Unlawful Under *Terry v. Ohio*  

The defense claims no pat-down occurred because the defendant “volunteered” to be searched. This is a distortion of the facts. The officer testified that after asking defendant if he had “anything that would concern me,” defendant “immediately put his hands on the side of the vehicle” and “offered me to search him” (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). But *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), requires reasonable suspicion of danger *before* an officer may conduct a pat-down for weapons. The officer admitted he had “no safety concerns” (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4) and observed no furtive movements, intoxication, or threats (Suppression Tr. 31:5-11, 33:3-19).  

The officer’s claim that defendant’s “offer” to be searched was voluntary is irrelevant to the legality of the pat-down. *Terry* prohibits officers from conducting a pat-down without reasonable suspicion of danger, regardless of whether the subject consents. As the Supreme Court held in *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366 (1993), “an officer may not seize non-threatening contraband detected through the sense of touch during a pat-down search for weapons unless the officer has probable cause to believe the item is contraband.” Here, the officer had no reasonable suspicion to justify even the initial pat-down. The defendant’s subsequent consent cannot retroactively validate an unconstitutional seizure.  

### III. Consent Obtained During an Illegal Detention Is Involuntary  

The defense relies on *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973), to argue consent was voluntary. But *Schneckloth* explicitly states that “consent to search must be voluntary” and “the prosecution bears the burden of proving voluntariness.” Voluntariness is assessed under the “totality of the circumstances,” including whether the defendant was “free to decline the request.” Here, the defendant was not free to decline because he was unlawfully detained.  

In *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), the Court held that “consent to search obtained during an illegal detention is not voluntary.” The defendant was detained for a traffic violation (driving with a suspended license), which was completed when the officer issued the citation. Yet the officer continued to detain him to ask further questions about his route and “whether he had anything that would concern me.” This prolonged detention was unsupported by reasonable suspicion.  

The defense claims the officer’s question—“can I ask you a question?”—transformed the encounter into a consensual one. But as the Supreme Court clarified in *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544 (1980), “the mere fact that a person is not told he is free to leave does not mean he is not seized.” Here, the officer’s conduct—maintaining control of the scene, summoning backup, and continuing to question the defendant after the traffic stop was complete—created an environment where a reasonable person would not feel free to refuse.  

### IV. The Officer’s Suspicion Was Not Objectively Reasonable  

The defense argues the officer’s suspicion of criminal activity was “not supported by the facts.” This is correct. The officer’s sole basis for suspicion was defendant’s claim of being “lost” and his request for clarification on license forfeiture procedures. But as the officer admitted, “aside from you not believing the story that he is lost, there’s no other, quote, suspicious, end quote, activity” (Suppression Tr. 33:16-19).  

Under *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119 (2000), “unprovoked flight” or “nervous, evasive behavior” may support reasonable suspicion. But here, the defendant was compliant, polite, and cooperative. He had no furtive movements (Suppression Tr. 31:22-25), no signs of intoxication (Suppression Tr. 28:5-9), and no suspicious activity beyond the officer’s subjective disbelief of his explanation. The officer’s belief that defendant’s story was a “ruse” because he asked for clarification on procedures—despite having already explained them—lacks objective basis. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Arvizu*, 534 U.S. 266 (2002), “reasonable suspicion requires specific, articulable facts that, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant suspicion.” A defendant’s request for clarification on procedures, without more, does not meet this standard.  

### Conclusion  

The officer’s detention of the defendant unlawfully extended beyond the scope of the traffic stop. The pat-down was conducted without reasonable suspicion, violating *Terry v. Ohio*. Consent obtained during this illegal detention was involuntary under *Florida v. Royer*. The evidence recovered from the vehicle must be suppressed.

The following factors demonstrate voluntariness:

1. **No coercion**: The officer did not draw his weapon (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5), had "no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4), and described the defendant as "agreeable" (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18).

3. **No prolonged detention**: The entire encounter from initial stop to discovery of evidence was approximately twenty-five minutes, with the traffic-related matters concluded in approximately fifteen minutes.

4. **Initiated by defendant**: The defendant "offered" to be searched "without any kind of probing." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-21). This affirmative gesture demonstrates willingness to cooperate, not submission to authority.

5. **Explicit freedom to leave**: The defendant was told he was free to go and that his ride was coming.

The prosecution's reliance on Florida v. Royer is misplaced because Royer involved a defendant who was not told he was free to leave and was led to a separate room. Here, by contrast, the defendant was explicitly told he was free to go and voluntarily chose to remain and answer questions.

## V. THE PROSECUTION'S REMAINING ARGUMENTS LACK MERIT

### A. The Prosecution Misapplies the Reasonable Suspicion Standard

The prosecution argues the officer lacked reasonable suspicion for a Terry stop. However, the defense does not rely on a Terry stop to justify the search. The search was justified by the defendant's voluntary consent. The prosecution's reasonable suspicion argument conflates two distinct Fourth Amendment analyses.

Even considering the officer's suspicion, the prosecution's argument fails. The officer's subjective beliefs, even if not fully supported by objective facts, do not invalidate a consensual search. As the Supreme Court held in United States v. The officer's fifteen years of experience and his observations about the defendant's route and behavior provided a basis for his suspicions, even if not sufficient for a Terry stop.

### B. The Prosecution Cannot Relitigate Factual Determinations

The trial court credited the officer's testimony that he told the defendant he was free to go, that the defendant offered to be searched, and that the encounter was consensual. These factual findings are supported by the record and should not be disturbed.

The evidence establishes that the officer lawfully concluded the initial detention for the traffic violation and suspended license. The defendant's offer to be searched, and his subsequent consent to search the vehicle, were voluntary and not the product of an illegal seizure. Therefore, the motion to suppress is denied.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## Introduction and Statement of the Issue

The defense respectfully submits that the search of the defendant's vehicle was constitutionally valid and that the motion to suppress should be denied. The prosecution's arguments rest on a fundamental mischaracterization of the facts and a misapplication of Fourth Amendment jurisprudence. The record clearly establishes that the initial seizure concluded before any request for consent to search, that the subsequent encounter was entirely consensual, and that the defendant's consent to search was voluntarily given. The prosecution cannot overcome these factual findings, which are supported by substantial evidence in the record.

## I. The Seizure Lawfully Concluded Before the Request for Consent

The prosecution's primary argument—that the defendant remained detained throughout the encounter—directly contradicts the factual findings credited by the court below. The officer's uncontradicted testimony establishes that after completing the citation and license forfeiture paperwork, he explicitly informed the defendant that he was free to leave. The transcript reflects: "I told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car" (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). This testimony is unambiguous and demonstrates that the officer communicated to the defendant that the detention had concluded.

Under the standard established in United States v. Mendenhall, 446 U.S. 544 (1980), a person is seized only if, in view of all the circumstances surrounding the incident, a reasonable person would have believed that he was not free to leave. Here, the officer's explicit statement that the defendant was "free to go" objectively communicated that the detention had ended. The prosecution's attempt to rely on United States v. Drayton, 536 U.S. 194 (2002), is misplaced because that case addresses whether a seizure occurred at all, not whether a seizure that has lawfully concluded can be extended through the mere presence of police lights or officers. In Drayton, the Supreme Court held that the presence of officers does not constitute a seizure absent evidence of coercion. Here, the officer went further by explicitly telling the defendant he was free to leave.

The prosecution argues that the officer's statement was followed immediately by the defendant re-engaging him, suggesting the detention never truly ended. However, this argument conflates the conclusion of a lawful seizure with the voluntary initiation of a new conversation. As the Eighth Circuit correctly held in United States v. West, 651 F.3d 924 (8th Cir. 2011), where an officer explicitly tells a suspect he is free to leave, subsequent questioning constitutes a consensual encounter. The facts here are even stronger than in West because the officer not only told the defendant he was free to go but actually began walking away from the vehicle.

The prosecution's reliance on United States v. Sharpe, 470 U.S. 675 (1985), is equally unavailing. Sharpe addressed whether an investigative detention was unreasonably prolonged, not whether a detention that has been lawfully concluded can be extended. Here, the officer completed the legitimate purpose of the stop—addressing the suspended license and issuing the citation—before the defendant was told he was free to go. The twenty-minute duration of the encounter was reasonable given the need to run license and warrant checks, complete paperwork, and explain the license forfeiture procedures.

## II. The Subsequent Encounter Was Consensual

The prosecution contends that the defendant's re-engagement with the officer was not truly consensual because it occurred in a coercive environment. This argument fails because it ignores the fundamental principle that a consensual encounter requires only that the defendant voluntarily choose to engage with the officer.

The facts establish that after the officer began walking away, the defendant initiated a new conversation by asking for further clarification on the license seizure form (Suppression Tr. 21:15). The officer then asked, "if I could ask him a question," to which the defendant "turned around and came back to me" and agreed to answer (Suppression Tr. 22:5-10). This sequence demonstrates that the defendant voluntarily chose to continue the interaction.

Under Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429 (1991), police questioning does not constitute a seizure unless the officer's conduct conveys a message that compliance is mandatory. Here, the officer's question—"can I ask you a question?"—was explicitly permissive and invited voluntary participation. The defendant was not ordered to answer; he was asked whether he would be willing to answer a question, and he agreed. The prosecution's reliance on Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), is inapposite because in Royer, the defendant was not told he was free to leave and was effectively detained in a separate room. Here, by contrast, the defendant had been explicitly told he was free to go and had a ride en route.

The prosecution argues that the rotating lights on both police vehicles and the presence of backup created a coercive environment. However, the Supreme Court has held that the presence of officers alone does not constitute a seizure. In United States v. Drayton, 536 U.S. 194 (2002), the Court held that officers may board a bus and ask questions without effecting a seizure so long as they do not convey a message that compliance is required. Here, the officer went further by explicitly stating the defendant was free to go. The rotating lights, which were activated for safety and documentation purposes, do not transform a consensual encounter into a seizure.

The prosecution's characterization of the environment as "coercive" is further undermined by the officer's own testimony. The officer testified that he had "no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4), that the defendant was "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative" (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19), and that he observed no furtive or suspicious movements (Suppression Tr. 31:22-25, 33:16-19, 42:2-43:3). A truly coercive environment would have produced safety concerns or suspicious behavior, but the record shows neither.

## III. The Defendant Voluntarily Consented to the Search

The prosecution's central argument—that the consent to search was involuntary—fails because it assumes the defendant was still detained when he consented. The voluntariness of consent must be assessed under the totality of the circumstances as established in Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218 (1973). The facts here demonstrate voluntary consent.

The defendant initiated the search by offering to be searched. The officer testified that "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle" (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). This affirmative gesture was made after the defendant had been told he was free to go, while the officer was walking away, and in response to a general question about whether the defendant had "anything that would concern me." The defendant's offer to be searched demonstrates a voluntary willingness to cooperate, not a submission to authority.

The prosecution argues that the officer's question about whether the defendant had anything that "would concern me" was coercive. However, this question was asked only after the defendant had voluntarily re-engaged the officer and agreed to answer a question. The question was ambiguous and did not specifically reference weapons or drugs. A reasonable person in the defendant's position would have understood that he could decline to answer or walk away, especially given that he had already been told he was free to go and was waiting for a ride.

The prosecution's attempt to liken this situation to Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218 (1973), is misguided. In Schneckloth, the Court held that consent obtained during an unlawful detention is not voluntary. Here, by contrast, the detention had lawfully concluded before the defendant re-engaged the officer. The prosecution cannot rely on Schneckloth to invalidate consent obtained after a lawful detention has ended.

The prosecution also mischaracterizes the officer's testimony regarding the sequence of events. The prosecution asserts that the officer "asked if he could ask a question" and then immediately asked about "anything that would concern me." However, the record shows that the defendant first asked the officer for clarification on the license seizure form (Suppression Tr. 21:15), and the officer answered that question. Only after completing that explanation did the officer ask if he could ask "a question." The defendant agreed, and only then did the officer ask about "anything that would concern me." This sequence demonstrates a gradual escalation of the conversation initiated by the defendant, not a coercive interrogation.

The totality of the circumstances supports a finding of voluntary consent. The officer had not drawn his weapon, testified he had no safety concerns, and the defendant was described as fully compliant and cooperative. The defendant was not under arrest, was not handcuffed, and had been told he was free to go. He had a ride coming and was not under any compulsion to submit to a search. Under these circumstances, the defendant's offer to be searched was voluntary.

## IV. No Illegal Terry Frisk Occurred

The prosecution argues that the officer conducted an unconstitutional pat-down in violation of Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968). This argument fails because the factual record does not support the claim that a Terry frisk occurred.

The prosecution mischaracterizes the officer's actions by suggesting he conducted a pat-down before asking for consent to search. However, the officer's testimony establishes that the defendant offered to be searched by placing his hands on the vehicle, not that the officer conducted a pat-down. The officer testified: "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle" (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). This language indicates the defendant initiated the search, not the officer.

The prosecution's reliance on Minnesota v. Dickerson, 508 U.S. 366 (1993), is inapplicable because that case presumes the existence of a lawful Terry frisk, which did not occur here. The officer did not conduct a pat-down for weapons; he accepted the defendant's offer to search the vehicle. The prosecution cannot bootstrap a Terry claim onto a consensual search that was initiated by the defendant.

Furthermore, even if a Terry frisk had occurred, the prosecution's argument would fail because the officer had no obligation to have reasonable suspicion before accepting a voluntary offer to be searched. The defendant was free to offer to be searched, and the officer was free to accept that offer. The Fourth Amendment does not require reasonable suspicion before accepting voluntary consent to search.

## V. Rebuttal of Prosecution's Arguments

The prosecution's closing statement mischaracterizes the record in several material respects. First, the prosecution asserts that the officer "never truly concluded the encounter." However, the officer's testimony is unambiguous: "I told him he was free to go" (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). The prosecution cannot rewrite this clear statement to suit its theory.

Second, the prosecution argues that the defendant's re-engagement with the officer demonstrates that the detention never ended. However, this argument ignores the distinction between the conclusion of a seizure and the voluntary initiation of a new conversation. The defendant was free to leave after being told he was free to go. His choice to re-engage the officer was an exercise of his freedom to converse, not evidence of ongoing detention.

Third, the prosecution contends that the officer's question about whether the defendant had "anything that would concern me" was coercive. However, this question was asked after the defendant had voluntarily agreed to answer a question, and the question itself was ambiguous. The defendant was not ordered to answer, and nothing in the question suggested that compliance was mandatory.

Fourth, the prosecution argues that the defendant's "offer" to be searched was actually a response to coercive questioning. However, the record shows that the defendant placed his hands on the vehicle "without any kind of probing" from the officer (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The defendant's initiative demonstrates voluntary cooperation, not submission to authority.

Fifth, the prosecution's reliance on the duration of the encounter is unavailing. The twenty-minute duration was reasonable given the need to run license and warrant checks, complete paperwork, and explain the license forfeiture procedures. The prosecution has cited no authority for the proposition that a reasonable duration of a traffic stop transforms a subsequent consensual encounter into a seizure.

## Conclusion

The defense respectfully submits that the motion to suppress should be denied. The evidence establishes that the officer lawfully concluded the initial detention for the traffic violation and suspended license. The subsequent interaction was a consensual encounter initiated by the defendant. The defendant's offer to be searched, and his subsequent consent to search the vehicle, were voluntary and not the product of an illegal seizure. The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the search violated the Fourth Amendment.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the preliminary ruling denying the motion to suppress and admit the evidence obtained from the defendant's vehicle.

Respectfully submitted,

**FINAL RULING**

Based upon the review of the factual findings, the closing arguments of the prosecution and defense, and the applicable precedent, the motion to suppress is **denied**. The evidence obtained from the defendant's vehicle is admissible.

**ANALYSIS**

This Court is tasked with determining whether the search of the defendant's vehicle violated the Fourth Amendment. The core dispute centers on whether the defendant was seized when he consented to the search, and whether that consent was voluntary. After careful consideration of the arguments presented, the Court finds that the initial seizure lawfully concluded prior to the request for consent, the subsequent encounter was consensual, and the defendant voluntarily consented to the search.

**I. The Seizure Lawfully Concluded Prior to the Request for Consent**

The prosecution argues that the seizure never truly ended because the officer's show of authority—rotating lights and the presence of backup—created a coercive environment that negated the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go." However, the factual findings do not support this conclusion.

The record clearly establishes that the officer completed the citation and license forfeiture paperwork, explained the documents to the defendant, and explicitly informed him that he was free to leave. The officer testified: "I told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). This communication, coupled with the physical act of turning and walking away, objectively signaled the end of the detention.

Under *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544 (1980), a person is "seized" only if, in view of all the circumstances, a reasonable person would not believe he is free to leave. Here, the officer's explicit statement that the defendant was free to go is the most significant circumstance. As the Eighth Circuit held in *United States v. West*, 651 F.3d 924 (8th Cir. 2011), when an officer explicitly tells a suspect he is free to leave, any subsequent questioning constitutes a consensual encounter, not a seizure.

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002), to argue that the presence of multiple officers and rotating lights rendered the detention ongoing, is unpersuasive. In *Drayton*, the Supreme Court held that the presence of officers alone does not constitute a seizure absent coercive conduct. Here, the officer's conduct—stating the defendant was free to go and walking away—negated any coercive effect of the lights or the presence of the backup officer. Therefore, the seizure ended before the officer asked for consent to search.

**II. The Subsequent Encounter Was Consensual**

The prosecution contends that the defendant's re-engagement with the officer was not voluntary because the officer had not yet "concluded" the encounter in a meaningful way. The facts, however, show that the defendant voluntarily initiated the second interaction.

After the officer began walking away, the defendant "engaged me again in conversation" by asking for further clarification on the license seizure form (Suppression Tr. 21:15). The officer answered the question, and then asked, "if I could ask him a question," to which the defendant "turned around and came back to me" and agreed to answer (Suppression Tr. 22:5-10). This sequence of events demonstrates that the defendant was not compelled to interact; rather, he chose to do so.

Under *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991), police questioning does not constitute a seizure unless the officer's conduct conveys a message that compliance is mandatory. Here, the officer's request to ask a question was permissive, and the defendant's response was voluntary. Because the defendant had been told he was free to go and voluntarily re-initiated contact, the encounter was consensual. The prosecution's comparison to *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), is inapposite because in *Royer*, the defendant was never told he was free to leave and was effectively detained.

**III. The Defendant Voluntarily Consented to the Search**

The prosecution argues that the defendant's consent was involuntary because it was the product of a coercive environment and the officer's leading question about whether he had "anything that would concern me." The Court disagrees.

The voluntariness of consent is assessed under the totality of the circumstances. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973). The facts indicate that the consent was voluntary. The officer had not drawn his weapon, testified he had no safety concerns, and the defendant was fully compliant. Critically, the defendant initiated the search by offering to be searched. The officer testified that "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle" (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2).

The prosecution argues that the officer's question, "we have problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia that they should not have," was coercive. However, this question was asked during a consensual encounter, after the defendant had been told he was free to go and had voluntarily agreed to answer questions. A reasonable person in the defendant's position, knowing he was free to leave and waiting for a ride, would have understood that he could refuse to answer or decline the search. The defendant's affirmative gesture of placing his hands on the vehicle and stating "I can search him" demonstrates a voluntary willingness to cooperate, not a submission to authority.

**IV. No Illegal *Terry* Frisk Occurred**

The prosecution argues that the officer conducted an unconstitutional pat-down in violation of *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), because he lacked reasonable suspicion to believe the defendant was armed and dangerous. This argument mischaracterizes the nature of the encounter.

The record does not support the claim that the officer conducted a *Terry* frisk. The officer testified that the defendant *offered* to be searched by placing his hands on the vehicle. Because the search was conducted pursuant to the defendant's voluntary offer and not as a result of a coercive pat-down for weapons, the *Terry* analysis does not apply. The prosecution's citation of *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366 (1993), is inapplicable because that case presumes the existence of a lawful *Terry* frisk, which did not occur here.

**CONCLUSION**

The evidence establishes that the officer lawfully concluded the initial detention for the traffic violation and suspended license. The subsequent interaction was a consensual encounter initiated by the defendant. The defendant's offer to be searched, and his subsequent consent to search the vehicle, were voluntary and not the product of an illegal seizure. Therefore, the motion to suppress is denied.
