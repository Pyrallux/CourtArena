COURT ARENA LOG - Case 187
FACTS:
Defendants-appellants the Argentine Republic (""Argentina"") and YPF S.A. (""YPF"") (together, ""defendants"") appeal an order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New *199York (Preska, J .), denying defendants' motions to dismiss under (1) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on grounds of foreign sovereign immunity and (2) Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) pursuant to the act of state doctrine. We affirm the district court's order insofar as it denied the motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act and we dismiss defendants' appeal as to the act of state doctrine.

BACKGROUND
Unless otherwise noted, the facts herein are undisputed. They are drawn from the complaint and the documents submitted by the parties in reference to defendants' motions to dismiss.

I. YPF Becomes a Publicly Traded Company

YPF is a petroleum company that was wholly owned and operated by the Argentine government until 1993. That year, in accordance with broader efforts to reform its economy, Argentina decided to privatize the petrol firm through an initial public offering (""IPO"") of nearly 100% of YPF's voting stock (the ""shares"").1 Argentina and YPF took a number of steps to entice investors to participate in the IPO and thereby ensure its success, two of which are particularly relevant to this case. First, they arranged for YPF to offer shares in the United States as American Depository Receipts (""ADRs"") listed on the New York Stock Exchange (""NYSE""). Second, they amended YPF's bylaws-that is, the contract governing the relationship among YPF, Argentina (in its capacity as a shareholder), and other YPF shareholders. In particular, the bylaws were amended to incorporate protections for investors from (1) hostile takeovers and (2) attempts by Argentina to renationalize the company. These takeover protections form the basis of this breach of contract dispute, and so we describe them in some detail.

Section 7(d) of the amended bylaws prohibits (with certain exceptions inapplicable here) the direct or indirect acquisition of YPF shares if the acquisition results in the acquirer controlling 15% or more of the shares, unless the acquirer makes a tender offer for all of the outstanding shares in accordance with certain procedures and at a price determined by a formula in the bylaws. Among the prescribed procedures, section 7(f) requires that any such tender offer comply with the rules and regulations imposed by the governments and stock exchanges where YPF's shares are listed. Because YPF's securities were to be listed on the NYSE, those conducting tender offers in accordance with these shareholder protection measures would be compelled by section 7(f) to comply with NYSE and Securities and Exchange Commission (""SEC"") rules and regulations. Section 7(f)(iv) further obligates the acquirer to publish notice of its tender offer ""in the business section of the major newspapers ... in the City of New York, U.S.A. and any other city where the shares [of YPF]
*200shall be listed."" App. 340. Perhaps most importantly for purposes of this appeal, section 28(A) of the bylaws extends the tender offer requirement of sections 7(e) and 7(f) to:
all acquisitions made by the [Government of Argentina], whether directly or indirectly, by any means or instrument, of shares or securities of [YPF], 1) if, as a consequence of such acquisition, the [Government] becomes the owner, or exercises the control of, the shares of [YPF], which, in addition to the prior holdings thereof of any class of shares, represent, in the aggregate, at least 49% of the capital stock [of YPF]; or 2) if the [Government] acquires at least 8% of class D outstanding shares of stock, while withholding class A shares of stock amounting at least to 5% of the capital stock.
App. 432.

The penalties for breaching these provisions are drastic. Section 7(h) provides that ""[s]hares of stock and securities acquired in breach of [the tender offer requirements] shall not grant any right to vote or collect dividends."" App. 342. And section 28(C) extends such treatment to shares acquired by Argentina, unless its breach is accidental. In that case, ""[t]he penalties provided for in subsection (h) of Section 7 shall be limited ... to the loss of the right to vote."" App. 355. At bottom, these shareholder protection measures appear to promise investors a compensated exit from their ownership position in the firm if Argentina were to decide to renationalize YPF.

Argentina and YPF touted these protections in the prospectus filed with the SEC in connection with the IPO. That document stated that ""[u]nder [YPF's] By-laws, in order to acquire a majority of [YPF's] capital stock ..., the Argentine Government first would be required to make a cash tender offer to all holders of [the shares] on terms and conditions specified in the By-laws."" App. 23. The prospectus further stated that ""any Control Acquisition carried out by the Argentine Government other than in accordance with th[at] procedure ... will result in the suspension of the voting, dividend and other distribution rights of the shares so acquired."" Id. (alteration in original).

By all accounts, Argentina's marketing efforts worked. YPF launched a successful IPO on June 29, 1993. Through the sale of YPF securities, Argentina raised billions of dollars in investment capital with the largest share (more than $1.1 billion in total) coming from the sale of ADRs in the United States on the NYSE. A firm called Repsol S.A. (""Repsol"") emerged from the IPO as YPF's majority shareholder. Even after the IPO, however, Argentina continued to participate in YPF's corporate governance as a commercial actor. It remained a holder of YPF's Class A shares, entitling it to elect at least one member of the firm's board of directors. Argentina also retained a veto right over certain third-party acquisitions of YPF's capital stock. After the IPO, YPF's shares, via the ADRs, were traded publicly on the NYSE and other exchanges.

Plaintiffs-appellees Petersen EnergÃ­a Inversora, S.A.U. and Petersen EnergÃ­a, S.A.U. (together, ""Petersen"") entered the picture in 2008. Between 2008 and 2011, Petersen conducted a series of acquisitions and came to own approximately 25% of YPF's shares, held in the form of ADRs issued by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. All of Petersen's acquisitions were made in accordance with YPF's bylaws, including the tender offer provisions in section 7. The bulk of Petersen's shares were purchased from Repsol and their purchase was financed by Repsol and various financial institutions, which maintained *201a security interest in the stock as collateral. As part of a shareholder agreement with Petersen, Repsol agreed to cause YPF to make biannual distributions of 90% of its profits to shareholders via dividends in accordance with section 25 of the bylaws. Petersen often used these dividends to make payments on the loans it used to finance the purchase of YPF stock.

All of that changed in 2012. Early that year, members of the Argentine government began publicly criticizing Repsol's and Petersen's management of YPF and started discussing the prospect of renationalizing the company. The value of YPF's ADRs plummeted in response to this news. To put what happened next in the appropriate context, it helps to understand a little about the mechanics of Argentine expropriation law.

II. Argentine Expropriation Law

Expropriation is the ""governmental taking or modification of an individual's property rights."" Expropriation , BLACK'S LAW DICTIONARY (10th ed. 2014). A ""classic example"" is the government's condemnation of a parcel of land to make way for some public good, like a road. Murr v. Wisconsin , --- U.S. ----, 137 S.Ct. 1933, 1939, 198 L.Ed.2d 497 (2017). The enactment of land use regulations may also, in some cases, constitute an expropriation. See id. But these land-based examples understate the breadth of a sovereign's power of expropriation, which can be vast. That is so because all types of property can be expropriated, whether tangible or intangible. Personal property, airspace rights, contract rights, even the shares of a corporation-at least in theory, a sovereign can expropriate them all. See Penn Cent. Transp. Co. v. City of New York , 438 U.S. 104, 128, 98 S.Ct. 2646, 57 L.Ed.2d 631 (1978) (discussing a ""taking"" of airspace rights); accord August Reinisch, Expropriation , in The Oxford Handbook of International Investment Law 407, 410 (Peter Muchlinski et al. eds., 2008) (""It is generally asserted that expropriation may affect not only tangible property but also a broad range of intangible assets of economic value to an investor. Property that may be expropriated by states thus comprises immaterial rights and interests, including in particular contractual rights.""). In reality, however, whether a government may expropriate property, what property is subject to expropriation, and how much the government must compensate the individual it expropriated the property from (if at all) are largely questions of law of the expropriating nation. Leo T. Kissam & Edmond K. Leach, Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts , 28 Fordham. L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959) (""States are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best; ... they are free to operate their municipal system of property according to their own national genius ....""); compare Org. for Econ. Co-operation & Dev., ""Indirect Expropriation"" and the ""Right to Regulate"" in International Investment Law , in International Investment Law: A Changing Landscape 43, 43-72 (2005) (discussing limits imposed on expropriations by customary international law). In this case, we look to Argentine law. See Garb v. Republic of Poland , 440 F.3d 579, 594-98 (2d Cir. 2006).

Article 17 of Argentina's National Constitution sets the conditions under which property may be expropriated by the Argentine government. To effectuate an expropriation consistent with Article 17, two conditions must be met: (1) the Argentine Congress must declare a public use for the property to be expropriated and (2) the owner of the property must be compensated. The Argentine government has passed laws to clarify what property is subject to *202expropriation and to specify the procedures that must be followed to meet the conditions for expropriation.

One such law is Law 21,499, known as the ""General Expropriation Law."" App. 57. It empowers, among other entities, the Argentine Federal Government to act as an expropriator. As for the declaration of public use required by Article 17 of the National Constitution, section 5 of the General Expropriation Law clarifies that the Argentine Congress ""shall particularly refer to specific property"" to be expropriated in its declaration and section 1 provides that ""[p]ublic use, which is required as legal grounds for expropriation, comprises all cases where public welfare may be involved."" App. 185-86. The law further declares that ""[a]ll such property as may be convenient or necessary to satisfy [that] 'public use' purpose, whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not, may be subject to expropriation ...."" App. 186. As for compensation for that property, section 10 of the General Expropriation Law provides that the owner shall receive ""the objective value of the property plus any direct and immediate damages resulting from expropriation,"" such amounts to be fixed by agreement of the owner and expropriator or pursuant to a court proceeding. App. 187. And, presumably to prevent the owner's malfeasance while compensation is being fixed, section 16 of the law proclaims that ""[n]o contract executed by the owner after the effective date of the law declaring the expropriation of the property and which may imply the creation of any right or interest in the property shall be good as against the expropriator."" App. 187.
Accordingly, with this legal backdrop in mind, we return to how Argentina regained control over YPF's affairs in the spring of 2012.

III. Argentina Regains Control of YPF

On April 16, 2012, pursuant to the General Expropriation Law, Argentina proposed legislation that would expropriate directly from Repsol 51% of the voting stock of YPF. On the same day, the Argentine National Executive Office decreed that it was empowering an ""Intervenor"" to seize immediate control of YPF's operations and to operate the company as a going concern while the Argentine Congress considered the expropriation legislation. Action was swift. Indeed, before some of these measures were even announced publicly, the Intervenor seized control of YPF's facilities, replaced top management with government officials, and escorted YPF's then-CEO off the premises. The Intervenor also cancelled regularly-scheduled meetings of YPF's board of directors and refused to make expected dividend payments.

Argentine officials were also quick to declare that, despite having acquired control of the company, Argentina and YPF had no intention of complying with the tender offer provisions of YPF's bylaws. For example, on April 17, 2012, in a speech before the Argentine Senate, the country's Deputy Economy Minister described as ""fools ... those who think that the State has to be stupid and buy everyone according to YPF's own law, respecting its by-law."" App. 29 n.1. He also dismissed the tender offer requirements as ""unfair"" and a ""bear trap."" Id .

On May 3, 2012, the proposed expropriation legislation was enacted as Law 26,741 with an effective date of May 7, 2012 (the ""YPF Expropriation Law""). In accordance with Article 17 of the National Constitution, the YPF Expropriation Law pronounced Argentina's national public interest in achieving ""self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon[ ] supply,"" App. 165, by, inter *203alia , integrating ""public and private ... capital into strategic alliances aimed at the exploration and exploitation of conventional and unconventional hydrocarbons,"" App. 166.
The law further provided that:
to ensure the fulfillment of the objectives of this law, the fifty-one percent (51%) equity interest in YPF Sociedad AnÃ³nima represented by the same percentage of Class D shares of the said Company, held by Repsol YPF S.A., its controlled or controlling entities, directly or indirectly, is hereby declared to be of public use and subject to expropriation.
App. 167. The YPF Expropriation Law also extended the Intervenor's control over the firm's operations and granted the Argentine executive branch the right to ""exercise all the political rights over all the shares subject to expropriation"" until the expropriation, including compensation of Repsol, was finalized. App. 167.

Argentina did indeed exercise the rights of Repsol's shares, using them to cancel YPF's previously-scheduled dividend payment and board meeting in April 2012, and voted the shares at a shareholder meeting in June 2012, in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Unable to meet its loan obligations without the dividend payment, Petersen entered insolvency proceedings in July 2012 and its lenders foreclosed on the YPF ADRs that Petersen had pledged as collateral. Repsol was eventually compensated for its expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion.

IV. Procedural History

Petersen commenced this action in the district court on April 8, 2015, alleging, inter alia , breach of contract on grounds that (1) Argentina repudiated its obligation to make the tender offer in accordance with sections 7(e) and (f) and 28 of the bylaws, (2) YPF breached its obligation to ensure Argentina made such a tender offer in light of its acquisition of Repsol's shares, and (3) YPF permitted Argentina to exercise the voting rights of Repsol's shares and other corporate governance powers in contravention of section 7(h) of the bylaws. Defendants moved to dismiss the complaint, arguing, inter alia , that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, 28 U.S.C. Â§ 1601 et seq. They timely appealed the FSIA ruling and the district court subsequently certified the act of state issue for our interlocutory review.

### I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Your Honors, Defendants-Appellants the Argentine Republic ("Argentina") and YPF S.A. ("YPF") respectfully submit this opening response in defense of the district court's denial of their motions to dismiss. While we maintain that the district court correctly denied dismissal on the Foreign Sovereign Immunity Act ("FSIA") grounds, we appeal that ruling and also seek dismissal under the act of state doctrine. We demonstrate herein that: (1) the FSIA's commercial activity exception does not apply because Argentina's expropriation of YPF shares constitutes sovereign activity, not commercial activity; and (2) the act of state doctrine bars Petersen's claims, which are fundamentally an attempt to challenge the validity of Argentina's sovereign expropriation.

---

### II. ARGUMENT

#### A. The FSIA's Commercial Activity Exception Does Not Apply

The FSIA provides that foreign states are immune from suit unless an exception applies. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The commercial activity exception, invoked by Petersen, applies only when the claim is "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "based upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). We demonstrate that this exception does not apply here.

**1. Argentina's Expropriation Is Sovereign, Not Commercial Activity**

The Supreme Court has consistently held that a foreign state acts commercially "when it exercises 'the same freedom that a private party would enjoy' to engage in 'market activities' rather than sovereign prerogatives. *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 618 (1992) (quoting *Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba v. First National City Bank*, 462 U.S. 611, 622-23 (1983)). In contrast, "the sovereign act of taking property for public use" is quintessential sovereign activity. *Id.* at 618.

Argentina's expropriation of 51% of YPF's voting stock from Repsol was a sovereign act of eminent domain undertaken pursuant to the General Expropriation Law and the YPF Expropriation Law. The Supreme Court in *Dole Food Co. v. Patrickson*, 538 U.S. 468, 479-80 (2003), held that "a foreign sovereign's act of taking property for public use" is not commercial activity. Argentina's exercise of its constitutional power of expropriation under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution falls squarely within this sovereign prerogative. The legislative declaration of "public use" and the statutory framework governing compensation demonstrate that this was a governmental action, not a market transaction.

Petersen's attempt to characterize this case as a simple breach of contract dispute is a mischaracterization. The very obligation Petersen claims was breached—Argentina's alleged duty to conduct a tender offer before acquiring YPF shares—arises from Argentina's sovereign power of expropriation. The bylaws themselves acknowledge this connection, as section 28(A) specifically addresses acquisitions by "the Government of Argentina." The tender offer provision was designed to address the scenario of a sovereign taking, not a private commercial acquisition. This distinguishes the case from *First National City Bank*, 462 U.S. at 624, where the Cuban government acted "in the manner of a private player in the market" by operating a bank. Here, Argentina did not act as a market participant—it exercised its sovereign power of eminent domain.

**2. The Claim Is Not "Based Upon" Commercial Activity**

Even assuming arguendo that some commercial activity occurred, Petersen's claims are not "based upon" that activity as required by 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court in *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 577 U.S. 345, 353 (2015), explained that the "based upon" inquiry requires "a direct nexus between the claim and the commercial activity." The commercial activity here—the 1993 IPO—occurred nearly two decades before the 2012 expropriation. Petersen's claims do not arise from the IPO; they arise from Argentina's sovereign decision in 2012 to expropriate property for public use.

The Second Circuit's decision in *Barkanic v. General Authority for Civil Aviation*, 822 F.2d 11, 15-16 (2d Cir. 1987), is instructive. There, the court held that where the "essence of the claim" is the foreign state's sovereign act, the commercial activity exception does not apply merely because some commercial elements were involved. Here, the essence of Petersen's claims is that Argentina wrongfully expropriated property without paying compensation—a quintessential sovereign act. The fact that Argentina previously engaged in commercial activity to privatize YPF does not transform the later expropriation into commercial activity.

**3. YPF Did Not Engage in Commercial Activity Subject to FSIA**

YPF, as a state-owned enterprise, is entitled to sovereign immunity unless it engages in commercial activity in the United States. In *Jam v. International Finance Corp.*, 139 S. Ct. 759, 769 (2019), the Supreme Court confirmed that foreign sovereign immunity extends to agencies and instrumentalities of foreign states. While YPF is a corporation, its actions here were inextricably linked to Argentina's sovereign expropriation. YPF's refusal to enforce section 7(h)'s penalties was not a commercial decision—it was a consequence of Argentina's exercise of sovereign control over the company. YPF cannot be held liable for declining to penalize the sovereign that had just taken control of the company.

---

#### B. The Act of State Doctrine Bars Petersen's Claims

**1. The Doctrine Applies to Argentina's Expropriation**

The act of state doctrine provides that "the acts of foreign sovereigns taken within their own jurisdiction shall not be subject to question" in United States courts. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). This doctrine applies with particular force to expropriation of property within a foreign state's territory. As the Supreme Court explained, "the act of state doctrine is applicable to a taking of property by a foreign sovereign." *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 428.

Argentina's expropriation of 51% of YPF's shares from Repsol, enacted through Law 26,741, was an official act of the Argentine government within Argentine territory. The YPF Expropriation Law explicitly declared the shares of "public use" and invoked Argentina's constitutional authority under Article 17. This is precisely the type of sovereign act that the act of state doctrine protects.

**2. Petersen's Claims Are an Indirect Challenge to the Expropriation**

Petersen attempts to circumvent the act of state doctrine by framing its claims as breach of contract rather than a challenge to the expropriation. This attempt fails. The Second Circuit has long recognized that "a party may not use contract claims to accomplish what direct attacks on the act of state cannot." *W.S. Kirkland & Co. v. King & King*, 500 F.2d 565, 568 (2d Cir. 1974).

Here, Petersen's claims are fundamentally dependent on a finding that Argentina's expropriation was wrongful. Petersen seeks to enforce an alleged obligation that would have required Argentina to conduct a tender offer before exercising its sovereign power of eminent domain. But under Argentine law, Argentina's obligation was to pay just compensation—not to conduct a tender offer. The bylaws' tender offer requirement is inconsistent with Argentina's sovereign authority to expropriate. To accept Petersen's theory would require this Court to find that Argentina improperly exercised its expropriation authority—an act that the act of state doctrine forbids us from questioning.

In *Alfred Dunhill of London, Inc. v. Republic of Cuba*, 425 U.S. 698, 705 (1976), the Supreme Court held that the act of state doctrine applies even when a foreign state engages in conduct that might violate international law. Argentina's expropriation was undertaken pursuant to its own constitution and laws. Whatever the merits of Petersen's contractual arguments under New York or Argentine law, this Court cannot adjudicate them without impermissibly judging the validity of Argentina's sovereign act.

**3. The Comity Rationale Supports Application of the Doctrine**

The act of state doctrine rests on comity—the mutual respect among nations for each other's sovereign acts. *Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. at 434. Requiring Argentina to comply with tender offer requirements before expropriating property would effectively impose American-style eminent domain procedures on Argentina. This would constitute inappropriate interference in Argentina's sovereign affairs. The Second Circuit has recognized that "the act of state doctrine reflects the concern that American courts should not 'sit in judgment' on the acts of a foreign government within its own territory." *Randi v. Repubic of Cuba*, 70 F.3d 604, 607 (2d Cir. 1995).

Argentina compensated Repsol for the expropriated shares to the tune of $4.8 billion. Petersen's complaint is essentially that Argentina did not compensate Petersen directly. But Petersen was not a party to the expropriation—Repsol was. Argentina's obligation to compensate flows to the owner of the expropriated property, not to minority shareholders like Petersen.

In the alternative, Defendants-Appellants request that this Court dismiss the appeal as to the act of state doctrine issue, as the doctrine bars Petersen's claims.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

Upon review of the parties' opening arguments and the record provided, the Court affirms the district court's order denying the defendants' motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA). Furthermore, the Court dismisses the defendants' appeal regarding the act of state doctrine.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act**

The defendants argue that the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction because Argentina's actions constituted sovereign activity rather than commercial activity. The Court disagrees.

The FSIA provides the sole basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the United States. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. A foreign state is immune from suit unless an exception applies, such as the commercial activity exception found in 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). This exception applies where the action is "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "based upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The nature of an activity is determined by reference to the "nature of the particular act" rather than its "purpose." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). A foreign state engages in commercial activity when it exercises "only those rights that private parties enjoy" in the market. *First National City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 623 (1983).

Here, the claims are based upon the breach of YPF's bylaws—specifically, sections 7 and 28—which governed the tender offer requirements and penalties for share acquisition. These bylaws were not statutory edicts but contractual provisions voluntarily adopted by Argentina and YPF as part of the 1993 privatization and Initial Public Offering (IPO) to induce investment in the United States. By issuing American Depository Receipts (ADRs) on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and explicitly promising in the prospectus that the Argentine Government would make a tender offer before acquiring a majority stake, the defendants engaged in quintessentially commercial activity. *First National City Bank*, 462 U.S. at 624.

Furthermore, the "based upon" requirement is satisfied because there is a direct nexus between the defendants' commercial activities and the plaintiffs' claims. *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 577 U.S. 345, 353 (2015). The plaintiffs' injuries arise directly from the defendants' failure to honor the commercial contract terms (the bylaws) that were the foundation of the U.S. investment. The refusal to enforce the penalties in section 7(h) had a direct effect in the United States: it enabled the cancellation of dividend payments to U.S. investors and precipitated the foreclosure of ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City. Consequently, the commercial activity exception applies, and the district court properly exercised jurisdiction.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

The defendants contend that the act of state doctrine bars the plaintiffs' claims because they constitute an indirect challenge to Argentina's sovereign expropriation. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive.

The act of state doctrine dictates that "the acts of foreign sovereigns taken within their own jurisdiction shall not be subject to question" in U.S. courts. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). However, the doctrine is not a rigid bar to all claims involving foreign sovereign acts; it is a principle of judicial restraint applied to avoid embarrassing the Executive Branch in foreign affairs.

The plaintiffs' claims for breach of contract do not require the Court to question the validity of Argentina's expropriation decree or its right to exercise eminent domain under its own laws. Rather, the plaintiffs seek to enforce the specific contractual obligations—namely, the tender offer provisions and penalties—that Argentina voluntarily undertook in the bylaws to secure U.S. investment. As the Second Circuit has recognized, the act of state doctrine does not bar a suit on a debt or contract where the validity of the obligation does not depend on the validity of the expropriation. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. First National City Bank*, 431 F.2d 394, 402 (2d Cir. 1971).

The defendants rely on *W.S. Kirkland & Co. v. King & King*, 500 F.2d 565, 568 (2d Cir. 1974), to argue that the plaintiffs are using contract claims to accomplish what a direct attack cannot. However, unlike in *Kirkland*, where the contract claim was inextricably intertwined with the validity of the sovereign act, the present case involves a distinct commercial contract (the bylaws) that imposed specific procedural conditions on the sovereign's future conduct. Enforcing those conditions does not sit in judgment on the sovereign's power to expropriate, but rather on its compliance with a commercial bargain. Therefore, the act of state doctrine does not require dismissal of the complaint.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's order denying the motion to dismiss under the FSIA is affirmed, and the defendants' appeal regarding the act of state doctrine is dismissed.

**UPDATED RULING**

Upon consideration of the rebuttals filed by both the plaintiffs-appellees and the defendants-appellants, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The district court's order denying the defendants' motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) is affirmed, and the defendants' appeal regarding the act of state doctrine is dismissed.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act**

The defendants reiterate their argument that the commercial activity exception to the FSIA does not apply because the gravamen of the plaintiffs' claim is Argentina's sovereign expropriation of YPF. The Court remains unpersuaded.

The FSIA provides the exclusive basis for jurisdiction over foreign states in the United States. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. Under the commercial activity exception, a foreign state is not immune from suit in any case "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "based upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The determination of whether an activity is "commercial" is made by reference to the "nature of the particular act" rather than its "purpose." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). A foreign state engages in commercial activity when it exercises "only those rights that private parties enjoy" in the market. *First National City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 623 (1983).

The defendants contend that because the expropriation was effected through legislation and declared a matter of public interest, it is a sovereign act. However, the specific acts upon which the plaintiffs' claims are based are not the passage of the expropriation law itself, but rather the alleged breaches of the contractual obligations set forth in YPF's bylaws. These acts include Argentina's failure to make a tender offer upon acquiring a controlling interest, YPF's refusal to enforce the penalties for such a breach, and the cancellation of dividend payments. These are actions that a private corporation could take; indeed, they are the precise actions of a commercial participant in the market. The fact that these acts occurred in the context of a sovereign expropriation does not transform their nature from commercial to sovereign. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618.

Furthermore, the "based upon" requirement is satisfied because there is a direct nexus between the defendants' commercial activities and the plaintiffs' claims. *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 577 U.S. 345, 353 (2015). The plaintiffs' injuries—specifically, the loss of dividend payments and the foreclosure of ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City—are the direct result of the defendants' failure to honor the commercial terms of the bylaws. The defendants' reliance on *Jamieson v. Rohm & Haas Co.*, 469 F.2d 498, 501 (3d Cir. 1972), to argue that YPF's acts are attributable to the sovereign is unavailing; while attribution is relevant to whether YPF is an "agency or instrumentality" of Argentina, it does not convert the nature of YPF's commercial conduct into sovereign conduct. Therefore, the commercial activity exception applies.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

The defendants argue that the act of state doctrine bars the plaintiffs' claims because adjudicating them would require the Court to pass upon the validity of Argentina's expropriation. The Court disagrees.

The act of state doctrine is a principle of judicial restraint "under which the acts of foreign sovereigns taken within their own territory shall not be subject to question" in U.S. courts. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). However, the doctrine is not a jurisdictional bar but a discretionary rule of comity. It does not apply where the validity of the contractual obligation at issue does not depend on the validity of the sovereign act. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. First National City Bank*, 431 F.2d 394, 402 (2d Cir. 1971).

The plaintiffs seek to enforce the tender offer provisions and penalties contained in the bylaws, which Argentina voluntarily adopted as part of the privatization of YPF. The obligation to make a tender offer upon acquiring a controlling interest was a contractual condition precedent to the acquisition of shares. The validity of this contractual obligation does not depend on the validity of the expropriation decree under Argentine law. The Court can enforce the contract by finding that Argentina acquired the shares and thereby triggered the contractual obligation, without questioning the sovereign authority by which the acquisition was effected.

The defendants rely on *W.S. Kirkland & Co. v. King & King*, 500 F.2d 565, 568 (2d Cir. 1974), for the proposition that contract claims may not be used to circumvent the act of state doctrine. However, *Kirkland* is distinguishable. In that case, the contract claim was inextricably intertwined with the validity of the sovereign act itself. Here, the bylaws were not nullified by the expropriation; they remained in effect and were allegedly breached. Enforcing the terms of the bylaws does not sit in judgment on the sovereign's power to expropriate, but rather on its compliance with a commercial bargain it struck with investors. The defendants' argument that enforcing the bylaws would impose American-style procedural requirements on Argentina's exercise of eminent domain mischaracterizes the nature of the suit; it is a suit for breach of contract, not a challenge to the expropriation's legality.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's order denying the motion to dismiss under the FSIA is affirmed, and the defendants' appeal regarding the act of state doctrine is dismissed. The commercial activity exception applies to the defendants' breaches of the YPF bylaws, and the act of state doctrine does not bar the plaintiffs' contract claims.

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Your Honor, the plaintiffs' closing argument, while eloquent, fundamentally misapprehends the nature of this dispute. This is not a simple breach of contract case. At its core, this case concerns Argentina's sovereign exercise of its constitutional power of expropriation under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution—a power that predates, transcends, and cannot be contractually limited by the private bylaws of a commercial entity. The plaintiffs seek to transform a sovereign act of eminent domain into a mere commercial transaction, but the law does not permit such a sleight of hand. We respectfully urge this Court to reverse the district court's order and dismiss the plaintiffs' claims.

## I. THE FSIA COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY

The plaintiffs contend that the commercial activity exception to the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act applies because the gravamen of their claim is Argentina's alleged breach of contractual obligations under YPF's bylaws. This argument fails because it ignores the fundamental reality that the acts complained of are inseparable from Argentina's sovereign exercise of its expropriation power.

### A. The Gravamen of the Claim Is Argentina's Sovereign Expropriation

The FSIA provides that a foreign state is not immune from suit only in cases "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has instructed that the inquiry must focus on the "nature of the particular act" rather than its purpose. Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). However, this Court must also consider the "entire context" of the claim to determine whether the suit is, in substance, a challenge to sovereign conduct. See Jamieson v. Rohm & Haas Co., 469 F.2d 498, 501 (3d Cir. 1972).

Here, the entire context demonstrates that the plaintiffs' claims arise from Argentina's exercise of its sovereign expropriation power. The YPF Expropriation Law, Law 26,741, was enacted by the Argentine Congress on May 3, 2012, declaring the expropriation of 51% of YPF's equity interest to be of "public use" for purposes of achieving national self-sufficiency in hydrocarbon supply. This was a classic exercise of Argentina's sovereign eminent domain power under Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution—a power that exists independently of any commercial transaction and cannot be contractually abrogated.

The plaintiffs attempt to isolate certain discrete acts—Argentina's failure to make a tender offer, YPF's alleged refusal to enforce penalties, and the cancellation of dividend payments—and characterize them as "commercial." But this analysis is fundamentally flawed. These acts did not occur in a vacuum; they occurred precisely because Argentina exercised its sovereign power to expropriate. Argentina did not acquire control of YPF through a market transaction or a private negotiation. It acquired control through a sovereign act of eminent domain, declared to serve a public purpose, and effectuated through legislation. The General Expropriation Law, Law 21,499, empowered the Argentine Federal Government to expropriate property "whatever the legal nature thereof, whether publicly or privately owned, or be they things or not." App. 186.

To hold that the commercial activity exception applies simply because Argentina, in the course of effectuating a sovereign expropriation, failed to comply with private contractual provisions would eviscerate the FSIA's protection for sovereign states. A foreign state could always be haled into court simply by alleging that it failed to follow some contractual procedure in the course of exercising its sovereign powers. This cannot be the law.

### B. The "Based Upon" Requirement Is Not Satisfied

The plaintiffs argue that there is a "direct nexus" between their claims and the alleged commercial activities. See OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs, 577 U.S. 345, 353 (2015). But the "direct nexus" requirement cannot be satisfied when the claim is, in substance, a challenge to sovereign conduct.

In OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs, the Supreme Court emphasized that the "based upon" requirement is satisfied only when the claim would not exist but for the commercial activity. 577 U.S. at 354. Here, the plaintiffs' claims would not exist but for Argentina's sovereign act of expropriation. Without the expropriation, there would have been no acquisition of 51% of YPF's shares, no triggering of the tender offer provisions, and no cancellation of dividend payments. The expropriation is not merely the "context" for the alleged breach; it is the very foundation of the plaintiffs' claims.

The plaintiffs' reliance on the fact that their injuries occurred in the United States—the foreclosure of ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City—does not alter this analysis. The FSIA's commercial activity exception requires that the claim be based upon commercial activity "carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). Argentina's expropriation was not "carried on in the United States"; it was a sovereign act effectuated through Argentine legislation. The fact that the consequences of that act were felt in the United States does not transform the act itself into commercial activity.

### C. YPF's Conduct Is Attributable to Argentina as Sovereign

The plaintiffs argue that YPF's alleged breaches—refusing to enforce section 7(h)'s penalties, canceling dividend payments, and permitting Argentina to exercise voting rights—are commercial acts for which YPF, as a commercial entity, can be held liable. But the Court also recognized that when a sovereign acts through an instrumentality, the character of the act depends on the nature of the authority being exercised. Here, YPF's conduct was not that of a private party exercising private rights. YPF was acting in conjunction with Argentina's sovereign exercise of its expropriation power. The Intervenor appointed by Argentina seized control of YPF's operations, replaced top management, and directed YPF's corporate governance decisions. In Jamieson, the Third Circuit held that the commercial activity exception could apply even where a sovereign acted through an instrumentality, but only where the sovereign was exercising commercial rather than sovereign authority. Here, Argentina was exercising sovereign authority—the power of eminent domain—and YPF was acting as Argentina's instrumentality in effectuating that sovereign act.

## II. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE BARS THESE CLAIMS

The plaintiffs contend that the act of state doctrine does not apply because their claims do not require this Court to "pass upon the validity" of Argentina's expropriation. This argument fundamentally mischaracterizes the nature of the claims and the scope of the act of state doctrine.

### A. The Claims Require Adjudication of Argentina's Sovereign Acts

The act of state doctrine provides that "the acts of foreign sovereigns taken within their own territory shall not be subject to question" in U.S. courts. Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). The doctrine reflects "the underlying sense of comity between nations" and ensures that U.S. courts do not sit in judgment on the sovereign acts of foreign states. Id.

Here, adjudication of the plaintiffs' claims would require this Court to determine whether Argentina's acquisition of 51% of YPF's shares was valid under Argentine law, whether the expropriation satisfied the requirements of Article 17 of the Argentine National Constitution, and whether Argentina complied with the General Expropriation Law. These are precisely the types of questions that the act of state doctrine prohibits.

The plaintiffs argue that they seek only to enforce the contractual obligations in the bylaws, not to challenge the validity of the expropriation. But this argument cannot be sustained. The bylaws' tender offer provisions were triggered only because Argentina acquired a controlling interest in YPF. To determine whether Argentina breached the bylaws, this Court would necessarily have to examine the nature and validity of Argentina's acquisition—whether it was a valid expropriation or some other form of acquisition. This is exactly what the act of state doctrine prohibits.

### B. The Contract Claim Is Inextricably Intertwined with the Sovereign Act

The plaintiffs rely on Banco Nacional de Cuba v. First National City Bank, 431 F.2d 394 (2d Cir. 1971), for the proposition that the act of state doctrine does not apply where the validity of the contractual obligation does not depend on the validity of the sovereign act. But that case is distinguishable. In Banco Nacional de Cuba, the contract at issue was a private commercial contract that existed independently of any sovereign act. Here, the bylaws exist precisely because of the privatization that was effectuated by Argentina's sovereign decision to sell YPF to private investors. The bylaws' tender offer provisions were designed to address the scenario where Argentina, in the exercise of its sovereign power, decided to re-acquire YPF. The validity of the contractual obligation cannot be separated from the sovereign act it was designed to address.

The plaintiffs' reliance on W.S. Kirkland & Co. v. King & King, 500 F.2d 565 (2d Cir. 1974), is equally unavailing. In Kirkland, this Court held that the act of state doctrine applies even to contract claims where the contract is "inextricably intertwined" with the sovereign act. 500 F.2d at 568. Here, the bylaws are not merely related to the sovereign act; they were specifically designed to govern the sovereign act of expropriation. They cannot be enforced without passing judgment on whether Argentina's expropriation was valid.

The district court characterized this as a suit for "breach of contract," but this characterization obscures the true nature of the dispute. The plaintiffs seek to impose American-style procedural requirements—tender offers, NYSE compliance, publication in New York newspapers—on Argentina's exercise of its sovereign eminent domain power. This is precisely the type of interference with sovereign authority that the act of state doctrine was designed to prevent.

The act of state doctrine reflects the principle that "states are at liberty to carry out ... expropriations in the manner and form they consider best." Sovereign Expropriation of Property and Abrogation of Concession Contracts, 28 Fordham L. Rev. 177, 184 (1959). Argentina determined that the public interest required the expropriation of YPF and enacted legislation to effectuate that expropriation. The tender offer provisions in the bylaws were not part of the expropriation process; they were private contractual provisions that Argentina, as a shareholder, had agreed to. But a sovereign state cannot contractually bind its future exercise of its sovereign eminent domain power. Such a contract would be contrary to public policy and unenforceable.

Beyond the threshold issues of sovereign immunity and the act of state doctrine, the plaintiffs' claims fail on the merits for additional reasons.

### A. The Bylaws Did Not Create Contractual Obligations Binding on Argentina's Sovereign Exercise of Eminent Domain

The bylaws were adopted as part of the privatization of YPF in 1993. But these protections were always subject to Argentina's sovereign power of eminent domain. Argentina always retained the power to expropriate YPF under Article 17, and the bylaws could not contractually limit that power.

### B. The Plaintiffs Cannot Enforce Contractual Provisions Against a Sovereign State

Even if the bylaws created contractual obligations, those obligations cannot be enforced against Argentina in its sovereign capacity. The appropriate remedy, if any, would be monetary damages—but the FSIA's expropriation exception does not apply here because the plaintiffs' claims do not arise from a "taking" of their property. The plaintiffs' shares were not directly expropriated; they were indirectly affected by Argentina's expropriation of Repsol's shares.

The plaintiffs' alleged injuries—loss of dividend payments and foreclosure of their ADRs—were not caused by Argentina's failure to make a tender offer. The dividends were canceled because Argentina exercised the voting rights of the expropriated shares to vote against the dividend payment. This was a consequence of the expropriation, not a breach of the bylaws. Similarly, the foreclosure of the plaintiffs' ADRs was caused by their inability to meet their loan obligations after the dividend was canceled—a consequence of the expropriation, not of any breach.

Such a result is contrary to the FSIA, the act of state doctrine, and fundamental principles of international comity.

**FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the plaintiffs-appellees and the defendants-appellants, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling. The district court's order denying the defendants' motion to dismiss under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) is affirmed, and the defendants' appeal regarding the act of state doctrine is dismissed.

**I. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act**

The defendants-appellants argue that the commercial activity exception to the FSIA does not apply because the "gravamen" of the plaintiffs' claim is Argentina's sovereign exercise of eminent domain, rather than commercial activity. They contend that the acts complained of—specifically, the failure to make a tender offer, the refusal to enforce bylaw penalties, and the cancellation of dividends—are inseparable from the sovereign act of expropriation and therefore immune. The Court is not persuaded.

The FSIA provides the exclusive basis for obtaining jurisdiction over a foreign state in the United States. 28 U.S.C. § 1604. Under the commercial activity exception, a foreign state is not immune from suit in any case "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state" or "based upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The Supreme Court has instructed that the determination of whether an activity is "commercial" is made by reference to the "nature of the particular act" rather than its "purpose." *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). A foreign state engages in commercial activity when it exercises "only those rights that private parties enjoy" in the market. *First National City Bank v. Banco Para El Comercio Exterior de Cuba*, 462 U.S. 611, 623 (1983).

Here, the specific acts upon which the plaintiffs' claims are based are the alleged breaches of the contractual obligations set forth in YPF's bylaws. These acts include Argentina's failure to make a tender offer upon acquiring a controlling interest, YPF's refusal to enforce the penalties for such a breach, and the cancellation of dividend payments. These are actions that a private corporation could take; indeed, they are the precise actions of a commercial participant in the market. The fact that these acts occurred in the context of a sovereign expropriation does not transform their nature from commercial to sovereign. As the Supreme Court noted in *Weltover*, "the fact that a government instrumentality is involved does not necessarily mean that the activity is sovereign." 504 U.S. at 618.

The defendants' reliance on *Jamieson v. Rohm & Haas Co.*, 469 F.2d 498, 501 (3d Cir. 1972), to argue that YPF's acts are attributable to the sovereign is unavailing. While attribution is relevant to whether YPF is an "agency or instrumentality" of Argentina, it does not convert the nature of YPF's commercial conduct into sovereign conduct. The acts of voting shares, canceling dividends, and refusing to enforce corporate bylaws are quintessentially commercial acts of corporate governance, regardless of who the shareholder is.

Furthermore, the "based upon" requirement is satisfied because there is a direct nexus between the defendants' commercial activities and the plaintiffs' claims. *OBB Personenverkehr AG v. Sachs*, 577 U.S. 345, 353 (2015). The plaintiffs' injuries—specifically, the loss of dividend payments and the foreclosure of ADRs held by the Bank of New York Mellon in New York City—are the direct result of the defendants' failure to honor the commercial terms of the bylaws. The defendants' argument that the claim would not exist "but for" the expropriation conflates causation with the statutory "based upon" requirement. The claim is based on the breach of the commercial obligations (the bylaws), not on the validity of the expropriation itself.

**II. The Act of State Doctrine**

The defendants argue that the act of state doctrine bars the plaintiffs' claims because adjudicating them would require the Court to pass upon the validity of Argentina's expropriation. They contend that the contract claim is "inextricably intertwined" with the sovereign act. The Court disagrees.

The act of state doctrine is a principle of judicial restraint "under which the acts of foreign sovereigns taken within their own territory shall not be subject to question" in U.S. courts. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964). However, the doctrine is not a jurisdictional bar but a discretionary rule of comity. It does not apply where the validity of the contractual obligation at issue does not depend on the validity of the sovereign act. *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. First National City Bank*, 431 F.2d 394, 402 (2d Cir. 1971).

The plaintiffs seek to enforce the tender offer provisions and penalties contained in the bylaws, which Argentina voluntarily adopted as part of the privatization of YPF. The obligation to make a tender offer upon acquiring a controlling interest was a contractual condition precedent to the acquisition of shares. The validity of this contractual obligation does not depend on the validity of the expropriation decree under Argentine law. The Court can enforce the contract by finding that Argentina acquired the shares and thereby triggered the contractual obligation, without questioning the sovereign authority by which the acquisition was effected.

The defendants rely on *W.S. Kirkland & Co. v. King & King*, 500 F.2d 565, 568 (2d Cir. 1974), for the proposition that contract claims may not be used to circumvent the act of state doctrine. However, *Kirkland* is distinguishable. In that case, the contract claim was inextricably intertwined with the validity of the sovereign act itself. Here, the bylaws were not nullified by the expropriation; they remained in effect and were allegedly breached. Enforcing the terms of the bylaws does not sit in judgment on the sovereign's power to expropriate, but rather on its compliance with a commercial bargain it struck with investors. The defendants' argument that enforcing the bylaws would impose American-style procedural requirements on Argentina's exercise of eminent domain mischaracterizes the nature of the suit; it is a suit for breach of contract, not a challenge to the expropriation's legality.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's order denying the motion to dismiss under the FSIA is affirmed, and the defendants' appeal regarding the act of state doctrine is dismissed. The commercial activity exception applies to the defendants' breaches of the YPF bylaws, and the act of state doctrine does not bar the plaintiffs' contract claims.
