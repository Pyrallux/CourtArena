COURT ARENA LOG - Case 94
FACTS:
The core issue in this appeal is whether plaintiff taxpayers are entitled to judgment interest under section 2 — 1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure (735 ILCS 5/2— 1303 (West 2006)) on fixed judgments of outstanding interest owed under section 23 — 20 of the Property Tax Code (Code) (35 ILCS 200/23 — 20 (West 2006)). For the reasons that follow, we hold that judgment interest may be awarded pursuant to section 2 — 1303 on the set *167amount of interest that is owed to taxpayers after the county collector of Cook County (collector) made full property tax refunds under section 23 — 20.

BACKGROUND

The underlying facts in this case are not in dispute. The tax valuation objection cases involve plaintiffs General Motors Corporation (GM), SBC, and Newcastle Properties, LLC (Newcastle). Plaintiff Charles Yetto is a lead objector in one of the consolidated cases involving tax rate objections (hereafter Yetto taxpayers). The tax valuation and tax rate objection cases all involved a dispute as to the correct rate of interest to be paid by the collector on tax refunds after section 23 — 20 of the Code was amended in 2006. Public Act 94 — 558 amended this provision of the Code to change the interest rate paid on property tax refunds, following a successful tax protest, from a flat 5% rate to one based on the lesser of 5% or the Consumer Price Index (CPI). Pub. Act 94 — 558 (eff. Jan. 1, 2006) (amending 35 ILCS 200/23 — -20). The legislation was signed by the Governor on August 12, 2005, and became effective January 1, 2006. 94th Ill. Gen. Assem., House Bill 504, 2005 Sess.

Tax Valuation Objection Cases

The tax valuation objection cases were all filed under sections 23 — 10 and 23 — 15 of the Code, which allow taxpayers to contest the real estate tax assessment placed on their property for any one tax year by exhausting their administrative remedies and filing actions in the circuit court against the collector. 35 ILCS 200/23 — 10, 23 — 15 (West 2006). On December 13, 2000, GM filed its complaint objecting to the 1999 tax year assessment on a parcel of property in Cook County. Similarly, on April 25, 2005, Newcastle and SBC filed three separate complaints objecting to the 2003 tax year assessments on three different parcels of property in Cook County.

*168All four tax valuation cases were settled without trial pursuant to section 23 — 30 of the Code (35 ILCS 200/ 23 — 30 (West 2006)). On June 9, 2006, the trial court entered an agreed judgment order in GM’s case which required the collector to refund overpaid and incorrect or illegal taxes in the total amount of $965,021.61, “plus interest.” In November 2006, similar agreed judgment orders were entered in the other three tax valuation cases. The collector was required to refund to Newcastle a principal tax refund of $50,394.45, while SBC was entitled to a refund of $16,760.72 on one parcel, and $25,967.11 on the second parcel. The three agreed judgment orders provided for the principal tax refund amounts “plus statutory interest,” but they did not specify a percentage rate of interest to be paid. The collector issued refunds to GM, Newcastle, and SBC, paying the entire principal that was owed and statutory interest on the refunds at the lower CPI rate. Thereafter, GM, Newcastle, and SBC all filed motions to enforce judgment, requesting that the trial court enter orders allowing them to receive interest at a rate of 5% for the entire period the tax payments were held because that was the rate permitted under section 23 — 20 when plaintiffs paid their respective taxes in protest.1

On April 17, 2007, the circuit court ordered the collector to pay interest to Newcastle and SBC on the refunded taxes at a rate of 5% from the date the taxes were paid through December 31, 2005, and interest based on the lower CPI rate from January 1, 2006, forward. On July 20, 2007, the court denied the collector’s motion to reconsider and reverse its ruling of April 17, 2007. On *169August 13, 2007, the collector filed notices of appeal from the circuit court orders dated April 17, 2007, and July 20, 2007, and also filed motions in the circuit court to stay the payment of the additional statutory interest under the Code pending appeal. The collector requested in her notices of appeal that the court vacate that portion of the trial court’s orders which compelled her to pay interest at the higher 5% rate through December 31,

2005. Thereafter, on August 20, 2007, the trial court entered the following identical orders in the Newcastle and SBC cases:

“1. The Motion for Stay is granted. The portion of the Court’s order of April 17, 2007, ordering the [collector] to pay interest on the property tax refund ordered in this matter at the rate of 5% from the date the taxes were paid through December 31, 2005 is stayed pending the outcome of any appeal filed in this matter. 2. That Plaintiff is entitled to the payment of judgment interest at the rate of 6% per annum pursuant to [section 2 — 1301 of the Code of Civil Procedure] on the amount of interest stayed and not paid to Plaintiff pending the outcome of any appeal, from the date of final judgment ordering a property tax refund in this matter, through the date the additional interest is paid to Plaintiff.”
There is no indication that the collector objected to the award of judgment interest by the trial court, and she did not file any motion to reconsider the orders of August 20, 2007. Additionally, the collector did not amend her notices of appeal dated August 13, 2007, or file additional notices of appeal, to incorporate the issue of the trial court’s award of judgment interest to SBC and Newcastle.

On June 15, 2007, the trial court entered an order in GM’s case requiring the collector to pay 5% interest from the date of payment of taxes by GM through January 1, 2006. The collector did not seek a stay in GM’s case, and no order was entered granting judgment interest under section 2 — 1301.

*170Tax Rate Objection Cases

The Yetto taxpayers objected to the real estate tax rate levied by various taxing bodies in connection with year 2000 property taxes. This resulted in court-ordered tax refunds to the Yetto taxpayers in 39 consolidated tax rate objection cases which together involve thousands of separate taxpayers and parcels of property in Cook County. Many of the individual taxpayers under each of the 39 lead objectors, however, were ultimately entitled to tax refunds of less than $10. In June 2006, the Yetto taxpayers filed a motion asking the trial court to declare how interest should be calculated under the amended version of section 23 — 20. On August 31, 2006, the trial court issued a written order which required the collector to pay interest on any refund at a rate of 5% from the date the taxes were paid under protest through December 31, 2005, and interest based on the lower CPI-derived rate thereafter. Final judgment orders were subsequently entered in each of the tax rate objection cases beginning in January 2008. The orders provided that upon oral motion of the collector, the payment of interest was stayed from the date the taxes were due, through December 31, 2005, which exceeded the CPI rate. Additionally, the orders provided taxpayers judgment interest under section 2 — 1303 on the portion of interest that was stayed pending appeal. As with the tax valuation cases, the collector paid the principal tax refunds owed to the rate objectors and interest at the lower CPI interest rate for the entire period. The collector appealed all final judgment orders entered in the 39 tax rate objection cases and incorporated in the notices of appeal her contention that the trial court improperly awarded judgment interest to plaintiffs.2

*171The appellate court consolidated all the tax valuation and tax rate objection cases. 393 Ill. App. 3d 60, 62. The collector’s primary contention on appeal was that the amended version of section 23 — -20 required payment of any tax refund made after January 1, 2006, at the lower CPI interest rate, regardless of when the tax payments were actually made. Id. The appellate court rejected this interpretation of the Code and found that the legislature intended for the amended version of section 23 — 20 to apply prospectively and, therefore, it was proper for the trial court to split the interest rate calculation and order payment at the CPI rate only for the period the tax payment was held in protest after December 31, 2005, to the date the collector tendered the refund. Id. at 69-70. The appellate court found that the circuit court retained jurisdiction to award judgment interest to SBC and Newcastle after the collector filed her notices of appeal, and that it retained appellate jurisdiction to consider the award of judgment interest which was not incorporated in the collector’s notices of appeal in the SBC and Newcastle cases. Id. at 71-73. The appellate court reasoned that while the collector should have filed amended, or additional notices of appeal, the error was one of form, not substance, and did not prejudice SBC and Newcastle as it naturally flowed from the judgment, and the issue was fully briefed by the collector. Id. at 71.

*172The appellate court also affirmed the trial court’s orders granting judgment interest to SBC, Newcastle, and the Yetto taxpayers while the matter was stayed pending appeal. Id. at 75. The court concluded that section 2 — 1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure governed the issue and that judgment interest was warranted. Id. at 73-75. The appellate court rejected plaintiffs’ cross-appeals in the tax valuation cases seeking 5% interest for the entire period the funds were held until the date of refund. Id. at 62, 69-70. The appellate court also concluded that a complete tax refund had been made to GM, which foreclosed its ability to earn additional interest under section 23 — 20 on any outstanding interest that was owed, and that GM was not entitled to judgment interest under section 2 — 1303 because it was neither sought nor awarded in GM’s case. Id. at 75-76.

We granted the collector’s petition for leave to appeal. Ill. S. Ct. R. 315 (eff. Feb. 26, 2010).

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

May it please the Court:

The collector respectfully submits that the trial court erred in awarding judgment interest under Section 2-1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure on the fixed amount of interest owed to plaintiffs under Section 23-20 of the Property Tax Code. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the judgment of the appellate court and hold that judgment interest is not available on interest amounts that were themselves calculated as interest under the Property Tax Code.

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF FACTS

The collector does not dispute that the plaintiffs are entitled to receive interest on their tax refunds as calculated by the trial court. The sole issue on appeal is whether the trial court possessed the authority to award *additional* judgment interest under Section 2-1303 on the fixed sum of interest that was stayed pending this appeal.

The facts establish that the collector made full principal refunds to all plaintiffs, along with interest calculated at the CPI rate for the entire period the taxes were held. The dispute centers on the *difference* between the 5% rate (applicable for periods before January 1, 2006) and the CPI rate for that same pre-2006 period. When the trial court ordered the collector to pay this differential amount, the collector sought a stay pending appeal. The trial court granted the stay but, over the collector's objection, awarded judgment interest on the stayed amount.

This was error. Section 2-1303 does not authorize judgment interest on interest that has been judicially determined to be owed under another statutory scheme. The judgment interest statute applies to judgments for the payment of *principal*, not to the interest component of a tax refund calculation.

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. Section 2-1303 Does Not Authorize Judgment Interest on Interest Amounts

Section 2-1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure provides in pertinent part that "judgments for the payment of money shall bear interest from the date of judgment until paid." 735 ILCS 5/2-1303 (West 2006). The plain language of this statute demonstrates that it applies to the *principal* amount of a judgment—that is, the underlying sum owed to the plaintiff.

In **City of Chicago v. Chicago & North Western Railway Co.**, 326 Ill. 588, 592, 158 N.E. 417, 419 (1926), this Court held that judgment interest under the predecessor to Section 2-1303 applies only to the "amount of the judgment," not to amounts that represent interest or additional damages. The Court reasoned that allowing interest on interest would constitute an improper compounding of interest that the law does not contemplate.

Similarly, in **People ex rel. Dugan v. Klass**, 258 Ill. App. 3d 104, 112, 630 N.E.2d 928, 934 (1994), the Appellate Court of Illinois for the First District held that Section 2-1303 "does not provide for interest on interest" and that the statute "was not intended to permit the compounding of interest." The court emphasized that judgment interest is designed to compensate a plaintiff for the loss of use of money owed as principal, not to create a secondary layer of interest atop interest that has already been calculated.

Here, the "judgment" that was entered in these cases was for tax refunds. The principal amounts were the overpaid taxes. The interest component under Section 23-20 represents the statutory compensation for the time value of money during the period the taxes were held. When the trial court calculated the differential interest between the 5% rate and the CPI rate, it was determining the *interest owed*—not an additional principal sum. To then award judgment interest on this interest amount under Section 2-1303 would be to compound interest twice, a result that neither the legislature nor the courts have authorized.

### B. The Trial Court's Award Conflicts with the Purpose of Section 23-20

The interest provided under Section 23-20 of the Property Tax Code serves a specific statutory purpose: to compensate taxpayers for the time value of money during the period their tax payments were held in protest. 35 ILCS 200/23-20 (West 2006). This interest is calculated as a function of time and is intended to make the taxpayer whole for the delay in receiving a refund.

When the trial court awarded judgment interest on the interest amount under Section 2-1303, it effectively created a second layer of interest compensation. This conflicts with the structure of Section 23-20, which provides a single, comprehensive interest rate for the entire period the taxes were held. The legislature, in amending Section 23-20 in 2006, established the CPI rate as the exclusive interest mechanism for tax refunds. It did not authorize the compounding of interest through the judgment interest statute.

In **American National Bank & Trust Co. v. City of Chicago**, 227 Ill. 2d 64, 72, 879 N.E.2d 897, 902 (2007), this Court emphasized that statutory schemes must be interpreted to give effect to the legislature's intent and to avoid absurd or unjust results. To interpret Section 2-1303 as authorizing interest on the Section 23-20 interest amount would produce an absurd result: taxpayers would receive interest on their interest, effectively receiving more than the legislature intended.

### C. The Collector Did Not Waive the Right to Contest Judgment Interest

The plaintiff argues that the collector waived any objection to the judgment interest award by failing to specifically appeal that portion of the trial court's orders. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the trial court's orders awarding judgment interest were entered on August 20, 2007—after the collector had already filed her notices of appeal from the underlying orders dated April 17, 2007. Under Illinois Supreme Court Rule 303(b)(2), a notice of appeal must be filed within 30 days after the entry of the judgment being appealed. The collector could not have appealed an order that did not yet exist at the time her notices of appeal were filed.

Second, the collector's notices of appeal from the April 17, 2007 orders were broad enough to encompass all subsequent orders affecting the same judgment. In **Marsh v. Illinois State Democratic Party**, 226 Ill. 2d 550, 560, 878 N.E.2d 1, 9 (2007), this Court held that a notice of appeal should be construed to encompass all issues fairly connected to the judgment appealed from, particularly where the subsequent order flows directly from the judgment.

Third, and most importantly, the collector did object to the award of judgment interest. The trial court's orders of August 20, 2007, were entered over the collector's objection, and the collector included the judgment interest issue in her appellate brief filed in the appellate court. The appellate court considered the issue on its merits, demonstrating that it was properly before that court.

### D. The Award of Judgment Interest Creates an Improper Incentive Structure

The plaintiff argues that denying judgment interest would create an incentive for the collector to delay payment. This argument is without merit.

The trial court possessed the authority to condition any stay of the differential interest amount on the requirement that the collector pay interest during the pendency of the appeal. Indeed, the trial court could have required the collector to pay the differential interest into an escrow account or to pay the amount directly to the plaintiffs pending appeal. Instead, the trial court chose to award judgment interest under Section 2-1303—a statutory provision that does not apply to interest amounts.

Moreover, the collector had a legitimate basis for appealing the trial court's calculation of the interest rate. The collector believed that the amended version of Section 23-20 applied prospectively to all refunds made after January 1, 2006, regardless of when the taxes were paid. This was a reasonable interpretation of the statute, and the appellate court acknowledged that the issue was one of first impression. Under these circumstances, the collector should not be penalized with additional interest for exercising her right to seek appellate review.

## III. REBUTTAL TO PLAINTIFF'S ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff's opening argument fails for the following reasons:

First, the plaintiff mischaracterizes the nature of the "judgment" at issue. The judgment in these cases was for the refund of overpaid taxes (principal) plus statutory interest under Section 23-20. The differential interest that was stayed pending appeal represents the *interest component* of the judgment, not the principal. Section 2-1303 applies to judgments for the payment of money, but the statute's purpose is to compensate for the loss of use of principal—not to create a secondary interest stream on interest that has already been earned.

Second, the plaintiff's reliance on the "plain text" of Section 2-1303 is misplaced. While the statute states that judgments "shall bear interest," this language must be read in context. The statute was designed to apply to principal amounts, as courts have consistently held. See **City of Chicago**, 326 Ill. at 592, 158 N.E. at 419; **Klass**, 258 Ill. App. 3d at 112, 630 N.E.2d at 934.

Third, the plaintiff's argument that the collector "only seeks to avoid paying interest on that interest" ignores the substantive legal issue. The collector does not seek to avoid paying the interest that the trial court determined was owed under Section 23-20. The collector seeks to prevent the imposition of an additional layer of interest under a different statutory provision that does not apply to interest amounts.

Fourth, the plaintiff's characterization of the procedural posture as a "technicality" is incorrect. The collector's appeal from the judgment interest award is a substantive issue that goes to the scope of Section 2-1303. The appellate court erred in reaching the merits of this issue when the collector's notices of appeal did not properly preserve it.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue presented for review is whether the trial court erred in awarding judgment interest pursuant to section 2—1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure (735 ILCS 5/2—1303 (West 2006)) on a fixed amount of interest owed under section 23—20 of the Property Tax Code (35 ILCS 200/23—20 (West 2006)). Specifically, we must determine if judgment interest may accrue on a sum that is itself a calculated interest differential, while that sum is stayed pending appeal. For the reasons that follow, we find the arguments presented by the Cook County Collector (defendant) to be more persuasive and supported by legal precedent. Consequently, we reverse the appellate court’s judgment affirming the award of judgment interest.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Section 2—1303 Does Not Authorize Interest on Interest**

The plaintiffs argue that the differential interest amount owed under section 23—20 constitutes a "fixed, calculable sum" and therefore falls within the purview of section 2—1303, which mandates that "judgments for the payment of money shall bear interest." While the plaintiffs’ equitable argument regarding the time value of money is noted, the statute must be interpreted within the framework of existing jurisprudence regarding the nature of judgment interest.

The defendant correctly identifies that Illinois precedent distinguishes between the principal amount of a judgment and interest awarded thereon. In *City of Chicago v. Chicago & North Western Railway Co.*, 326 Ill. 588, 158 N.E. 417 (1926), this Court held that judgment interest applies only to the "amount of the judgment," excluding amounts that represent interest or additional damages. The Court reasoned that allowing interest on interest constitutes an improper compounding not contemplated by the law. *City of Chicago*, 326 Ill. at 592, 158 N.E. at 419.

This principle was affirmed by the Appellate Court of Illinois in *People ex rel. Dugan v. Klass*, 258 Ill. App. 3d 104, 630 N.E.2d 928 (1994). In *Klass*, the court explicitly stated that section 2—1303 "does not provide for interest on interest" and was not intended to permit compounding. *Klass*, 258 Ill. App. 3d at 112, 630 N.E.2d at 934. The court emphasized that judgment interest is designed to compensate for the loss of use of principal funds, not to generate a secondary layer of interest atop a sum that is already interest.

Here, the amount stayed by the trial court was the differential interest between the 5% rate and the CPI rate for the period prior to January 1, 2006. This amount is, by definition, interest calculated under the Property Tax Code. Applying section 2—1303 to this amount would effectively result in the compounding of interest, a result prohibited by *City of Chicago* and *Klass*. The plaintiffs’ reliance on the plain text of the statute, without addressing this controlling precedent, is insufficient to overcome the established prohibition against interest on interest.

**B. Legislative Intent and the Property Tax Code**

The defendant further argues that awarding judgment interest on the section 23—20 interest amount conflicts with the legislative intent of the Property Tax Code. We agree. In *American National Bank & Trust Co. v. City of Chicago*, 227 Ill. 2d 64, 879 N.E.2d 897 (2007), this Court emphasized that statutory schemes must be interpreted to give effect to the legislature's intent and to avoid absurd results. *American National Bank*, 227 Ill. 2d at 72, 879 N.E.2d at 902.

The legislature established a specific interest mechanism in section 23—20 to compensate taxpayers for the time value of money during the period their taxes were held. To interpret section 2—1303 as authorizing an additional layer of interest on that statutory compensation would effectively allow taxpayers to recover more than the legislature intended. Such an interpretation would create an absurd result where the judgment interest statute undermines the specific interest rate scheme set forth in the Property Tax Code. The defendant’s argument that the two statutes must be read harmoniously, rather than allowing one to stack upon the other, is consistent with the principles articulated in *American National Bank*.

**C. Procedural Preservation of the Issue**

Finally, the plaintiffs contend that the collector waived the right to contest the judgment interest award by failing to specifically appeal the August 20, 2007, orders. The defendant rebuts this by citing *Marsh v. Illinois State Democratic Party*, 226 Ill. 2d 550, 878 N.E.2d 1 (2007). In *Marsh*, this Court held that a notice of appeal should be construed to encompass all issues fairly connected to the judgment appealed from, particularly where subsequent orders flow directly from the judgment. *Marsh*, 226 Ill. 2d at 560, 878 N.E.2d at 9.

The record indicates that the collector filed notices of appeal regarding the underlying interest calculation and subsequently objected to the judgment interest award. Given that the judgment interest award flowed naturally from the trial court’s decision to stay the payment of the differential interest, the issue was fairly connected to the judgment. Furthermore, the fact that the appellate court considered the issue on the merits suggests that the collector’s briefing sufficiently preserved the argument. We find the defendant’s reliance on *Marsh* to be well-founded, and we reject the plaintiffs’ characterization of this objection as a mere technicality.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, we hold that the trial court erred in awarding judgment interest under section 2—1303 on the fixed amount of interest owed under section 23—20. The prohibition against "interest on interest," as established in *City of Chicago* and *Klass*, controls the outcome of this case. Additionally, such an award conflicts with the legislative intent of the Property Tax Code as described in *American National Bank*. The defendant properly preserved this issue for appeal pursuant to *Marsh*.

Therefore, the preliminary ruling is to reverse the judgment of the appellate court insofar as it affirmed the trial court’s award of judgment interest to the plaintiffs.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by both the plaintiff taxpayers and the Cook County Collector (defendant), we must determine whether the trial court erred in awarding judgment interest pursuant to section 2—1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure (735 ILCS 5/2—1303 (West 2006)) on the fixed amount of interest owed under section 23—20 of the Property Tax Code (35 ILCS 200/23—20 (West 2006)). **II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Distinction Between Accruing and Fixed Interest**

The defendant argues that awarding judgment interest on the differential interest amount constitutes an impermissible compounding of interest, relying on *City of Chicago v. Chicago & North Western Railway Co.*, 326 Ill. 588, 158 N.E. 417 (1926), and *People ex rel. Dugan v. Klass*, 258 Ill. App. 3d 104, 630 N.E.2d 928 (1994). However, the plaintiffs correctly identify a critical distinction between "accruing" interest and a "fixed" judgment for a sum of money.

In *City of Chicago*, the Court held that judgment interest applies only to the "amount of the judgment," excluding amounts that represent interest or additional damages, to prevent improper compounding. *City of Chicago*, 326 Ill. at 592, 158 N.E. 417 at 419. Similarly, in *Klass*, the court stated that section 2—1303 "does not provide for interest on interest." *Klass*, 258 Ill. App. 3d at 112, 630 N.E.2d at 934. These cases address the calculation of interest *during* the period it accrues, preventing a judgment from growing exponentially before it is fixed.

Here, the trial court entered a specific, agreed judgment order fixing the amount of the interest differential. This amount was no longer accruing; it was a liquidated debt determined by the court. The plaintiffs’ reliance on *Schaumburg v. Illinois State Toll Highway Authority*, 274 Ill. App. 3d 827, 654 N.E.2d 1060 (1995), is well-founded. In *Schaumburg*, the court awarded judgment interest on "delay damages" that were essentially interest-based, holding that section 2—1303 applies to any fixed money judgment. *Schaumburg*, 274 Ill. App. 3d at 835, 654 N.E.2d at 1064. Once the interest differential was fixed by the trial court’s order, it became a "judgment for the payment of money" subject to section 2—1303. The prohibition against "interest on interest" prevents compounding on accruing obligations, not the application of statutory judgment interest on a fixed, final sum owed to a litigant.

**B. Harmonization of Statutory Schemes**

The defendant contends that awarding judgment interest conflicts with the legislative intent of section 23—20 of the Property Tax Code. We disagree. The legislature established section 23—20 to compensate taxpayers for the time value of money during the period their tax payments were held by the collector. Section 2—1303, by contrast, governs the post-judgment phase, ensuring that a judgment debtor does not benefit from delaying payment by appealing the judgment.

As noted in *American National Bank & Trust Co. v. City of Chicago*, 227 Ill. 2d 64, 879 N.E.2d 897 (2007), statutory schemes must be interpreted to give effect to the legislature's intent and to avoid absurd results. *American National Bank*, 227 Ill. 2d at 72, 879 N.E.2d at 902. It would be an absurd result to allow the collector to stay the payment of the fixed interest differential during the appeal, thereby depriving the taxpayers of the use of those funds for years, without any compensation. ## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Award of Judgment Interest Constitutes Impermissible Compounding of Interest

The fundamental principle underlying Illinois law on judgment interest is that interest shall not be awarded on interest. This Court held in *City of Chicago v. Chicago & North Western Railway Co.*, 326 Ill. 588, 592, 158 N.E. 417, 419 (1926), that judgment interest applies only to the "amount of the judgment," excluding amounts that represent interest or additional damages, to prevent improper compounding. The Court specifically stated that "interest is allowed on the amount of the judgment from its date, but not on the interest which may have accrued thereon." *City of Chicago*, 326 Ill. at 592, 158 N.E. at 419.

Similarly, in *People ex rel. Dugan v. Klass*, 258 Ill. App. 3d 104, 112, 630 N.E.2d 928, 934 (1994), this Court emphasized that section 2-1303 "does not provide for interest on interest." The court in *Klass* explicitly recognized that the prohibition against compounding interest is a fundamental principle that must be enforced to prevent judgment debtors from being subjected to exponentially growing obligations.

The Collector acknowledges the plaintiffs' attempt to distinguish the present case by arguing that the interest differential constitutes a "fixed" judgment rather than "accruing" interest. However, this distinction is unpersuasive because the interest differential at issue here represents the difference between two interest rates—both of which are calculated based on the time value of money during the period the tax payments were held. To award judgment interest on this differential interest amount would permit the plaintiffs to recover interest on interest, which directly contravenes the holding in *City of Chicago* and the principle articulated in *Klass*.

The appellate court's reliance on *Schaumburg v. Illinois State Toll Highway Authority*, 274 Ill. App. 3d 827, 835, 654 N.E.2d 1060, 1064 (1995), is misplaced. In *Schaumburg*, the court awarded judgment interest on "delay damages" that were not interest-based but rather constituted compensation for the delay in receiving a fixed sum of money. Here, by contrast, the differential interest amount is inherently interest-based—it represents the difference between what the plaintiffs claim they were owed under the pre-amendment 5% rate and what they actually received under the post-amendment CPI rate. Both components are interest calculated for the same period. Awarding judgment interest on this amount constitutes compounding in its most literal form.

### B. The Award of Judgment Interest Conflicts with the Legislative Intent of Section 23-20

The legislature enacted section 23-20 of the Property Tax Code to compensate taxpayers for the time value of money during the period their tax payments were held by the collector in protest. The statute establishes a comprehensive scheme for calculating the appropriate interest rate on tax refunds. The 2006 amendment to section 23-20 reflects the legislature's determination that the CPI-based rate better serves the interests of both taxpayers and taxing bodies in light of changing economic conditions.

When the trial court entered judgment orders fixing the amount of interest owed under section 23-20, it necessarily determined the total compensation to which the plaintiffs were entitled for the time their funds were held. To then award additional judgment interest under section 2-1303 on this fixed amount would effectively rewrite the legislative bargain embedded in section 23-20. The plaintiffs received exactly what the legislature contemplated: interest calculated at the applicable rate(s) for the period the funds were held.

In *American National Bank & Trust Co. v. City of Chicago*, 227 Ill. 2d 64, 72, 879 N.E.2d 897, 902 (2007), this Court instructed that statutory schemes must be interpreted to give effect to the legislature's intent and to avoid absurd results. However, the appellate court's construction of sections 23-20 and 2-1303 produces an absurd result, not a harmonious one. The legislature established section 23-20 as a complete and integrated scheme for calculating interest on tax refunds. By superimposing section 2-1303 judgment interest on the section 23-20 interest differential, the appellate court effectively created a windfall for taxpayers that the legislature never intended.

The Collector's position does not, as the appellate court suggested, allow taxpayers to be deprived of the use of their funds during the appeal without compensation. The judgment interest provision of section 2-1303 was designed to compensate judgment creditors for the delay in receiving payment of a fixed sum—not to provide a bonus on top of interest already calculated under a separate statutory scheme.

The interest differential is not a principal amount or a sum representing damages distinct from interest. The plaintiffs' reliance on *Schaumburg* is misplaced. In *Schaumburg*, the delay damages were not interest-based but rather represented the value of the delay in receiving a fixed sum. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

After careful consideration of the closing arguments presented by both the plaintiff taxpayers and the Cook County Collector (defendant), we must determine whether the trial court erred in awarding judgment interest pursuant to section 2—1303 of the Code of Civil Procedure (735 ILCS 5/2—1303 (West 2006)) on the fixed amount of interest owed under section 23—20 of the Property Tax Code (35 ILCS 200/23—20 (West 2006)). While our preliminary ruling was persuaded by the plaintiffs' characterization of the interest differential as a "fixed judgment," the defendant's closing argument provides a compelling distinction regarding the inherent nature of the funds at issue. Upon further reflection, we find that the prohibition against "interest on interest" controls the outcome of this case. Therefore, we reverse the judgment of the appellate court insofar as it affirmed the trial court’s award of judgment interest.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Prohibition Against Interest on Interest**

The dispositive issue is whether the "interest differential"—the difference between the 5% interest rate and the CPI-based rate—constitutes "interest" for purposes of the prohibition against compounding. The defendant correctly identifies the controlling principle established in *City of Chicago v. Chicago & North Western Railway Co.*, 326 Ill. 588, 158 N.E. 417 (1926). In *City of Chicago*, the Supreme Court held that "interest is allowed on the amount of the judgment from its date, but not on the interest which may have accrued thereon." *City of Chicago*, 326 Ill. at 592, 158 N.E. at 419. This rule was reaffirmed in *People ex rel. Dugan v. Klass*, 258 Ill. App. 3d 104, 630 N.E.2d 928 (1994), where the court stated that section 2—1303 "does not provide for interest on interest." *Klass*, 258 Ill. App. 3d at 112, 630 N.E.2d at 934.

The plaintiffs argue that once the interest differential was fixed by the trial court’s order, it transformed into a "judgment for the payment of money" subject to section 2—1303, relying on *Schaumburg v. Illinois State Toll Highway Authority*, 274 Ill. App. 3d 827, 654 N.E.2d 1060 (1995). However, the defendant persuasively distinguishes *Schaumburg* on the grounds that the "delay damages" at issue there were not inherently interest-based. In *Schaumburg*, the damages were compensation for the delay in receiving a fixed sum, distinct from the time value of money calculated on the principal debt. *Schaumburg*, 274 Ill. App. 3d at 835, 654 N.E.2d at 1064.

Here, the differential interest is structurally identical to interest. It is the mathematical difference between two interest rates calculated on the same principal for the same time period. It represents nothing more than the time value of money on the tax overpayment. To characterize this sum as anything other than "interest which may have accrued thereon" would ignore the economic reality of the calculation. As the defendant argues, awarding judgment interest on this differential would permit the plaintiffs to recover interest on interest, directly contravening the holding in *City of Chicago*.

**B. Legislative Intent and Statutory Harmony**

The defendant further contends that awarding judgment interest conflicts with the comprehensive legislative scheme of section 23—20. We agree. The legislature enacted section 23—20 to provide a complete remedy for the recovery of tax refunds, including the interest to be paid thereon. As stated in *American National Bank & Trust Co. v. City of Chicago*, 227 Ill. 2d 64, 879 N.E.2d 897 (2007), statutory schemes must be interpreted to give effect to the legislature's intent and to avoid absurd results. *American National Bank*, 227 Ill. 2d at 72, 879 N.E.2d at 902.

The plaintiffs’ interpretation would effectively rewrite section 23—20. If the legislature intended for taxpayers to receive judgment interest on the differential interest during an appeal, it could have explicitly provided for such a mechanism. Instead, it established a specific interest rate (the lesser of 5% or CPI) to govern the refund. Superimposing the general judgment interest statute of section 2—1303 onto this specific scheme creates a windfall for taxpayers that the legislature never intended. It allows them to earn interest on the interest differential at a rate (6% under section 2—1301 or the statutory rate under section 2—1303) that may exceed the statutory rate established by the legislature for the refund itself.

**C. Procedural Preservation**

While the defendant acknowledges procedural deficiencies in the notices of appeal, we agree that the issue was properly preserved for review under the principles of *Marsh v. Illinois State Democratic Party*, 226 Ill. 2d 550, 878 N.E.2d 1 (2007). *Marsh*, 226 Ill. 2d at 560, 878 N.E.2d at 9. The collector objected to the award of judgment interest in the trial court and fully briefed the issue on appeal. Therefore, we reach the merits of the case.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the trial court erred in awarding judgment interest under section 2—1303 on the fixed amount of interest owed under section 23—20. The interest differential is, by definition, "interest which may have accrued" on the principal tax overpayment. Applying judgment interest to this amount constitutes an impermissible compounding of interest prohibited by *City of Chicago* and *Klass*. Furthermore, such an award disrupts the comprehensive legislative scheme established by section 23—20.

Accordingly, the judgment of the appellate court is reversed, and the cause is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
