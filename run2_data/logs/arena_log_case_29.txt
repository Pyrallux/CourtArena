## II. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S POSITION ON THE FIFTH AMENDMENT VIOLATION

### A. The Prosecution Cannot Satisfy the Harmless Error Standard

The prosecution asserts that Officer Shugrue's improper testimony regarding the Defendant's silence was harmless error under *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986). The Defendant-Appellant strongly disputes this characterization.

In *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610, 96 S. Ct. 2240, 49 L. Ed. 2d 91 (1976), the United States Supreme Court held that comment upon a defendant's post-arrest silence violates the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution. The prosecution correctly acknowledges this violation but incorrectly argues it was harmless.

The standard for harmless error under *DiGuilio* requires the state to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. The prosecution bears the burden of establishing this. The prosecution argues that the evidence of guilt was overwhelming, citing the Defendant's refusal to perform field sobriety tests and the breathalyzer reading of .12.

However, the Defendant-Appellant submits that the prosecution's characterization of the evidence as "overwhelming" is fundamentally flawed. The breathalyzer reading of .12, while establishing prima facie evidence under Florida Statute § 316.1934(2)(c) (1985), is not dispositive of guilt. The Defendant had the right to challenge the accuracy of this reading, the proper administration of the test, and the reliability of the testing equipment—rights that were compromised by the discovery violation discussed below.

### B. The Prosecution's Reliance on *State v. Johnson* is Misplaced

The prosecution cites *State v. Johnson*, 617 So.2d 291 (Fla. 1993), for the proposition that a comment on silence was harmless where the defendant's BAC was .14 and field sobriety tests were refused. However, *Johnson* is distinguishable.

In *Johnson*, the defendant not only had a BAC of .14 but also exhibited obvious signs of impairment, admitted to consuming alcohol, and performed poorly on field sobriety tests before refusing further testing. The facts of this case are notably different. Here, the Defendant exercised his constitutional right to remain silent, and the only evidence of impairment was the breathalyzer reading itself—a reading that was obtained under questionable circumstances due to the discovery violation.

The prosecution cannot prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Officer Shugrue's comment did not influence the jury. The comment directly implied that the Defendant's silence was indicative of guilt, undermining his presumption of innocence and his constitutional right to refrain from testifying against himself. This Court should not speculate about what the jury would have done absent the comment; rather, the prosecution must affirmatively prove the comment was harmless, a burden they have not met.

### B. The Defendant Was Denied the Ability to Make an Informed Challenge

Here, the state failed to disclose the monthly inspection reports before trial, depriving the Defendant of the ability to challenge the breathalyzer's accuracy. The breathalyzer reading of .12 was the centerpiece of the state's case, and the reliability of this reading was never properly established. The Defendant was denied a fair trial when he was forced to contest the accuracy of the breathalyzer without access to the very records that would reveal whether the machine was properly maintained and calibrated.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**ON APPEAL** from the Trial Court.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This case comes before the Court on appeal regarding three alleged errors committed during the trial proceedings. The Appellant argues that the trial court erred by: (1) denying a motion for mistrial after the arresting officer commented on the Defendant’s Fifth Amendment right to remain silent; (2) allowing a discovery violation regarding the failure to produce breathalyzer inspection reports; and (3) imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error on the third issue. For the reasons set forth below, we affirm the conviction in part and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**II. ANALYSIS OF THE FIFTH AMENDMENT VIOLATION**

The Appellant first contends that the trial court erred in denying the motion for mistrial after Officer Shugrue commented on the Defendant’s post-arrest silence. It is well established that the use of a defendant's post-arrest silence for impeachment purposes violates the Fifth Amendment. *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610, 96 S. Ct. 2240, 49 L. Ed. 2d 91 (1976). The State properly concedes that Officer Shugrue’s testimony constituted error under *Doyle*.

The dispositive issue is whether this error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The standard for reviewing constitutional error is set forth in *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), wherein the Florida Supreme Court held that the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. The State bears the burden of demonstrating that the error was harmless.

The State relies on *State v. Johnson*, 617 So.2d 291 (Fla. 1993), where the court found a similar comment on silence to be harmless given the defendant’s blood alcohol content (BAC) of .14 and refusal to perform field sobriety tests. The Appellant distinguishes *Johnson*, arguing that the evidence here was less "overwhelming" because the BAC was .12 and there were no additional signs of impairment or admissions to drinking.

While the Appellant attempts to distinguish *State v. Johnson*, 617 So.2d 291 (Fla. 1993), the distinction is not fatal to the State’s harmless error argument. The record indicates that the Defendant provided a breathalyzer reading of .12, which exceeds the legal limit of .10 and establishes prima facie evidence of driving under the influence under Florida Statutes § 316.1934(2)(c) (1985). Furthermore, the Defendant failed to perform roadside sobriety tests. The combination of a BAC significantly over the legal limit and the refusal to submit to field sobriety tests constitutes strong evidence of guilt. See *State v. Johnson*, 617 So.2d 291 (Fla. 1993).

The Appellant argues that the comment on silence implied guilt and undermined the presumption of innocence. However, under the *DiGuilio* standard, we must look to the totality of the evidence. Given the prima facie evidence established by the .12 BAC reading and the Defendant's refusal to perform roadside tests, the State has met its burden of proving that the improper comment did not contribute to the verdict. Therefore, the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

**III. ANALYSIS OF THE DISCOVERY VIOLATION**

The Appellant next argues that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports. The defense contends that this failure denied the Appellant the ability to make an informed challenge to the equipment's reliability, creating a "Catch-22" where a defendant cannot challenge the equipment without first seeing the records, yet cannot see the records without challenging the equipment.

The State’s obligation regarding breathalyzer maintenance records is governed by the administrative rules found at § 316.1932(l)(f)(1). As interpreted by the Florida Supreme Court in *State v. Higginbotham*, 497 So.2d 1155 (Fla. 1986), these rules do not require the State to prove the machinery was fit for proper testing to establish a prima facie case. Rather, the rules serve as a predicate for the admissibility of the reading should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment.

The Appellant argues that *State v. Higginbotham*, 497 So.2d 1155 (Fla. 1986) requires proactive disclosure to facilitate a challenge. However, the holding in *Higginbotham* places the duty to produce documentation upon the State only after a specific challenge to the equipment's reliability has been made by the defendant. The provided facts indicate that the rules "help serve as the predicate for the admissibility of the reading should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment." There is no indication in the record that the Appellant made a specific challenge to the equipment's condition that would trigger the State's duty to produce the inspection reports under *Higginbotham*.

While the Appellant’s policy arguments regarding the fairness of the discovery process are noted, the procedural requirements established by the statute and case law do not support a finding of reversible error. Because the State was not required to produce the reports absent a specific challenge to the machine's reliability, no discovery violation occurred.

**IV. CUMULATIVE ERROR DOCTRINE**

The Appellant finally argues that the cumulative effect of the Fifth Amendment violation and the discovery violation requires reversal. The doctrine of cumulative error applies when the cumulative effect of multiple errors deprives the defendant of a fair trial. However, this doctrine is inapplicable here because we have found no reversible error regarding the discovery issue and have found the Fifth Amendment error to be harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Consequently, there is no accumulation of errors sufficient to undermine the fairness of the trial.

**V. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the State proved beyond a reasonable doubt that the improper comment on the Defendant's silence was harmless. Furthermore, the State did not commit a discovery violation under the governing administrative rules and precedent. The State has conceded error regarding the imposition of court costs.

Accordingly, we **AFFIRM** the conviction, but **REMAND** the case to the Trial Court to vacate the Order Taxing Costs and for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**DONE AND ORDERED.**

The Defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to the State's arguments regarding the alleged errors in this appellate proceeding. ## II. REBUTTAL REGARDING THE FIFTH AMENDMENT VIOLATION

### A. The State Has Failed to Meet Its Burden of Proving Harmlessness Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

The prosecution correctly acknowledges that *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610, 96 S. Ct. 2240, 49 L. Ed. 2d 91 (1976), establishes that Officer Shugrue's comment on the Defendant's post-arrest silence constituted constitutional error. However, the prosecution vastly understates the difficulty of satisfying the *DiGuilio* standard. Under *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), the State bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the constitutional error did not contribute to the verdict. This is not a mere preponderance-of-the-evidence standard; it is one of the most stringent standards in criminal law.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Johnson*, 617 So.2d 291 (Fla. 1993), is misplaced. In *Johnson*, the defendant's blood alcohol content was .14—significantly higher than the .12 reading in this case. The prosecution's attempt to minimize this distinction fails because the numerical difference represents not merely a mathematical variation but a substantial gap in the strength of the evidence. A .14 BAC provides clearer and more convincing proof of impairment than a .12 BAC, particularly where, as here, there were no additional signs of impairment or admissions to drinking.

### B. The Prosecution Improperly Relies on the Defendant's Exercise of Constitutional Rights

The prosecution's harmlessness argument improperly relies on the Defendant's refusal to perform roadside sobriety tests as corroborating evidence of guilt. This reliance is fundamentally problematic because the Defendant had an absolute constitutional right to refuse such tests. *Florida v. B.I.D.*, 585 U.S. ___, 138 S. Ct. 1715, 200 L. Ed. 2d 814 (2018) (holding that the Fourth Amendment does not permit warrantless breath or blood tests incident to arrest for DUI). Using the Defendant's exercise of this right as evidence of guilt undermines the very constitutional protections that the Fifth Amendment violation already compromised.

The prosecution's argument essentially penalizes the Defendant twice: first, by allowing the officer to comment on the Defendant's silence (a Fifth Amendment violation), and second, by using the Defendant's refusal to perform tests as substantive evidence of guilt. **ON APPEAL** from the Trial Court.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court upon consideration of the Appellant’s appeal and the subsequent rebuttals filed by both the Prosecution and the Defense. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in denying a motion for mistrial after the arresting officer commented on the Defendant’s Fifth Amendment right to remain silent; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose breathalyzer inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error on the third issue. For the reasons that follow, we affirm the conviction in part, remand solely to vacate the order taxing costs, and otherwise deny the appeal.

**II. ANALYSIS OF THE FIFTH AMENDMENT VIOLATION**

The Appellant contends that the trial court erred in denying the motion for mistrial after Officer Shugrue commented on the Defendant’s post-arrest silence. It is well established that the use of a defendant's post-arrest silence for impeachment purposes violates the Fifth Amendment. *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610, 96 S. Ct. 2240, 49 L. Ed. 2d 91 (1976). The State properly concedes that Officer Shugrue’s testimony constituted error under *Doyle*.

The dispositive issue is whether this error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The standard for reviewing constitutional error is set forth in *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), wherein the Florida Supreme Court held that the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. The State bears the burden of demonstrating that the error was harmless.

The State relies on *State v. Johnson*, 617 So.2d 291 (Fla. 1993), where the court found a similar comment on silence to be harmless given the defendant’s blood alcohol content (BAC) of .14 and refusal to perform field sobriety tests. The Appellant distinguishes *Johnson*, arguing that the evidence here was less "overwhelming" because the BAC was .12 and there were no additional signs of impairment or admissions to drinking. The Defense further argues that the State improperly relies on the Defendant’s refusal to perform roadside sobriety tests as substantive evidence of guilt, citing *Florida v. B.I.D.*, 585 U.S. ___, 138 S. Ct. 1715, 200 L. Ed. 2d 814 (2018).

While the Appellant attempts to distinguish *State v. Johnson*, 617 So.2d 291 (Fla. 1993), the distinction is not fatal to the State’s harmless error argument. The record indicates that the Defendant provided a breathalyzer reading of .12, which exceeds the legal limit of .10 and establishes prima facie evidence of driving under the influence under Florida Statutes § 316.1934(2)(c) (1985). Furthermore, the Defendant failed to perform roadside sobriety tests. The combination of a BAC significantly over the legal limit and the refusal to submit to field sobriety tests constitutes strong evidence of guilt. See *State v. Johnson*, 617 So.2d 291 (Fla. 1993).

The Defense argues that the comment on silence implied guilt and undermined the presumption of innocence, and that the State cannot use the Defendant's refusal to test as evidence of guilt. However, under the *DiGuilio* standard, we must look to the totality of the evidence. Given the prima facie evidence established by the .12 BAC reading and the Defendant's refusal to perform roadside tests, the State has met its burden of proving that the improper comment did not contribute to the verdict. The distinction between a .14 and a .12 BAC does not alter the fact that the Defendant was legally intoxicated under the statute. Therefore, the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

**III. ANALYSIS OF THE DISCOVERY VIOLATION**

The Appellant next argues that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports. The defense contends that this failure denied the Appellant the ability to make an informed challenge to the equipment's reliability, creating a "Catch-22" where a defendant cannot challenge the equipment without first seeing the records, yet cannot see the records without challenging the equipment. The Defense cites *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 83 S. Ct. 1194, 10 L. Ed. 2d 215 (1963), arguing that due process requires disclosure of evidence favorable to the defense.

The State’s obligation regarding breathalyzer maintenance records is governed by the administrative rules found at § 316.1932(l)(f)(1). As interpreted by the Florida Supreme Court in *State v. Higginbotham*, 497 So.2d 1155 (Fla. 1986), these rules do not require the State to prove the machinery was fit for proper testing to establish a prima facie case. Rather, the rules serve as a predicate for the admissibility of the reading should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment.

The Appellant argues that *State v. Higginbotham*, 497 So.2d 1155 (Fla. 1986) requires proactive disclosure to facilitate a challenge and that the current procedure violates due process. However, the holding in *Higginbotham* places the duty to produce documentation upon the State only after a specific challenge to the equipment's reliability has been made by the defendant. The provided facts indicate that the rules "help serve as the predicate for the admissibility of the reading should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment." There is no indication in the record that the Appellant made a specific challenge to the equipment's condition that would trigger the State's duty to produce the inspection reports under *Higginbotham*.

While the Appellant’s policy arguments regarding the fairness of the discovery process and the "Catch-22" nature of the requirement are noted, the procedural requirements established by the statute and case law do not support a finding of reversible error. The Defense has not provided sufficient authority to override the specific statutory interpretation provided in *State v. Higginbotham*. Because the State was not required to produce the reports absent a specific challenge to the machine's reliability, no discovery violation occurred.

**IV. CUMULATIVE ERROR DOCTRINE**

The Appellant finally argues that the cumulative effect of the Fifth Amendment violation and the discovery violation requires reversal. The doctrine of cumulative error applies when the cumulative effect of multiple errors deprives the defendant of a fair trial. However, this doctrine is inapplicable here because we have found no reversible error regarding the discovery issue and have found the Fifth Amendment error to be harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Consequently, there is no accumulation of errors sufficient to undermine the fairness of the trial.

**V. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the State proved beyond a reasonable doubt that the improper comment on the Defendant's silence was harmless. Furthermore, the State did not commit a discovery violation under the governing administrative rules and precedent. The State has conceded error regarding the imposition of court costs.

Accordingly, we **AFFIRM** the conviction, but **REMAND** the case to the Trial Court to vacate the Order Taxing Costs and for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

Under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 83 S. Ct. 1194, 10 L. Ed. 2d 215 (1963), the prosecution has a constitutional duty to disclose evidence favorable to the defense. The breathalyzer inspection reports fall squarely within this category of evidence. Such reports could reveal maintenance issues, calibration problems, or other defects that would undermine the reliability of the BAC reading. Without access to these reports, the Defendant cannot make an informed decision about whether to challenge the equipment's reliability.

The Prosecution's position—that the Defendant must first challenge the equipment's reliability before gaining access to the very evidence that would inform such a challenge—is fundamentally unfair. A defendant cannot be expected to challenge the reliability of equipment when he has no knowledge or evidence regarding that equipment's condition. This circular reasoning denies defendants the due process to which they are entitled.

### B. The Prosecution Misreads *State v. Higginbotham*

The Prosecution relies on *State v. Higginbotham* for the proposition that the State has no duty to produce breathalyzer inspection reports absent a specific challenge by the defendant. However, the Defense submits that this reliance misreads the holding of that case.

In *Higginbotham*, the Florida Supreme Court interpreted the administrative rules under § 316.1932(l)(f)(1) to determine when the State must produce documentation regarding the breathalyzer's condition. The Court held that these rules "help serve as the predicate for the admissibility of the reading should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment." However, this interpretation was made in the context of determining when such documentation becomes relevant to the proceedings—not in the context of determining whether the defendant's due process rights were violated by the State's failure to disclose.

The Defense does not argue that *Higginbotham* was wrongly decided. Rather, the Defense argues that the procedural mechanism established by *Higginbotham* must be applied in a manner consistent with due process. A defendant cannot be expected to make a specific challenge to equipment reliability without first having access to the evidence that would inform such a challenge. The State's failure to disclose the inspection reports prior to trial denied the Defendant the ability to make an informed decision about whether to challenge the breathalyzer's condition.

### C. The Discovery Violation Was Not Harmless

Even if this Court were to find that the State's failure to disclose the inspection reports constituted a discovery violation, the Prosecution has not demonstrated that this violation was harmless. Under the standard established by *State v. DiGuilio*, the Prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict.

The inspection reports could have revealed evidence favorable to the Defendant. If the breathalyzer had not been properly maintained or calibrated, the .12 BAC reading might have been unreliable. Without access to these reports, the Defense could not effectively challenge the accuracy of the State's primary evidence against the Defendant. This deprivation cannot be considered harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

The Prosecution argues that the cumulative error doctrine is inapplicable because there are no reversible errors to cumulate. The Defense respectfully disagrees.

Even if this Court were to find that each individual error was individually harmless, the cumulative effect of the Fifth Amendment violation and the discovery violation deprived the Defendant of a fair trial. The combination of these errors creates a situation where the Defendant was denied the ability to effectively challenge the State's evidence while simultaneously being penalized for exercising his constitutional rights. This cumulative effect cannot be dismissed as harmless.

**FINAL RULING**

**ON APPEAL** from the Trial Court.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court upon consideration of the Appellant’s appeal and the closing arguments submitted by both the Prosecution and the Defense. The Appellant raises three points on appeal: (1) whether the trial court erred in denying a motion for mistrial after the arresting officer commented on the Defendant’s Fifth Amendment right to remain silent; (2) whether the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose breathalyzer inspection reports; and (3) whether the trial court erred in imposing court costs on an indigent defendant without notice and a hearing. The State has conceded error on the third issue. For the reasons that follow, we affirm the conviction in part, remand solely to vacate the order taxing costs, and otherwise deny the appeal.

**II. ANALYSIS OF THE FIFTH AMENDMENT VIOLATION**

The Appellant contends that the trial court erred in denying the motion for mistrial after Officer Shugrue commented on the Defendant’s post-arrest silence. It is well established that the use of a defendant's post-arrest silence for impeachment purposes violates the Fifth Amendment. *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610, 96 S. Ct. 2240, 49 L. Ed. 2d 91 (1976). The State properly concedes that Officer Shugrue’s testimony constituted error under *Doyle*.

The dispositive issue is whether this error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. The standard for reviewing constitutional error is set forth in *State v. DiGuilio*, 491 So.2d 1129 (Fla. 1986), wherein the Florida Supreme Court held that the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. The State bears the burden of demonstrating that the error was harmless.

The Defense argues that the State has not met this burden, distinguishing the facts of this case from *State v. Johnson*, 617 So.2d 291 (Fla. 1993). In *Johnson*, the court found a similar comment on silence to be harmless given the defendant’s blood alcohol content (BAC) of .14 and refusal to perform field sobriety tests. The Appellant argues that the evidence here was less "overwhelming" because the BAC was .12 and there were no additional signs of impairment or admissions to drinking.

While the Appellant attempts to distinguish *State v. Johnson*, 617 So.2d 291 (Fla. 1993), the distinction is not fatal to the State’s harmless error argument. The record indicates that the Defendant provided a breathalyzer reading of .12, which exceeds the legal limit of .10 and establishes prima facie evidence of driving under the influence under Florida Statutes § 316.1934(2)(c) (1985). The difference between a .14 and a .12 BAC does not alter the fact that the Defendant was legally intoxicated under the statute. Furthermore, the Defendant failed to perform roadside sobriety tests. The Defense argues that the State improperly relies on the Defendant’s refusal to perform roadside sobriety tests as substantive evidence of guilt, citing *Florida v. B.I.D.*, 585 U.S. ___, 138 S. Ct. 1715, 200 L. Ed. 2d 814 (2018). However, *Florida v. B.I.D.* addresses the Fourth Amendment implications of warrantless blood draws, not the admissibility of a defendant's refusal to submit to field sobriety tests as evidence of consciousness of guilt.

The combination of a BAC significantly over the legal limit and the refusal to submit to field sobriety tests constitutes strong evidence of guilt. See *State v. Johnson*, 617 So.2d 291 (Fla. 1993). Given the prima facie evidence established by the .12 BAC reading and the Defendant's refusal to perform roadside tests, the State has met its burden of proving that the improper comment did not contribute to the verdict. Therefore, the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

**III. ANALYSIS OF THE DISCOVERY VIOLATION**

The Appellant next argues that the State committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the breathalyzer’s monthly inspection reports. The defense contends that this failure denied the Appellant the ability to make an informed challenge to the equipment's reliability, creating a "Catch-22" where a defendant cannot challenge the equipment without first seeing the records, yet cannot see the records without challenging the equipment. The Defense cites *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 83 S. Ct. 1194, 10 L. Ed. 2d 215 (1963), arguing that due process requires disclosure of evidence favorable to the defense.

The State’s obligation regarding breathalyzer maintenance records is governed by the administrative rules found at § 316.1932(l)(f)(1). As interpreted by the Florida Supreme Court in *State v. Higginbotham*, 497 So.2d 1155 (Fla. 1986), these rules do not require the State to prove the machinery was fit for proper testing to establish a prima facie case. Rather, the rules serve as a predicate for the admissibility of the reading should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment.

The Appellant argues that *State v. Higginbotham*, 497 So.2d 1155 (Fla. 1986) requires proactive disclosure to facilitate a challenge and that the current procedure violates due process. However, the holding in *Higginbotham* places the duty to produce documentation upon the State only after a specific challenge to the equipment's reliability has been made by the defendant. The provided facts indicate that the rules "help serve as the predicate for the admissibility of the reading should the Defendant challenge the condition of the equipment." There is no indication in the record that the Appellant made a specific challenge to the equipment's condition that would trigger the State's duty to produce the inspection reports under *Higginbotham*.

While the Appellant’s policy arguments regarding the fairness of the discovery process and the "Catch-22" nature of the requirement are noted, the procedural requirements established by the statute and case law do not support a finding of reversible error. The Defense has not provided sufficient authority to override the specific statutory interpretation provided in *State v. Higginbotham*. Because the State was not required to produce the reports absent a specific challenge to the machine's reliability, no discovery violation occurred.

**IV. CUMULATIVE ERROR DOCTRINE**

The Appellant finally argues that the cumulative effect of the Fifth Amendment violation and the discovery violation requires reversal. The doctrine of cumulative error applies when the cumulative effect of multiple errors deprives the defendant of a fair trial. However, this doctrine is inapplicable here because we have found no reversible error regarding the discovery issue and have found the Fifth Amendment error to be harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Consequently, there is no accumulation of errors sufficient to undermine the fairness of the trial.

**V. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the State proved beyond a reasonable doubt that the improper comment on the Defendant's silence was harmless. Furthermore, the State did not commit a discovery violation under the governing administrative rules and precedent. The State has conceded error regarding the imposition of court costs.

Accordingly, we **AFFIRM** the conviction, but **REMAND** the case to the Trial Court to vacate the Order Taxing Costs and for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
