FACTS
In July 2005, appellant Stacy Juetten went to the LasikPlus Vision Center in Edina for a preoperative assessment to determine if she was suited for vision-correcting eye surgery. Juetten’s medical history indicated that she was diabetic and had a hyperactive thyroid. Poorly controlled diabetes and hyperthyroidism can diminish a patient’s fitness for the surgery. But in Juetten’s case, both conditions were under control with medication, and neither condition had damaged her eyes.
A physician examined Juetten’s eyes and discovered superficial punctuate keratitis (SPK). SPK is a condition involving pinpoint inflammations on the eye’s surface. The presence of SPK makes surgery less advisable because surgery can aggravate existing SPK and cause dry-eye syndrome. But Juetten’s SPK was “trace” — the lowest level of SPK detectable — and could have resulted from the examination itself. Because Juetten’s SPK seemed minimal and she had no history of dry-eye problems, the examining doctor informed her that she was a good candidate for surgery. The doctor also discussed with Juetten the potential common complications of surgery, including dryness and vision problems.
Juetten returned to LasikPlus Vision Center for her surgery, which was performed by Dr. David Whiting. Before operating, Dr. Whiting generally reviews a patient’s medical records, examines her eyes, and determines whether she is a good candidate for surgery. Dr. Whiting was aware that Juetten had trace SPK in both eyes but did not discuss the potential cumulative effect of having SPK, diabetes, and a history of thyroid abnormality with Juetten before the surgery.
Within days after her surgery, Juetten developed severe SPK, dry eyes, and vision problems. After efforts to treat these problems failed, Juetten sued respondent LCA-Vision, which owned and operated LasikPlus Vision Center. Juetten based her claim against LCA-Vision on its vicarious liability for the alleged negligence of Dr. Whiting and the examining doctor, who she claimed had failed to recognize that her pre-existing conditions were contraindications to surgery and failed to treat her SPK or warn her of the risks of and alternatives to surgery.
*775A plaintiff in a medical malpractice suit must serve on the defendant, within 180 days after commencement of the suit, affidavits identifying the expert witnesses the plaintiff expects to testify on her behalf and the substance of the experts’ testimony. MinmStat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4 (2008). The penalty for noncompliance with the statutory requirement is mandatory dismissal with prejudice of “each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case.” Id., subd. 6(b) (2008).
Juetten failed to serve LCA-Vision with the required affidavits within the 180-day deadline. LCA-Vision therefore moved to dismiss.  This amendment essentially changed Dr. Whiting’s status from nonparty tortfeasor to liable tortfea-sor for the same conduct that formed the basis of the procedurally faltering vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision.
The district court held that there was no good cause to extend the 180-day affidavit-service deadline and granted LCA-Vision’s motion to dismiss based on Juetten’s failure to timely serve her expert affidavits. That same month, Juetten served expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting. Dr. Whiting moved to dismiss, arguing that the content of Juetten’s expert affidavits did not comply with the substantive requirements of Minn.Stat. § 145.682. Dr. Whiting also moved for summary judgment on procedural grounds based on law of the case, res judicata, and collateral estoppel since the deadline-related basis for dismissal had already been established.
In February 2009, the district court granted Dr. Whiting summary judgment based on law of the case but stated that the substance of Juetten’s expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. Juetten appeals the grant of summary judgment. Dr. Whiting filed notices of review and challenges the district court’s dicta approving the substance of Juetten’s affidavits and its rejection of his alternative arguments for res judicata and collateral estoppel.

## INTRODUCTION

Respondent Dr. David Whiting respectfully requests that this Court affirm the district court's grant of summary judgment in his favor. ## STATEMENT OF FACTS

The relevant facts are largely undisputed and were properly set forth in the district court's findings.

Stacy Juetten presented to LasikPlus Vision Center in July 2005 for a preoperative assessment for LASIK surgery. She had a medical history significant for diabetes and hyperthyroidism, both of which were controlled with medication. An examination revealed "trace" superficial punctuate keratitis (SPK), the lowest detectable level, which the examining physician determined did not preclude surgery. Juetten was informed she was a good candidate, and the doctor discussed potential complications including dryness and vision problems.

Dr. Whiting performed the surgery in July 2005. Prior to operating, he reviewed Juetten's records and examined her eyes. He was aware of the trace SPK but determined Juetten remained a good surgical candidate. Within days following surgery, Juetten developed severe SPK, dry eyes, and vision problems.

Juetten commenced this action against LCA-Vision, which owned and operated LasikPlus, asserting vicarious liability for the alleged negligence of Dr. Whiting and the examining doctor. Critically, Juetten failed to serve the required expert affidavits on LCA-Vision within 180 days of commencing the suit, as mandated by Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subds. 2(2), 4. LCA-Vision moved to dismiss, and the district court granted the motion, dismissing Juetten's claims with prejudice.

Thereafter, Juetten served expert affidavits on Dr. Whiting. Dr. Whiting moved for summary judgment, arguing that the affidavits were untimely and that the law of the case precluded relitigation of the procedural issues already resolved against Juetten. Juetten now appeals the grant of summary judgment to Dr. Whiting.

---

## LEGAL ANALYSIS

### I. The District Court Correctly Applied the Law of the Case Doctrine

The district court properly granted summary judgment based on the law of the case. When the district court dismissed Juetten's claims against LCA-Vision for failure to comply with the 180-day affidavit deadline, that ruling became binding on all related claims arising from the same cause of action. Juetten's claim against Dr. Whiting is not a separate cause of action—it is part of the same medical malpractice claim arising from the identical surgical procedure. The factual nexus between the two claims is undeniable: both depend on allegations that the performance of LASIK surgery on Juetten breached the applicable standard of care.

The law of the case doctrine provides that when a court decides a rule of law, that decision governs the same issues in subsequent proceedings in the same case. *N. States Power Co. v. Metro. Utils. Dist.*, 258 Minn. 221, 224, 100 N.W.2d 661, 664 (1960). This doctrine applies with particular force to procedural determinations that go to the heart of a plaintiff's ability to maintain an action. Here, the dismissal of LCA-Vision necessarily encompassed the underlying claim of negligence in performing the surgery—the same negligence alleged against Dr. Whiting. To hold otherwise would allow a plaintiff to accomplish through indirection what the statute expressly forbids: maintaining a medical malpractice claim without timely expert support.

The plaintiff argues that her claim against Dr. Whiting is somehow distinct from her claim against LCA-Vision. This argument fails. Both claims arise from the exact same operative facts: the July 2005 LASIK surgery performed by Dr. Whiting at the LasikPlus facility. The vicarious liability claim against LCA-Vision necessarily depended on establishing Dr. Whiting's negligence. Having failed to timely serve expert affidavits supporting that proposition, Juetten cannot now evade the statutory mandate by naming Dr. Whiting directly.

### II. The Mandatory Statutory Requirement Was Properly Enforced

Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b), provides that failure to serve expert affidavits within 180 days requires dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case." The statute admits no discretion. As the Minnesota Supreme Court held in *Sorenson v. St. Paul Ramsey Medical Center*, 457 N.W.2d 188, 191 (Minn. 1990), the affidavit requirement is "a jurisdictional condition precedent" to maintaining a medical malpractice action, and failure to comply "bars the action entirely."

The plaintiff contends that this Court should affirm the dismissal based on the statute's plain language. We agree—and that reasoning compels affirmance of the summary judgment entered in Dr. Whiting's favor. The statute's mandatory language applies not only to the specific defendant named in the untimely affidavit, but to all claims arising from the same cause of action. *See Olson v. Klemm*, 710 N.W.2d 379, 382 (Minn. App. 2006) (affirming dismissal of all defendants for procedural noncompliance because "the claims against each defendant arose from the same operative facts and required expert testimony to establish liability").

The district court's application of law of the case was not merely reasonable—it was compelled by the statute's express terms and binding precedent.

### III. The District Court Correctly Rejected Juetten's Attempt to Relitigate the Procedural Issue

Juetten cannot now challenge the procedural dismissal that formed the basis of the law of the case. She had a full and fair opportunity to oppose LCA-Vision's motion to dismiss. She failed to demonstrate good cause for extending the 180-day deadline. The district court rejected her arguments, and she did not appeal that ruling. Having lost on the procedural issue in the first instance, Juetten cannot resurrect her claim by naming a different defendant in a subsequent motion.

The district court properly declined to reconsider the procedural default. The doctrines of res judicata and collateral estoppel, while not necessary to the disposition, provide additional support. Juetten is seeking to relitigate the exact same issue—whether her failure to timely serve expert affidavits requires dismissal—against a different defendant. The answer, under both the statute and controlling precedent, remains the same.

### IV. The Dicta Regarding Affidavit Substance Does Not Require Reversal

The district court's observation, in dicta, that Juetten's expert affidavits would have satisfied the statutory substantive requirements had they been timely served does not alter the analysis. This Court should not confuse the procedural requirement—which is jurisdictional and mandatory—with the substantive content of the affidavits. The statute contains both procedural (timing) and substantive (content) requirements, and Juetten failed the former. *Sorenson*, 457 N.W.2d at 191.

The plaintiff argues that the substance of her affidavits is somehow relevant to this appeal. It is not. The procedural default alone is fatal. The district court's comment on substantive adequacy was unnecessary to its holding and does not affect the validity of the summary judgment.

Moreover, Dr. Whiting raised alternative arguments below regarding res judicata and collateral estoppel. City of Minneapolis*, 308 Minn. 374, 378, 241 N.W.2d 801, 804 (1976).

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on an appeal from the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of respondent Dr. David Whiting. Appellant Stacy Juetten appeals the dismissal of her medical malpractice claim, which was predicated on the same surgical conduct that formed the basis of a previously dismissed claim against LCA-Vision. The Court has reviewed the opening arguments provided by the parties and the factual record. Based on the arguments presented and the applicable law, the Court finds that the district court’s decision to grant summary judgment was proper.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Statutory Affidavit Requirement is Mandatory and Jurisdictional**

The central issue in this appeal is whether Appellant’s failure to serve expert affidavits on LCA-Vision within the statutory period bars her subsequent claim against Dr. Whiting. Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) (2008) mandates that a plaintiff must serve affidavits identifying expert witnesses and the substance of their testimony within 180 days of commencing a medical malpractice suit. The statute prescribes a specific penalty for noncompliance: mandatory dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case."

The arguments presented correctly identify that this requirement is not merely procedural but is a jurisdictional condition precedent. As established by the Minnesota Supreme Court in *Sorenson v. St. Paul Ramsey Medical Center*, 457 N.W.2d 188, 191 (Minn. 1990), the affidavit requirement is "a jurisdictional condition precedent" to maintaining a medical malpractice action, and failure to comply "bars the action entirely." Because Appellant failed to serve the required affidavits on LCA-Vision within the 180-day deadline, the district court was compelled to dismiss that claim. The arguments establish that this statutory bar is rigid and leaves no room for judicial discretion to overlook the procedural default.

**B. Dismissal Extends to All Claims Arising from the Same Operative Facts**

Appellant contends that her claim against Dr. Whiting is distinct from the dismissed claim against LCA-Vision. However, the arguments presented demonstrate that both claims arise from the identical operative facts: the LASIK surgery performed in July 2005. The claim against LCA-Vision was based on vicarious liability for Dr. Whiting’s alleged negligence, while the current claim asserts direct liability for that same negligence.

The Court finds persuasive the authority cited in the opening arguments, specifically *Olson v. Klemm*, 710 N.W.2d 379, 382 (Minn. App. 2006), which affirms the dismissal of all defendants for procedural noncompliance when "the claims against each defendant arose from the same operative facts and required expert testimony to establish liability." Similarly, *In re: Estate of Johnson*, 745 N.W.2d 290, 294 (Minn. App. 2008), emphasizes that Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b), requires dismissal of the entire cause of action for failure to serve expert affidavits within 180 days, regardless of whether other defendants are named separately.

Here, the medical issues involved—diabetes, hyperthyroidism, and trace SPK—necessitate expert testimony to establish a prima facie case of negligence. Because Appellant failed to meet the statutory deadline in the context of the LCA-Vision litigation, and because the claim against Dr. Whiting relies on the same evidence of negligence and the same underlying medical facts, the statutory dismissal must extend to Dr. Whiting. To allow Appellant to proceed against Dr. Whiting would permit an end-run around the statute’s mandatory penalty, effectively nullifying the dismissal of the LCA-Vision claim.

**C. The Law of the Case Doctrine Precludes Relitigation**

The district court granted summary judgment based on the law of the case doctrine. The arguments correctly note that this doctrine provides that when a court decides upon a rule of law, that decision should govern the same issues in subsequent stages of the litigation. *N. States Power Co. v. Metro. Utils. Dist.*, 258 Minn. 221, 224, 100 N.W.2d 661, 664 (1960).

The district court’s initial dismissal of the LCA-Vision claim established that Appellant’s cause of action for medical malpractice regarding the July 2005 surgery was procedurally barred due to the lack of timely expert affidavits. This procedural ruling is binding on the parties in subsequent proceedings involving the same case. As argued, Appellant had a full opportunity to contest the dismissal of the LCA-Vision claim and failed to do so successfully. She cannot now circumvent that final judgment by asserting the same negligence claim against a different defendant. The district court’s application of the law of the case doctrine was therefore appropriate.

**D. The Substance of the Affidavits is Irrelevant**

Appellant places significant weight on the district court’s dicta suggesting that the substance of her expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. However, as argued, this observation does not cure the procedural defect. The jurisdictional nature of the 180-day deadline means that timeliness is a prerequisite to the court considering the substance of the testimony. *Sorenson*, 457 N.W.2d at 191. The arguments presented accurately state that the procedural default is fatal to the claim, regardless of the substantive quality of the expert testimony eventually proffered.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the Court finds that the district court did not err in granting summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting. The mandatory requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 were not met, and the law of the case doctrine precludes Appellant from relitigating the procedural deficiencies of her medical malpractice claim against a different defendant. The arguments in favor of affirmance are consistent with statutory mandates and controlling precedent.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.

The district court's dismissal of the LCA-Vision claim established a final, binding ruling that the appellant could not proceed with her medical malpractice claim arising from the July 2005 surgery because she failed to timely serve expert affidavits. The appellant had a full and fair opportunity to challenge that ruling and failed to do so successfully. She cannot now resurrect the same claim against Dr. Whiting by relitigating the procedural defect.

The defense submits that the law of the case doctrine serves the essential purposes of promoting judicial efficiency and preventing endless relitigation of issues already resolved. To allow the appellant to proceed against Dr. Whiting would permit an end-run around the final judgment in the LCA-Vision litigation and undermine the finality that the law of the case doctrine is designed to protect.

---

## V. THE SUBSTANCE OF THE AFFIDAVITS IS IRRELEVANT TO THE TIMELINESS INQUIRY

The defense notes that the appellant places significant weight on the district court's observation that the substance of her expert affidavits "would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served." However, this observation is mere dicta and does not cure the procedural defect.

As *Sorenson makes clear, 457 N.W.2d at 191, timeliness is a threshold jurisdictional requirement that must be satisfied before the court can ever assess the substantive adequacy of the affidavits. The statute creates a two-step process: first, timely service of affidavits; second, substantive review of their content. The appellant failed at the first step, and that failure is fatal, regardless of how substantial her expert testimony might have been.

The defense submits that the appellant cannot rely on post-hoc justifications for her procedural default. The statute's purpose is to ensure that defendants receive notice of the expert testimony against them within a reasonable time, not to allow plaintiffs to proffer expert affidavits whenever convenient and then argue that the substance would have been sufficient.

## VI. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS SUPPORT AFFIRMANCE

The defense submits that the policy considerations underlying Minn. Stat. § 145.682 strongly support affirmance. The statute was enacted to address concerns about the cost and complexity of medical malpractice litigation and to ensure that plaintiffs bring their claims in a timely and orderly fashion. The mandatory affidavit requirement serves the dual purposes of weeding out frivolous claims early in the litigation and providing defendants with adequate notice of the evidence against them.

If this Court were to reverse the district court's judgment, plaintiffs would be free to ignore the 180-day deadline with impunity, knowing that they could simply sue additional defendants if their initial claims were dismissed for procedural noncompliance. This would render the statute's mandatory penalties meaningless and undermine the legislative intent behind the statute.

The defense submits that the appellant here engaged in procedural gamesmanship by first suing LCA-Vision on a vicarious liability theory, failing to comply with the statutory requirements, and then suing Dr. Whiting directly in an attempt to revive her claim. This tactic cannot be permitted.

The district court's decision was correct and should be upheld.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court upon review of the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of respondent Dr. David Whiting. Appellant Stacy Juetten appeals the dismissal of her medical malpractice claim, which asserts direct liability for the same surgical conduct that formed the basis of a previously dismissed claim against respondent LCA-Vision. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments of both the prosecution and the defense. Notably, both parties urge this Court to affirm the district court’s decision, relying on the mandatory nature of the statutory affidavit requirement, the unity of operative facts, and the law of the case doctrine. Based on the comprehensive arguments presented and the applicable law, the Court finds that the district court’s decision to grant summary judgment was proper and must be affirmed.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Statutory Affidavit Requirement Is Mandatory and Jurisdictional**

The threshold issue is whether Appellant’s failure to serve expert affidavits on LCA-Vision within the statutory period bars her subsequent claim against Dr. Whiting. Both the prosecution and defense correctly identify that Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b) (2008) mandates that a plaintiff must serve affidavits identifying expert witnesses and the substance of their testimony within 180 days of commencing a medical malpractice suit. The statute prescribes a specific penalty for noncompliance: mandatory dismissal with prejudice of "each cause of action as to which expert testimony is necessary to establish a prima facie case."

The arguments presented establish that this requirement is not merely procedural but is a jurisdictional condition precedent. As held by the Minnesota Supreme Court in *Sorenson v. St. Paul Ramsey Medical Center*, 457 N.W.2d 188, 191 (Minn. 1990), the affidavit requirement is "a jurisdictional condition precedent" to maintaining a medical malpractice action, and failure to comply "bars the action entirely." Because Appellant failed to serve the required affidavits on LCA-Vision within the 180-day deadline, the district court was compelled to dismiss that claim. The arguments demonstrate that this statutory bar is rigid and leaves no room for judicial discretion to overlook the procedural default.

**B. Dismissal Extends to All Claims Arising From the Same Operative Facts**

Appellant contends that her claim against Dr. Whiting is distinct from the dismissed claim against LCA-Vision. However, the arguments presented by both parties demonstrate that both claims arise from the identical operative facts: the LASIK surgery performed in July 2005. The claim against LCA-Vision was based on vicarious liability for Dr. Whiting’s alleged negligence, while the current claim asserts direct liability for that same negligence.

The Court finds the authority cited by both parties to be dispositive. Specifically, *Olson v. Klemm*, 710 N.W.2d 379, 382 (Minn. App. 2006), affirms the dismissal of all defendants for procedural noncompliance when "the claims against each defendant arose from the same operative facts and required expert testimony to establish liability." The arguments emphasize that Minn. Stat. § 145.682, subd. 6(b), requires dismissal of the entire cause of action for failure to serve expert affidavits within 180 days, regardless of whether other defendants are named separately.

Here, the medical issues involved—diabetes, hyperthyroidism, and trace SPK—necessitate expert testimony to establish a prima facie case of negligence. Because Appellant failed to meet the statutory deadline in the context of the LCA-Vision litigation, and because the claim against Dr. Whiting relies on the same evidence of negligence and the same underlying medical facts, the statutory dismissal must extend to Dr. Whiting. To allow Appellant to proceed against Dr. Whiting would permit an end-run around the statute’s mandatory penalty, effectively nullifying the dismissal of the LCA-Vision claim.

**C. The Law of the Case Doctrine Precludes Relitigation**

The district court granted summary judgment based on the law of the case doctrine. The arguments presented by both parties correctly note that this doctrine provides that when a court decides upon a rule of law, that decision should govern the same issues in subsequent stages of the litigation. *N. States Power Co. v. Metro. Utils. Dist.*, 258 Minn. 221, 224, 100 N.W.2d 661, 664 (1960).

The district court’s initial dismissal of the LCA-Vision claim established that Appellant’s cause of action for medical malpractice regarding the July 2005 surgery was procedurally barred due to the lack of timely expert affidavits. This procedural ruling is binding on the parties in subsequent proceedings involving the same case. As argued, Appellant had a full opportunity to contest the dismissal of the LCA-Vision claim and failed to do so successfully. She cannot now circumvent that final judgment by asserting the same negligence claim against a different defendant. The district court’s application of the law of the case doctrine was therefore appropriate.

**D. The Substance of the Affidavits Is Irrelevant**

Appellant places significant weight on the district court’s dicta suggesting that the substance of her expert affidavits would have satisfied the statute had they been timely served. However, as argued by both parties, this observation does not cure the procedural defect. The jurisdictional nature of the 180-day deadline means that timeliness is a prerequisite to the court considering the substance of the testimony. *Sorenson*, 457 N.W.2d at 191. The arguments presented accurately state that the procedural default is fatal to the claim, regardless of the substantive quality of the expert testimony eventually proffered.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, and the unanimous agreement of the parties, the Court finds that the district court did not err in granting summary judgment in favor of Dr. Whiting. The mandatory requirements of Minn. Stat. § 145.682 were not met, and the law of the case doctrine precludes Appellant from relitigating the procedural deficiencies of her medical malpractice claim against a different defendant. The arguments in favor of affirmance are consistent with statutory mandates and controlling precedent.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED** that the district court’s grant of summary judgment is affirmed.
