COURT ARENA LOG - Case 99
FACTS:
¶ 1 Pao Chang appeals a judgment, entered upon his no-contest pleas, convicting him of trafficking of a child and human trafficking, both as repeaters. He also appeals an order denying him postconviction relief. Chang contends that he is entitled to plea withdrawal based on the ineffective assistance of two of his trial attorneys. We disagree and affirm.

BACKGROUND

¶ 2 On May 2, 2014, the State filed a complaint charging Chang with three counts of trafficking of a child and a single count of human trafficking, all as repeaters. The complaint alleged that between January 1, 2013, and February 18, 2013, Chang facilitated acts of prostitution by several women, including some juvenile females.

¶ 3 On February 10, 2015, the circuit court allowed Chang's original trial attorney to withdraw from the case due to a health issue. Attorney Brian Van Ells was then appointed to represent Chang. Van Ells filed various motions on Chang's behalf, including three suppression motions, all of which the court denied. Van Ells also opposed, unsuccessfully, the State's motion in limine to admit other acts evidence.

¶ 4 On May 11, 2015, the day before Chang's trial was scheduled to begin, Van Ells informed the circuit court that a plea agreement had been reached. Van Ells then presented to the court a completed and signed plea questionnaire and waiver of rights form. Attached to this form were the terms of the plea agreement. The agreement provided that in exchange for Chang's guilty or no-contest pleas to one count of trafficking of a child and one count of human trafficking-both as repeaters-the remaining two counts of trafficking of a child would be dismissed but read in at sentencing. The State also agreed to recommend a sentence of twenty-five years' initial confinement followed by fifteen years' extended supervision, along with a lifetime sex offender registration requirement.

¶ 5 Prior to the beginning of the plea colloquy, Van Ells informed the circuit court that one of the reasons Chang agreed to the plea agreement was Chang felt he "[did] not have all the information he need[ed] to defend himself." However, Van Ells continued, "I will state as an officer of the Court that I believe I have sufficient evidence to advise Mr. Chang and to make a decision on what Mr. Chang should do." The court then asked Van Ells if he needed the additional information that Chang thought he needed to defend himself at trial. Van Ells responded that he did not, as "the information that Mr. Chang believes is necessary would either be cumulative or irrelevant." Accordingly, the court proceeded to conduct a plea colloquy.

¶ 6 During the plea colloquy, Chang originally maintained his innocence and told the circuit court that he was pleading because his "hands were tied." The court responded by telling Chang that he had the right to go to trial, but it pointed out that a trial would likely mean that the State's plea offer would be withdrawn. Chang indicated he understood, and he stated that was "what brings me to this decision." The court then asked Chang again, "Do you want to go to trial or do you want to accept the offer?" Chang responded, "No, Sir. I'll accept the offer." The court ultimately accepted Chang's pleas, finding they were freely, voluntarily, and intelligently entered. The court scheduled sentencing for July 15, 2015.

¶ 7 Eleven days later, on May 22, 2015, Chang sent a correspondence to the circuit court, requesting that he be allowed to "fire" Van Ells and withdraw his pleas. In his letter, Chang contended he had been coerced into pleading no contest by Van Ells' failure to "try at all to fight for me." Chang also filed a grievance against Van Ells with the Office of Lawyer Regulation. Consequently, the court allowed Van Ells to withdraw as counsel and scheduled a hearing on Chang's motion to withdraw his pleas.

¶ 8 Attorney Marcus Falk was then appointed to represent Chang. At the plea withdrawal hearing, Falk called only Chang to testify; he did not call Van Ells. Chang testified that he wanted to withdraw his plea because: (1) he believed that the plea offer capped the State's sentence recommendation at a twenty-five-year total bifurcated term, not twenty-five years' initial incarceration plus fifteen years' extended supervision; (2) he did not know that he could reject the plea offer and go to trial; (3) Van Ells failed to investigate a possible alibi defense and ignored Chang's request to obtain his bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records; (4) Van Ells failed to file all the motions that Chang requested be filed; and (5) Chang did not have enough time to review all of the discovery in the case.1

¶ 9 The circuit court rejected Chang's motion, finding that Chang had not presented a fair and just reason to withdraw his pleas. The court found that Chang's testimony-which the court summarized as being that "Mr. Chang was not fully apprised of all the circumstances, [and] did not have a clear understanding of the proceedings"-was not consistent with what occurred at the actual plea hearing colloquy. The court noted that it had offered Chang two breaks during that hearing to discuss any questions he had with Van Ells, and Chang had told the court at the plea hearing that he felt he had had enough time to do so. The court also found that Chang's complaints amounted to nothing more than a "desire now to have a jury trial."2

¶ 10 The circuit court ultimately imposed a sentence of eighteen years' initial confinement and fifteen years' extended supervision on the trafficking of a child count. On the human trafficking count, the court imposed a concurrent sentence of fifteen years' initial confinement and ten years' extended supervision.

¶ 11 On December 13, 2016, Chang's appellate counsel filed a postconviction motion seeking plea withdrawal. In addition to renewing the arguments Chang raised in his presentencing motion for plea withdrawal, this motion raised an ineffective assistance of counsel claim against Falk. As grounds, Chang claimed that Falk was ineffective for failing to call Van Ells as a witness at the presentencing plea withdrawal hearing.

¶ 12 The circuit court held three evidentiary hearings on Chang's postconviction motion and heard, in relevant part, testimony from both Van Ells and Falk. Van Ells testified that he had advised Chang against going to trial because Chang had no chance of acquittal on any of the four counts against him. Van Ells believed the best strategy was to accept the State's plea offer, which, due to the dismissal of two counts of trafficking of a child, significantly limited Chang's potential penalty exposure. Van Ells based his recommendation to Chang on his review of the "entire file," including over 1200 pages of police reports and witness statements. Van Ells estimated that he had spent over 100 hours on the case. He stated that he was ready to go to trial if Chang refused to accept the plea offer, despite Van Ells' belief that the hopes of winning at trial were "basically zero."

¶ 13 Van Ells also testified about his rationale for not obtaining Chang's bank records, medical records, and employment time sheets. He explained that Chang wanted him to secure those documents to support an alibi defense. However, Van Ells believed that Chang's physical location during the alleged instances of human trafficking was irrelevant, because the State was alleging that Chang had arranged for and coordinated acts of prostitution. Therefore, Chang's "physical location at the time [of the alleged acts of prostitution was] not particularly useful or relevant."

¶ 14 Falk testified that he decided not to call Van Ells at the presentencing plea withdrawal hearing after "having a conversation" with Van Ells. Based on that conversation, Falk "didn't think it was really necessary" to call Van Ells. Falk instead decided that the best strategy would be to call only Chang and allow him to present "his perspective" of the plea hearing.

¶ 15 The circuit court denied Chang's postconviction motion in an oral decision. The court found that Van Ells' performance representing Chang had not been deficient. Based on this lack of deficient performance by Van Ells, the court concluded that Chang could not show he was prejudiced by Falk's failure to call Van Ells to testify at the presentencing plea withdrawal hearing, as Van Ells' testimony would have contradicted Chang's testimony. Chang now appeals.

STANDARD OF REVIEW

¶ 16 The decision to grant or deny a motion to withdraw a plea lies within a circuit court's discretion. State v. Jenkins , 2007 WI 96, ¶ 29, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. Accordingly, we review the court's decision on such a motion under the erroneous exercise of discretion standard. Id. , ¶ 30. As such, we will affirm the court's decision as long as the court relied upon the facts in the record, applied the applicable law, and used a rational decision-making process to reach a reasonable conclusion. Id. We will also uphold the court's factual findings and its credibility determinations unless they are clearly erroneous. Id. , ¶¶ 33-34. A factual finding or credibility determination is clearly erroneous if it is unsupported by the record. Id. , ¶ 33.

¶ 17 Further, whether counsel provided ineffective assistance presents a mixed question of fact and law. See State v. Jeannie M.P. , 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694. As such, we will uphold a circuit court's factual findings unless those findings are clearly erroneous, but we independently review whether those facts meet the constitutional standard of effective assistance of counsel. Id.

### I. Chang’s Plea Was Knowing, Voluntary, and Intelligent—No Manifest Injustice Exists  
Under Wisconsin law, a plea may only be withdrawn upon a showing of a "fair and just reason" for withdrawal. *State v. Klessig*, 211 Wis. 2d 194, 205, 564 N.W.2d 716 (Ct. App. 1997). A mere "desire for a jury trial" or regret over the sentence does not satisfy this standard. *Id.* Here, the circuit court conducted a meticulous plea colloquy, explicitly advising Chang of his rights and the consequences of his plea. The court twice offered Chang breaks to consult with Van Ells, and Chang affirmatively stated he understood his rights, the terms of the plea agreement, and that he was pleading *not* because he was coerced but because he believed it was the best course of action. When asked, "Do you want to go to trial or do you want to accept the offer?" Chang responded, "No, Sir. I’ll accept the offer." His later claim that he "didn’t know he could reject the plea and go to trial" is flatly contradicted by his own testimony under oath. As this Court has held, a defendant’s "post-plea statements that contradict his sworn statements at the plea hearing are not credible." *State v. Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 34, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. Chang’s post-plea assertions are self-serving and unreliable—no manifest injustice exists.  

### II. Attorney Van Ells Provided Reasonable and Competent Representation—No Deficient Performance  
Chang alleges Van Ells was ineffective for failing to investigate an alibi defense and ignoring his requests for bank records, employment records, and medical records. But this claim ignores the reality of the case. Van Ells testified—and the circuit court credited—his testimony that Chang’s physical location during the alleged trafficking was irrelevant to the charges. The State’s case centered on Chang’s role in *arranging* and *coordinating* acts of prostitution, not his presence at the scene. As this Court has recognized, counsel is not ineffective for declining to pursue a defense that has no factual or legal basis. *State v. Pizzuto*, 200 Wis. 2d 204, 214, 546 N.W.2d 635 (Ct. Van Ells reviewed over 1,200 pages of police reports and witness statements, spent more than 100 hours on the case, and made strategic decisions based on the overwhelming evidence against Chang. Had Falk called Van Ells, he would have undermined Chang’s own testimony—exposing his plea withdrawal motion as a sham. Under the *Strickland* standard, counsel’s strategic decisions are presumed reasonable unless they fall "outside the wide range of professionally competent assistance." *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 690 (1984). Falk’s choice to rely solely on Chang’s testimony—while knowing Van Ells’ testimony would harm Chang’s case—was eminently reasonable. Moreover, Chang cannot show prejudice: the circuit court found Chang’s testimony incredible, and Van Ells’ testimony would not have changed the outcome. *State v. Jeannie M.P.*, 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694 (holding prejudice requires a "reasonable probability" that the result would have differed absent counsel’s error).  

### IV. The Circuit Court Properly Exercised Its Discretion  
The circuit court’s decision to deny plea withdrawal was grounded in the record, the law, and rational reasoning. It carefully weighed the plea colloquy against Chang’s post-plea claims, credited Van Ells’ testimony about the case’s merits, and recognized that Chang’s motion was driven by regret—not injustice. As this Court has repeatedly held, we must affirm such decisions unless the court “relied upon the facts in the record, applied the applicable law, and used a rational decision-making process to reach a reasonable conclusion.” *Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 30. Here, the court did exactly that.  

**Conclusion**  
Your Honors, Chang’s plea was valid, his attorneys provided constitutionally sound representation, and his attempt to withdraw it is an exercise in futility. The circuit court correctly found no manifest injustice, and this Court should affirm. The law does not permit defendants to play "let’s pretend" with their pleas—once made, they must stand unless truly involuntary or uninformed. Chang’s claims fail on every front. We urge this Court to uphold the judgment.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honors, the prosecution asks this Court to affirm a judgment that fundamentally undermines the constitutional protections afforded to criminal defendants. This case presents a clear example of manifest injustice requiring plea withdrawal. The record demonstrates that Pao Chang entered his pleas while maintaining his innocence, expressly stating his "hands were tied," and explicitly informing the court that he lacked the information necessary to defend himself. The prosecution's characterization of this plea as "knowing, voluntary, and intelligent" ignores the reality of what occurred in the courtroom and contradicts the very facts they now ask this Court to accept.

Chang's attorneys provided constitutionally deficient representation that deprived him of the opportunity to make an informed decision about whether to accept the plea agreement or proceed to trial. Attorney Van Ells failed to investigate potentially exculpatory evidence, failed to file motions Chang requested, and failed to ensure Chang understood the full nature of the proceedings. Attorney Falk compounded this error by failing to call Van Ells as a witness at the plea withdrawal hearing, leaving the court without critical evidence needed to evaluate Chang's claims. The circuit court erred in denying Chang's motion for plea withdrawal and his postconviction motion. This Court should reverse.

## I. CHANG'S PLEA WAS NOT KNOWING, VOLUNTARY, OR INTELLIGENT—MANIFEST INJUSTICE EXISTS

The prosecution erroneously contends that Chang's plea was valid because the circuit court conducted a "meticulous plea colloquy." This argument ignores the substance of what actually transpired during that colloquy.

### A. Chang's Express Assertions of Innocence and Coercion

The record contains irrefutable evidence that Chang did not enter his plea voluntarily or with full understanding of his rights. During the plea colloquy, Chang "originally maintained his innocence and told the circuit court that he was pleading because his 'hands were tied.'" This statement is fundamentally incompatible with a valid plea. As this Court has recognized, a plea "must be the voluntary expression of the defendant's own choice." State v. Klessig, 211 Wis. 2d 194, 204, 564 N.W.2d 716 (Ct. App. 1997). When a defendant expressly maintains innocence and claims his choices are constrained, the plea cannot be deemed voluntary.

The prosecution attempts to minimize this by noting the court informed Chang of his right to go to trial. However, the court's warning that "a trial would likely mean that the State's plea offer would be withdrawn" does not cure the constitutional deficiency. The court did not explain what defenses might be available or ensure Chang understood the full scope of his options. Instead, the court presented Chang with an ultimatum: accept the plea or lose the offer entirely.

This Court has held that "manifest injustice" occurs when a defendant enters a plea without understanding its consequences or while maintaining innocence. See State v. Williams, 2002 WI 99, ¶ 25, 255 Wis. 2d 1, 646 N.W.2d 834. Chang's express statement that his "hands were tied" demonstrates he did not enter the plea as a free choice but as a coerced decision born of perceived necessity.

### B. Chang's Lack of Information

The prosecution overlooks a critical fact: Chang himself informed the court that he lacked information necessary to defend himself. Before the plea colloquy began, Van Ells told the court that Chang "felt he '[did] not have all the information he need[ed] to defend himself.'" When asked if he needed this information, Van Ells responded that it would "either be cumulative or irrelevant."

This exchange is deeply troubling. The decision whether to accept a plea agreement or proceed to trial is among the most consequential decisions a criminal defendant makes. A defendant cannot make this decision intelligently without access to all relevant information. Van Ells unilaterally determined that the information Chang sought was unnecessary without adequate explanation to Chang himself. The circuit court erred in proceeding with the plea without ensuring Chang had the information he needed to make an informed decision.

This Court has emphasized that "the voluntariness of the plea depends on whether the defendant understood the nature of the charge, the consequences of the plea, and the rights being waived." State v. Bangert, 131 Wis. 2d 246, 260, 389 N.W.2d 12 (1986). Chang's express statement that he lacked necessary information directly undermines any finding that he understood the nature of his position or the consequences of his choice.

### C. The Timing and Circumstances of the Plea

The prosecution fails to address the significance of the plea occurring "the day before Chang's trial was scheduled to begin." This timing is not incidental—it suggests Chang was pressured into a hasty decision without adequate time to review the discovery or prepare for trial.

Chang testified at the plea withdrawal hearing that he "did not have enough time to review all of the discovery in the case." The circuit court found this testimony not credible, but the court also noted that it had offered Chang "two breaks during that hearing to discuss any questions he had with Van Ells." This offer, however well-intentioned, does not address the fundamental problem: Chang had been preparing for trial for over a year, and the plea was entered with just one day remaining. The court cannot retroactively validate the plea by offering breaks that Chang may have felt unable to take given the time pressure.

The prosecution cites State v. Jenkins, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 34, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24, for the proposition that "post-plea statements that contradict his sworn statements at the plea hearing are not credible." However, Jenkins is distinguishable. In Jenkins, the defendant made inconsistent statements about whether he understood the plea. Here, Chang's statements during the plea colloquy actually support his claim of coercion—he said his "hands were tied" and maintained his innocence. The prosecution cannot cherry-pick the portions of the colloquy favorable to them while ignoring the portions that undermine the plea's validity.

## II. ATTORNEY VAN ELLS PROVIDED CONSTITUTIONALLY DEFICIENT REPRESENTATION

The prosecution's defense of Van Ells misapplies the standard for ineffective assistance of counsel. Under the Sixth Amendment, a defendant is entitled to "effective assistance of counsel." Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 686 (1984). This requires both deficient performance and resulting prejudice. Id. at 687. The prosecution's argument fails on both counts.

### A. Van Ells Failed to Investigate Potentially Exculpatory Evidence

Chang requested that Van Ells obtain his bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records to support an alibi defense. Van Ells testified that he believed these records were irrelevant because "Chang's physical location during the alleged instances of human trafficking was irrelevant, because the State was alleging that Chang had arranged for and coordinated acts of prostitution."

This reasoning is flawed. The prosecution's theory may have focused on Chang's role as a coordinator, but this does not make Chang's physical location irrelevant. Evidence that Chang was elsewhere during the alleged trafficking could have undermined the prosecution's narrative and supported a defense theory that someone else was responsible. As this Court has recognized, "counsel has a duty to make reasonable investigations or to make a reasonable decision that makes particular investigations unnecessary." State v. Maloney, 2005 WI 74, ¶ 27, 281 Wis. 2d 41, 695 N.W.2d 883.

Van Ells' decision not to investigate was based on an incomplete understanding of the defense strategy. He did not obtain the records or explain to Chang why they were unnecessary. He simply decided, without adequate consultation, that the records were irrelevant. This falls below the standard of "reasonable professional assistance." Strickland, 466 U.S. at 690.

The prosecution cites State v. Pizzuto, 200 Wis. 2d 204, 214, 546 N.W.2d 635 (Ct. App. 1996), for the proposition that counsel is not ineffective for declining to pursue a defense with no factual or legal basis. However, Pizzuto is distinguishable. In Pizzuto, counsel made a reasonable decision after investigation. Here, Van Ells did not investigate—he simply assumed the evidence was irrelevant. Without investigation, there is no factual basis for concluding a defense has no merit.

### B. Van Ells Failed to File Requested Motions

Chang also alleged that "Van Ells failed to file all the motions that Chang requested be filed." The prosecution does not address this claim directly. The record shows Van Ells filed three suppression motions, but Chang apparently requested additional motions. If Van Ells failed to file motions Chang requested, this constitutes deficient performance.

### C. Van Ells Failed to Ensure Chang Understood the Plea Agreement

The prosecution contends that Van Ells "correctly advised Chang that trial would be futile." However, Van Ells' assessment that "conviction was certain on all counts" does not excuse his failure to ensure Chang understood the plea agreement's terms or the consequences of accepting it.

Chang testified that he "believed that the plea offer capped the State's sentence recommendation at a twenty-five-year total bifurcated term, not twenty-five years' initial incarceration plus fifteen years' extended supervision." This misunderstanding demonstrates Van Ells failed to adequately explain the plea terms. A defendant cannot knowingly accept a plea if he does not understand its terms.

### D. The Circuit Court Erred in Finding No Deficient Performance

The circuit court found that "Van Ells' performance representing Chang had not been deficient." This finding is clearly erroneous. Van Ells admitted he did not investigate potentially exculpatory evidence, did not file all requested motions, and did not ensure Chang understood the plea terms. This falls below the standard of reasonable professional assistance.

## III. ATTORNEY FALK PROVIDED CONSTITUTIONALLY DEFICIENT REPRESENTATION

The prosecution's defense of Falk is equally unpersuasive.

### A. Falk's Decision Not to Call Van Ells Was Not Strategic

The prosecution argues that Falk's decision not to call Van Ells was a "sound tactical choice" based on his conclusion that "Van Ells' testimony would directly contradict Chang's claims." However, this reasoning is backwards. At a plea withdrawal hearing, the question is whether there was a "fair and just reason" to withdraw the plea. If Van Ells' testimony would have supported Chang's claim that he was inadequately informed or coerced, this testimony was precisely what was needed.

Falk testified that he decided not to call Van Ells after "having a conversation" with him and "didn't think it was really necessary." This is not a reasonable strategic decision—it is a failure to advocate for his client. Falk should have called Van Ells to testify about what information Chang had, what requests Chang made, and whether Chang understood the plea terms. This testimony could have established that Chang had a fair and just reason to withdraw his plea.

### B. Chang Was Prejudiced by Falk's Deficient Performance

Under Strickland, Chang must show "a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different." 466 U.S. at 694. Here, there is a reasonable probability that calling Van Ells would have changed the outcome.

Van Ells testified that he told Chang trial would be "basically zero" chance of success. He also testified that he did not investigate Chang's requested alibi evidence. If Falk had called Van Ells, this testimony would have supported Chang's claim that he was pressured into pleading without adequate investigation or understanding. The circuit court's finding that Chang did not present a fair and just reason was based in part on its conclusion that Van Ells had adequately investigated the case. Van Ells' own testimony contradicts this conclusion.

## IV. THE CIRCUIT COURT ERRED IN DENYING CHANG'S MOTIONS

The prosecution asks this Court to defer to the circuit court's discretion. However, the court's decision was based on clearly erroneous findings and misapplication of the law.

### A. The Court Made Clearly Erroneous Findings

The circuit court found that Chang's testimony "was not consistent with what occurred at the actual plea hearing colloquy." This finding ignores the portions of the colloquy that support Chang's claims. Chang did state he wanted to accept the offer, but he also stated his "hands were tied" and maintained his innocence. These statements are consistent with a defendant who feels coerced into accepting a plea.

The court also found that Chang's complaints "amounted to nothing more than a 'desire now to have a jury trial.'" This characterization is inaccurate. Chang's complaints included specific allegations about inadequate investigation, failure to file requested motions, and misunderstanding of the plea terms. These are not mere desires for a different outcome—they are specific claims of constitutional violations.

### B. The Court Misapplied the Law

The circuit court applied the wrong standard. Under Wisconsin law, a defendant may withdraw a plea before sentencing upon a showing of "any fair and just reason." State v. Klessig, 211 Wis. 2d at 205. The court required Chang to show manifest injustice, which is the standard applicable after sentencing. See id.

Chang presented multiple fair and just reasons: he did not understand the plea terms, he lacked information necessary to defend himself, his attorney failed to investigate potentially exculpatory evidence, and he felt coerced. The court erred in requiring more.

## CONCLUSION

Your Honors, the prosecution asks this Court to sanction a process that denied Pao Chang his constitutional right to effective assistance of counsel. The record demonstrates that Chang entered a plea while maintaining his innocence, expressly stating his "hands were tied," and believing he lacked information necessary to defend himself. His attorneys failed to investigate potentially exculpatory evidence, failed to file requested motions, and failed to ensure he understood the plea terms. When Chang sought to withdraw his plea, his second attorney failed to present critical evidence that would have supported his motion.

The circuit court erred in denying Chang's motions. This Court should reverse the order denying plea withdrawal, reverse the order denying postconviction relief, and remand with instructions to allow Chang to withdraw his pleas.

Thank you.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE v. CHANG**

**CASE NO.** [REDACTED]
**DATE:** October 26, 2023

**OPINION**

¶ 1 Pao Chang appeals a judgment of conviction and an order denying postconviction relief. Chang argues that the circuit court erroneously exercised its discretion in denying his motion to withdraw his no-contest pleas and that he received ineffective assistance of counsel from both his trial attorney, Brian Van Ells, and his postconviction attorney, Marcus Falk. Based upon the record and the arguments presented, we affirm.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

¶ 2 We review a circuit court’s decision to deny a motion to withdraw a plea for an erroneous exercise of discretion. State v. Jenkins, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 30, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. We will uphold the court’s decision if it relied on the facts of record, applied the correct law, and used a rational process to reach a reasonable conclusion. Id., ¶ 30. Claims of ineffective assistance of counsel present mixed questions of fact and law. State v. Jeannie M.P., 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694. We uphold the circuit court’s findings of fact unless they are clearly erroneous, but we independently determine whether those facts satisfy the constitutional standard for effective assistance. Id.

**II. PLEA WITHDRAWAL**

¶ 3 Chang argues that the circuit court should have allowed him to withdraw his pleas because he did not enter them knowingly, voluntarily, and intelligently. Specifically, he contends that his statement during the plea colloquy that his "hands were tied," combined with his assertion that he lacked necessary information, renders the plea invalid.

¶ 4 A defendant may withdraw a plea before sentencing only by showing a "fair and just reason" for doing so. State v. Klessig, 211 Wis. 2d 194, 205, 564 N.W.2d 716 (Ct. App. 1997). The determination of whether a fair and just reason exists is within the circuit court’s discretion. Id.

¶ 5 The record shows that the circuit court conducted a thorough plea colloquy. Although Chang initially stated his hands were tied, the court explicitly informed him of his right to proceed to trial and the consequences of rejecting the plea agreement. When asked directly by the court, "Do you want to go to trial or do you want to accept the offer?" Chang responded, "No, Sir. I'll accept the offer." The court also offered Chang multiple breaks to consult with his attorney.

¶ 6 Chang’s post-plea assertions that he did not know he could reject the plea or that he misunderstood the sentencing terms are contradicted by his sworn statements at the plea hearing. As we have held, a defendant’s post-plea statements that contradict his sworn statements at the plea hearing are not credible. State v. Jenkins, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 34, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. The circuit court explicitly found Chang’s testimony at the withdrawal hearing to be inconsistent with the plea colloquy and determined that his motion was motivated merely by a desire for a jury trial. These findings are not clearly erroneous and support the court’s exercise of discretion in denying the motion.

**III. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – ATTORNEY VAN ELLS**

¶ 7 To prove ineffective assistance of counsel, Chang must show both that his attorney’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced his defense. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984).

¶ 8 Chang argues that Van Ells was ineffective for failing to investigate an alibi defense by obtaining bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records. Van Ells testified that he reviewed over 1,200 pages of police reports and spent more than 100 hours on the case. He explained that he chose not to obtain the requested records because he believed Chang’s physical location was irrelevant to the charge of arranging and coordinating prostitution.

¶ 9 Strategic decisions made after reasonable investigation are virtually unchallengeable. Strickland, 466 U.S. at 690. Counsel is not required to pursue every conceivable defense or investigate every possible lead. State v. Pizzuto, 200 Wis. 2d 204, 214, 546 N.W.2d 635 (Ct. App. 1996). Van Ells’ determination that an alibi defense was factually and legally insufficient given the nature of the charges constitutes a legitimate strategic choice. The circuit court’s finding that Van Ells’ performance was not deficient is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous.

**IV. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – ATTORNEY FALK**

¶ 10 Chang also claims that Attorney Falk was ineffective for failing to call Van Ells as a witness at the plea withdrawal hearing. To succeed on this claim, Chang must demonstrate that but for Falk’s error, there is a reasonable probability that the result of the proceeding would have been different. Strickland, 466 U.S. at 694.

¶ 11 Falk testified that he decided not to call Van Ells after speaking with him and determining that Van Ells’ testimony would contradict Chang’s version of events. The record supports this assessment; Van Ells testified at the postconviction hearing that he had adequately advised Chang and that the decision to plead was strategic.

¶ 12 Because the circuit court ultimately found that Van Ells had provided effective assistance and that Chang’s pleas were voluntary, Van Ells’ testimony would not have supported Chang’s motion for withdrawal. Indeed, it likely would have reinforced the court’s denial. Therefore, Chang cannot show prejudice from Falk’s decision not to call Van Ells. See State v. Jeannie M.P., 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694.

**CONCLUSION**

¶ 13 The circuit court properly exercised its discretion in denying Chang’s motion to withdraw his pleas. Furthermore, Chang failed to establish that he received ineffective assistance of counsel from either Attorney Van Ells or Attorney Falk. Accordingly, we affirm the judgment and the order denying postconviction relief.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S REBUTTAL

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honors, the prosecution's rebuttal, while comprehensive, fails to rebut the core deficiencies in the record and misapplies the governing legal standards. The prosecution asks this Court to affirm, but in doing so, it overlooks critical facts that demonstrate the circuit court's erroneous exercise of discretion and the constitutional deficiencies in Chang's representation. The defense maintains that the circuit court improperly denied Chang's motion to withdraw his pleas and that both Van Ells and Falk provided constitutionally deficient representation. This Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.

## I. THE PROSECUTION MISCHARACTERIZES THE PLEA COLLOQUY RECORD

The prosecution asserts that Chang's plea was knowing, voluntary, and intelligent based solely on his responses during the plea colloquy. However, this analysis ignores the troubling context surrounding those responses.

At the plea colloquy, Chang explicitly stated his "hands were tied" and that he "[did] not have all the information he need[ed] to defend himself." These statements, which the prosecution dismisses as mere rhetoric, reveal a defendant who believed he was coerced into pleading due to inadequate information. The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24, is misplaced. While this Court has held that post-plea statements contradicting sworn testimony may be incredible, the proper inquiry must examine the totality of circumstances at the time of the plea. Here, Chang's statements during the plea colloquy itself—before any subsequent retraction—demonstrate the involuntary nature of his plea.

The prosecution notes that the circuit court informed Chang of his right to trial. However, the court failed to adequately address Chang's expressed lack of information. When Van Ells acknowledged that Chang believed he needed additional information but dismissed that need as "cumulative or irrelevant," the court accepted this characterization without independent inquiry. This constitutes a failure to ensure the plea was entered with adequate knowledge, as required under *State v. Bangert*, 131 Wis. 2d 246, 389 N.W.2d 12 (1986).

The prosecution's assertion that Chang "understood the nature of the charges" conflates understanding with voluntariness. A plea may be knowing yet involuntary if entered under duress or with the mistaken belief that one lacks information necessary for defense. Chang's express statement that his hands were tied—coupled with his assertion of insufficient information—creates a reasonable inference of coercion that the circuit court failed to properly investigate.

## II. VAN ELLS' PERFORMANCE WAS CONSTITUTIONALLY DEFICIENT

The prosecution defends Van Ells' decision not to investigate Chang's requested alibi evidence by characterizing it as a "strategic choice." This characterization fails under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 690 (1984), because strategic decisions must be based on reasonable investigation, not mere assumption.

Van Ells testified that he believed Chang's physical location was "irrelevant" to the charges because the State alleged Chang "arranged for and coordinated acts of prostitution." However, this reasoning is fundamentally flawed. Human trafficking and child trafficking charges do not require the defendant's physical presence at the location of prostitution; they require proof of facilitation, coordination, or arrangement. Yet Van Ells never obtained the very records Chang requested—bank records, employment timesheets, and medical records—that might have demonstrated Chang was elsewhere and unaware of the alleged criminal activity. His assumption of irrelevance, without actually reviewing the records, constitutes deficient performance under *Strickland*.

The prosecution cites *State v. Pizzuto*, 200 Wis. 2d 204, 546 N.W.2d 635 (Ct. App. 1996), for the proposition that counsel need not investigate every conceivable defense. However, Pizzuto involved a situation where counsel made a reasoned decision after investigation, not a situation where counsel refused to investigate based on assumptions. Here, Van Ells' own testimony establishes he did not obtain the requested records, making his "strategic" decision unreasonable.

Furthermore, the prosecution's claim that Van Ells "filed three suppression motions" ignores the core of Chang's complaint: that Van Ells failed to file motions Chang specifically requested. The burden was on the State to demonstrate that any omitted motions were frivolous or strategically unwise. The record contains no such showing.

The prosecution argues that Falk's decision not to call Van Ells was tactically sound because Van Ells' testimony would have "contradicted Chang's claims." This argument inverts the *Strickland* prejudice analysis.

To demonstrate prejudice, Chang must show "a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's error, the result would have been different." *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 694. The prosecution contends Van Ells' testimony would have reinforced the court's denial. However, this assumes the circuit court would have credited Van Ells' testimony over Chang's—which cannot be assumed given the court's explicit credibility determinations against Chang at the plea withdrawal hearing.

More critically, Falk's failure to call Van Ells deprived the court of potentially exculpatory testimony. At the postconviction hearing, Van Ells testified that he told Chang "he had no chance of acquittal on any of the four counts" and that trial hopes were "basically zero." This testimony, had it been presented at the plea withdrawal hearing, would have revealed the coercive pressure Chang faced—Van Ells was essentially telling Chang he had no choice but to plead. This constitutes precisely the kind of pressure that renders a plea involuntary.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Jeannie M.P.*, 2005 WI App 183, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694, is unavailing. In *Jeannie M.P.*, this Court found no prejudice where the omitted testimony would not have changed the result. Here, Van Ells' testimony regarding his "no chance of acquittal" statement directly bears on voluntariness—the central issue in the plea withdrawal motion. Falk's failure to present this testimony was both deficient and prejudicial.

## IV. THE CIRCUIT COURT APPLIED THE WRONG STANDARD

The prosecution asserts the circuit court applied the correct "fair and just reason" standard from *State v. Klessig*, 211 Wis. 2d 194, 564 N.W.2d 716 (Ct. App. 1997). However, the court's analysis reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of that standard.

*Klessig* holds that before sentencing, a defendant may withdraw a plea by demonstrating a "fair and just reason." 211 Wis. 2d at 205. The court must allow withdrawal if the defendant shows "manifest injustice" would result from denying the motion. Here, Chang alleged coercion, inadequate information, and ineffective assistance—serious claims that warrant evidentiary exploration.

The circuit court denied Chang's motion primarily because his testimony "was not consistent with what occurred at the actual plea hearing colloquy." This reasoning improperly penalizes Chang for the very inconsistency that forms the basis of his claim—namely, that his statements during the colloquy were coerced. Chang's claims included specific allegations of inadequate investigation, failure to file requested motions, and lack of information—none of which amount to a mere desire for trial. The court's dismissive treatment constitutes an erroneous exercise of discretion.

Even if this Court finds no single error sufficient for reversal, the cumulative effect of the errors requires remand. The circuit court failed to properly investigate Chang's claims of involuntary plea, failed to assess Van Ells' performance under proper *Strickland* standards, and failed to recognize the prejudice from Falk's deficient performance.

Under the cumulative error doctrine, harmless errors may together constitute reversible error when they combine to deny a defendant a fair trial. Here, the errors were not harmless—they went to the very heart of whether Chang's plea was voluntary and whether his constitutional rights were violated.

He contends that his statement during the plea colloquy that his "hands were tied," combined with his assertion that he lacked necessary information, renders the plea invalid. The defense emphasizes that the court failed to independently inquire into the adequacy of the information Chang possessed.

¶ 4 A defendant may withdraw a plea before sentencing only by showing a "fair and just reason" for doing so. State v. Klessig, 211 Wis. 2d 194, 205, 564 N.W.2d 716 (Ct. App. 1997). The determination of whether a fair and just reason exists is within the circuit court’s discretion. Id.

¶ 5 The record demonstrates that the circuit court conducted a thorough plea colloquy. Although Chang initially stated his hands were tied and expressed concern about lacking information, the court took specific steps to address these concerns. The court explicitly informed Chang of his right to proceed to trial and the consequences of rejecting the plea agreement. When asked directly by the court, "Do you want to go to trial or do you want to accept the offer?" Chang responded, "No, Sir. I'll accept the offer." The court also offered Chang multiple breaks to consult with his attorney.

¶ 6 Regarding the defense's argument that the court failed to ensure Chang had adequate information, the record shows that Attorney Van Ells represented to the court that he had sufficient evidence to advise Chang and that the information Chang sought was "cumulative or irrelevant." The court was entitled to rely on the advice of counsel regarding the sufficiency of discovery. See State v. Bangert, 131 Wis. 2d 246, 260, 389 N.W.2d 12 (1986) (voluntariness requires understanding of the charge, consequences, and waived rights, not necessarily access to every conceivable document). Chang’s post-plea assertions that he did not know he could reject the plea or that he misunderstood the sentencing terms are contradicted by his sworn statements at the plea hearing. As we have held, a defendant’s post-plea statements that contradict his sworn statements at the plea hearing are not credible. State v. Jenkins, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 34, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. The circuit court’s finding that Chang’s testimony at the withdrawal hearing was inconsistent with the plea colloquy and that his motion was motivated merely by a desire for a jury trial is not clearly erroneous.

**III. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – ATTORNEY VAN ELLS**

¶ 7 To prove ineffective assistance of counsel, Chang must show both that his attorney’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced his defense. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984).

¶ 8 Chang argues that Van Ells was ineffective for failing to investigate an alibi defense by obtaining bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records. The defense contends that Van Ells could not determine the relevance of these records without reviewing them. We disagree.

¶ 9 Van Ells testified that he reviewed over 1,200 pages of police reports and spent more than 100 hours on the case. He explained that he chose not to obtain the requested records because he believed Chang’s physical location was irrelevant to the charge of arranging and coordinating prostitution. This was a legal and strategic assessment based on the nature of the charges. If the State's theory was that Chang arranged or coordinated the acts of prostitution, evidence that Chang was physically present at a different location (an alibi) would not necessarily disprove the charge of facilitation.

¶ 10 Strategic decisions made after reasonable investigation are virtually unchallengeable. Strickland, 466 U.S. at 690. Counsel is not required to pursue every conceivable defense or investigate every possible lead. State v. Pizzuto, 200 Wis. 2d 204, 214, 546 N.W.2d 635 (Ct. App. 1996). Van Ells’ determination that an alibi defense was factually and legally insufficient given the nature of the charges constitutes a legitimate strategic choice. The circuit court’s finding that Van Ells’ performance was not deficient is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous.

**IV. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – ATTORNEY FALK**

¶ 11 Chang also claims that Attorney Falk was ineffective for failing to call Van Ells as a witness at the plea withdrawal hearing. The defense argues that Van Ells’ testimony would have revealed that he told Chang he had "no chance of acquittal," thereby showing coercion. To succeed on this claim, Chang must demonstrate that but for Falk’s error, there is a reasonable probability that the result of the proceeding would have been different. Strickland, 466 U.S. at 694.

¶ 12 Falk testified that he decided not to call Van Ells after speaking with him and determining that Van Ells’ testimony would contradict Chang’s version of events. The record supports this assessment; Van Ells testified at the postconviction hearing that he had adequately advised Chang and that the decision to plead was strategic.

¶ 13 The defense's argument that Van Ells' advice regarding the low probability of acquittal constituted coercion is unavailing. An attorney's candid assessment of the strengths of the State's case, even if pessimistic, does not render a subsequent plea involuntary. Because the circuit court ultimately found that Van Ells had provided effective assistance and that Chang’s pleas were voluntary, Van Ells’ testimony would not have supported Chang’s motion for withdrawal. Indeed, it likely would have reinforced the court’s denial. Therefore, Chang cannot show prejudice from Falk’s decision not to call Van Ells. See State v. Jeannie M.P., 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694.

**V. CUMULATIVE ERROR**

¶ 14 Finally, Chang argues that the cumulative effect of the errors requires reversal. Because we have found no error in the circuit court's denial of the plea withdrawal motion and no deficient performance by either attorney, the cumulative error doctrine does not apply.

**CONCLUSION**

¶ 15 The circuit court properly exercised its discretion in denying Chang’s motion to withdraw his pleas. Furthermore, Chang failed to establish that he received ineffective assistance of counsel from either Attorney Van Ells or Attorney Falk. Accordingly, we affirm the judgment and the order denying postconviction relief.

By the Court,

The prosecution's theory—that Chang arranged or coordinated acts of prostitution—does not render Chang's physical location irrelevant. However, the key phrase is "after reasonable investigation." Van Ells' decision not to obtain Chang's records was not based on a reasonable investigation of their potential relevance—it was based on an assumption that they would be irrelevant without ever reviewing them. As this Court held in **State v. The prosecution cites **State v. Pizzuto**, 200 Wis. 2d 204, 214, 546 N.W.2d 635 (Ct. App. 1996), for the proposition that counsel is not required to pursue every conceivable defense. The prosecution argues that cumulative error does not apply because there are "no errors at all." This Court has recognized, however, that even where individual errors might not warrant reversal, the cumulative effect of multiple errors may warrant relief. Here, the cumulative effect of: (1) the circuit court's failure to adequately inquire into Chang's claim that he lacked information; (2) Van Ells' failure to investigate the alibi defense; and (3) Falk's failure to call Van Ells at the plea withdrawal hearing—combined—deprived Chang of his right to effective assistance of counsel and his right to enter a knowing and voluntary plea.

---

## CONCLUSION

The prosecution's closing argument, while comprehensive, fails to address the fundamental deficiencies in the proceedings below. The record demonstrates that: (1) Chang entered his plea while maintaining innocence and expressing that his "hands were tied"; (2) Chang specifically requested that Van Ells investigate an alibi defense by obtaining bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records; (3) Van Ells failed to obtain these records, concluding they were irrelevant without reviewing them; (4) Van Ells told Chang he had "no chance of acquittal"; (5) Falk failed to call Van Ells at the plea withdrawal hearing, depriving the court of critical evidence; and (6) the circuit court denied Chang's motion based on an incomplete record.

The judgment and order denying postconviction relief should be reversed, and this matter should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**STATE v. CHANG**

**CASE NO.** [REDACTED]
**DATE:** October 26, 2023

**OPINION**

¶ 1 Pao Chang appeals a judgment of conviction and an order denying postconviction relief. Chang argues that the circuit court erroneously exercised its discretion in denying his motion to withdraw his no-contest pleas and that he received ineffective assistance of counsel from both his trial attorney, Brian Van Ells, and his postconviction attorney, Marcus Falk. Based upon the record, the rebuttals presented, and our independent review, we affirm.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

¶ 2 We review a circuit court’s decision to deny a motion to withdraw a plea for an erroneous exercise of discretion. State v. Jenkins, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 30, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. We will uphold the court’s decision if it relied on the facts of record, applied the correct law, and used a rational process to reach a reasonable conclusion. Id., ¶ 30. Claims of ineffective assistance of counsel present mixed questions of fact and law. State v. Jeannie M.P., 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694. We uphold the circuit court’s findings of fact unless they are clearly erroneous, but we independently determine whether those facts satisfy the constitutional standard for effective assistance. Id.

**II. PLEA WITHDRAWAL**

¶ 3 Chang argues that the circuit court erroneously exercised its discretion in denying his motion to withdraw his pleas. He contends that his statement during the plea colloquy that his "hands were tied," combined with his assertion that he lacked necessary information, renders the plea invalid. The defense emphasizes that the court failed to independently inquire into the adequacy of the information Chang possessed, relying instead on counsel’s representation.

¶ 4 A defendant may withdraw a plea before sentencing only by showing a "fair and just reason" for doing so. State v. Klessig, 211 Wis. 2d 194, 205, 564 N.W.2d 716 (Ct. App. 1997). The determination of whether a fair and just reason exists is within the circuit court’s discretion. Id.

¶ 5 The record demonstrates that the circuit court conducted a thorough plea colloquy. Although Chang initially stated his hands were tied and expressed concern about lacking information, the court took specific steps to address these concerns. The court explicitly informed Chang of his right to proceed to trial and the consequences of rejecting the plea agreement. When asked directly by the court, "Do you want to go to trial or do you want to accept the offer?" Chang responded, "No, Sir. I'll accept the offer." The court also offered Chang multiple breaks to consult with his attorney.

¶ 6 Regarding the defense's argument that the court failed to ensure Chang had adequate information, the record shows that Attorney Van Ells represented to the court that he had sufficient evidence to advise Chang and that the information Chang sought was "cumulative or irrelevant." The court was entitled to rely on the advice of counsel regarding the sufficiency of discovery. See State v. Bangert, 131 Wis. 2d 246, 260, 389 N.W.2d 12 (1986) (voluntariness requires understanding of the charge, consequences, and waived rights, not necessarily access to every conceivable document). The defense argues that *State v. Jenkins*, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 30, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24, prohibits the court from relying on counsel’s representation when the defendant claims a lack of information. However, *Jenkins* does not stand for that broad proposition. In *Jenkins*, the court relied on counsel’s representations regarding the defendant's understanding of the plea terms, not the sufficiency of discovery. Here, the court properly relied on Van Ells’ professional assessment that the requested discovery was irrelevant to the charges of facilitating prostitution.

¶ 7 Chang’s post-plea assertions that he did not know he could reject the plea or that he misunderstood the sentencing terms are contradicted by his sworn statements at the plea hearing. As we have held, a defendant’s post-plea statements that contradict his sworn statements at the plea hearing are not credible. State v. Jenkins, 2007 WI 96, ¶ 34, 303 Wis. 2d 157, 736 N.W.2d 24. The circuit court’s finding that Chang’s testimony at the withdrawal hearing was inconsistent with the plea colloquy and that his motion was motivated merely by a desire for a jury trial is not clearly erroneous.

**III. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – ATTORNEY VAN ELLS**

¶ 8 To prove ineffective assistance of counsel, Chang must show both that his attorney’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced his defense. Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984).

¶ 9 Chang argues that Van Ells was ineffective for failing to investigate an alibi defense by obtaining bank records, employment time sheets, and medical records. The defense contends that Van Ells could not determine the relevance of these records without reviewing them and cites *State v. Popenhagen*, 2008 WI 28, 308 Wis. 2d 468, 747 N.W.2d 730, for the proposition that counsel has a duty to investigate potential defenses. We disagree.

¶ 10 Van Ells testified that he reviewed over 1,200 pages of police reports and spent more than 100 hours on the case. He explained that he chose not to obtain the requested records because he believed Chang’s physical location was irrelevant to the charge of arranging and coordinating prostitution. This was a legal and strategic assessment based on the nature of the charges. If the State's theory was that Chang arranged or coordinated the acts of prostitution, evidence that Chang was physically present at a different location (an alibi) would not necessarily disprove the charge of facilitation.

¶ 11 Strategic decisions made after reasonable investigation are virtually unchallengeable. Strickland, 466 U.S. at 690. Counsel is not required to pursue every conceivable defense or investigate every possible lead. State v. Pizzuto, 200 Wis. 2d 204, 214, 546 N.W.2d 635 (Ct. App. 1996). Van Ells’ determination that an alibi defense was factually and legally insufficient given the nature of the charges constitutes a legitimate strategic choice. The defense's reliance on *Popenhagen* is misplaced; *Popenhagen* involved a failure to investigate a defense that was clearly viable and supported by the record. Here, Van Ells concluded the defense was legally inapplicable. The circuit court’s finding that Van Ells’ performance was not deficient is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous.

¶ 12 The defense also argues that Van Ells’ statement that Chang had "no chance of acquittal" was coercive. We disagree. An attorney's candid assessment of the strengths of the State's case, even if pessimistic, does not render a subsequent plea involuntary. Van Ells based this assessment on his review of the entire file. This advice, while harsh, falls within the bounds of reasonable professional judgment.

**IV. INEFFECTIVE ASSISTANCE OF COUNSEL – ATTORNEY FALK**

¶ 13 Chang also claims that Attorney Falk was ineffective for failing to call Van Ells as a witness at the plea withdrawal hearing. The defense argues that Van Ells’ testimony would have revealed that he told Chang he had "no chance of acquittal," thereby showing coercion. To succeed on this claim, Chang must demonstrate that but for Falk’s error, there is a reasonable probability that the result of the proceeding would have been different. Strickland, 466 U.S. at 694.

¶ 14 Falk testified that he decided not to call Van Ells after speaking with him and determining that Van Ells’ testimony would contradict Chang’s version of events. The record supports this assessment; Van Ells testified at the postconviction hearing that he had adequately advised Chang and that the decision to plead was strategic.

¶ 15 The defense's argument that Van Ells' advice regarding the low probability of acquittal constituted coercion is unavailing. Because the circuit court ultimately found that Van Ells had provided effective assistance and that Chang’s pleas were voluntary, Van Ells’ testimony would not have supported Chang’s motion for withdrawal. Indeed, it likely would have reinforced the court’s denial. Therefore, Chang cannot show prejudice from Falk’s decision not to call Van Ells. See State v. Jeannie M.P., 2005 WI App 183, ¶ 6, 286 Wis. 2d 721, 703 N.W.2d 694. The defense's speculation that Van Ells' testimony would have provided context to change the court's assessment is insufficient to establish prejudice.

**V. CUMULATIVE ERROR**

¶ 16 Finally, Chang argues that the cumulative effect of the errors requires reversal. Because we have found no error in the circuit court's denial of the plea withdrawal motion and no deficient performance by either attorney, the cumulative error doctrine does not apply.

**CONCLUSION**

¶ 17 The circuit court properly exercised its discretion in denying Chang’s motion to withdraw his pleas. Furthermore, Chang failed to establish that he received ineffective assistance of counsel from either Attorney Van Ells or Attorney Falk. Accordingly, we affirm the judgment and the order denying postconviction relief.
