Your Honor, the evidence discovered in this case must be suppressed because the officer’s search of the vehicle was unconstitutional under the Fourth Amendment. The Fourth Amendment prohibits unreasonable searches and seizures, and a pat-down for weapons—known as a Terry frisk—requires specific, articulable facts establishing reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and dangerous. As the Supreme Court held in *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), an officer may conduct a limited pat-down only when "a reasonably prudent man in the circumstances would be warranted in the belief that his safety or that of others was in danger." This standard demands more than a "hunch" or subjective suspicion; it requires objective facts connecting the suspect to an immediate threat of violence.

Here, the officer testified repeatedly that he had no safety concerns whatsoever. He explicitly stated, "I had no safety concerns" (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4), and confirmed there were "no indicia of intoxication or being under the influence of drugs" (Suppression Tr. 31:5-11), "no burnt or fresh marijuana" (Suppression Tr. 38:14-17), and "no furtive or suspicious movements by defendant during the encounter" (Suppression Tr. 31:22-25, 33:16-19, 42:2-43:3). These facts are undisputed and demonstrate a complete absence of reasonable suspicion justifying a pat-down.

The Supreme Court reinforced this principle in *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323 (2009), where it held that during a traffic stop, an officer may conduct a pat-down only when "the officer has reasonable suspicion that the person detained may be armed and dangerous." In *Johnson*, the Court emphasized that the officer must "be able to point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant that intrusion." Yet here, the officer could point to no such facts. Defendant was "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative" throughout the encounter (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19), and the officer’s weapon was never drawn (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5). Because the pat-down lacked reasonable suspicion, it was an unconstitutional seizure under the Fourth Amendment. Any consent obtained thereafter is not voluntary but the product of coercion. # DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this opening response in opposition to the Prosecution's motion to suppress evidence. The Prosecution's argument rests on a fundamental mischaracterization of the facts and the legal framework governing this encounter. As set forth below, the search of the vehicle was lawful for multiple independent reasons: (1) the initial encounter was not a seizure requiring Fourth Amendment protections; (2) the vehicle search was supported by valid consent; (3) even if the pat-down was improper, the vehicle search was an independent source not tainted by any alleged illegality; and (4) the automobile exception applies regardless of any prior interaction. The Defense respectfully requests that this Court deny the Prosecution's motion to suppress.

## I. THE INITIAL ENCOUNTER WAS NOT A SEIZURE

The Prosecution erroneously characterizes this encounter as a "traffic stop." The facts demonstrate conclusively that the defendant was not seized within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment when he initially pulled his vehicle to the side of the road.

The officer testified unambiguously that "regardless of any observations of the motorist's driving, the officer did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, the car pulled over on its own" (Suppression Tr. 13, 26, 30, 46, 59-60). The defendant voluntarily pulled his vehicle to the shoulder of the road. The officer did not activate his lights until after the defendant had already stopped his vehicle. (Suppression Tr. 13.)

In *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121 (1983), the Supreme Court held that a temporary detention is not a seizure requiring Fourth Amendment protections when the individual is free to leave. Here, the defendant was not detained—he voluntarily stopped his own vehicle. The officer's activation of his lights after the defendant had already stopped does not transform a voluntary action into a seizure. See *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002) (finding no seizure where police approach passengers on bus and ask questions, absent physical restraint or show of authority).

The Prosecution's reliance on *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), is misplaced because *Terry* addresses seizures. There was no seizure here until after the defendant voluntarily stopped his vehicle and engaged with the officer.

## II. EVEN ASSUMING ARGUENDO THAT A SEIZURE OCCURRED, THE OFFICER HAD LAWFUL AUTHORITY TO DETAIN THE DEFENDANT

Even if this Court finds that a seizure occurred when the officer activated his lights and approached the vehicle, such a seizure was lawful because the officer had reasonable suspicion that the defendant had committed a traffic violation—driving while operating a vehicle with a suspended license.

The officer observed the defendant operating a vehicle at 3:42 AM, driving 35 miles per hour in a 45-mile-per-hour zone, making an unusual lane change pattern, and stopping his vehicle. While these observations alone might not establish a traffic violation, once the officer approached and requested the defendant's license and registration, he discovered that the defendant's driver's license was suspended. (Suppression Tr. 17:17-19.)

In *Pennsylvania v. Labron*, 518 U.S. 938 (1996), the Supreme Court held that an officer may lawfully detain a driver while investigating the status of the driver's license. The officer's discovery of a suspended license provided probable cause to believe that the defendant had committed the offense of driving while license suspended or revoked, which is a criminal offense in Pennsylvania. See 75 Pa. Cons. Stat. § 1543.

## III. THE VEHICLE SEARCH WAS SUPPORTED BY VALID CONSENT

The Prosecution's central argument—that the search of the vehicle was the fruit of an unlawful pat-down—fails because the search of the vehicle was supported by independent, valid consent that was not the product of any coercion.

### A. The Defendant Voluntarily Consented to the Search of His Vehicle

The facts establish that after being informed he was free to go, the defendant voluntarily engaged the officer in further conversation and explicitly consented to the search of his vehicle. The defendant "placed his hands on the side of the vehicle and states, I can search him." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.)

The officer testified that he "didn't obtain any sort of written consent" (Suppression Tr. 23:2-3), but he described the defendant's demeanor in giving consent as "agreeable." (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18.) Most significantly, the defendant was "fully compliant, polite, respectful, and cooperative" throughout the encounter. (Suppression Tr. 33:3-19.)

In *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973), the Supreme Court held that the voluntariness of consent must be determined from the totality of the circumstances, and that voluntary consent may be given even without warnings. The defendant here was informed he was free to go, was not under arrest, was not handcuffed, and the officer's weapon was never drawn. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5.) The defendant voluntarily approached the officer and offered to allow the search.

### B. The Prosecution Mischaracterizes the Sequence of Events

The Prosecution conflates the pat-down of the defendant's person with the search of the vehicle. These were separate events. The officer first asked if the defendant "had anything that would concern me," and the defendant responded by placing his hands on the vehicle and offering to be searched. (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.)

The vehicle search followed this interaction. The officer "searched first in the compartment of the vehicle, then moved into the trunk." (Suppression Tr. 24:10-14.) The prosecution has not established that the vehicle search was dependent upon the pat-down of the defendant's person.

### C. The Defendant Was Free to Leave Before the Consent

Critically, the facts demonstrate that before the officer asked for consent to search, the defendant was told he was "free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10.) The defendant had already contacted his girlfriend to pick him up because he could not drive with a suspended license. (Suppression Tr. 19:12-18.)

In *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194 (2002), the Supreme Court emphasized that the voluntariness of consent must be assessed in light of whether the person felt free to refuse. Here, the defendant explicitly knew he was free to leave. His continued engagement with the officer and his offer to consent were thus genuinely voluntary.

## IV. THE AUTOMBILE EXCEPTION PROVIDES AN INDEPENDENT BASIS FOR THE SEARCH

Even if this Court were to find that the pat-down was improper or that consent was somehow tainted, the search of the vehicle was independently justified under the automobile exception to the Fourth Amendment.

### A. Probable Cause Existed to Search the Vehicle

The officer had probable cause to believe the vehicle contained evidence of a crime. The officer testified that he formed "reasonable suspicion" to search the vehicle based on: (1) where the defendant was coming from; (2) the defendant's re-engagement about the I.D. procedures; and (3) the defendant's "overly cooperative" behavior in placing his hands on the vehicle. (Suppression Tr. 56:21-57:9.)

In *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925), the Supreme Court established the automobile exception, holding that probable cause to believe that a vehicle contains evidence of a crime is sufficient to justify a warrantless search. The officer's observations—combined with the defendant's presence in a vehicle at 3:42 AM, his claimed confusion about his location despite having been to the area before, and his unusual behavior—provided probable cause to search the vehicle.

### B. The Search Was Not a Pretext for a Fishing Expedition

The Prosecution argues that the officer lacked probable cause. However, the standard is not certainty—it is probable cause, which exists when "there is a fair probability that evidence of a crime will be found in a particular place." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983).

The officer's experience of approximately fifteen years in law enforcement, his knowledge of the area's drug trafficking patterns, and the defendant's inconsistent explanations provided a sufficient basis for probable cause. See *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798 (1982) (holding that a warrantless search of a vehicle is permissible when there is probable cause to believe the vehicle contains evidence of a crime).

## V. THE FRUIT OF THE POISONOUS TREE DOCTRINE DOES NOT APPLY

The Prosecution's reliance on *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963), is misplaced because the vehicle search was an independent source not tainted by any alleged illegality.

### A. The Pat-Down and Vehicle Search Were Independent Events

The officer testified that he asked the defendant if he "could ask him a question" (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25), and the defendant agreed. The officer then made a statement about "problems with people bringing things out of Philadelphia" and asked if defendant "had anything that would concern me." (Suppression Tr. 22:25.)

At that point, the defendant "without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2.) The vehicle search followed this voluntary offer.

Even assuming the pat-down was improper, the vehicle search was not a direct continuation of the pat-down—it was a separate interaction that occurred after the defendant was told he was free to go. The defendant voluntarily offered consent to search his vehicle. This is not a case where the officer used the pat-down as a pretext to extend the detention and search the vehicle. See *United States v. Hughes*, 416 F.3d 131 (2d Cir. 2005) (finding that an illegal pat-down does not taint a subsequent voluntary consent to search a vehicle).

### B. The Attenuation Doctrine Applies

Even if there were some causal connection between an alleged illegality and the consent, the connection is too attenuated to require suppression. The factors courts consider include: (1) the time elapsed between the illegality and the consent; (2) the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975).

Here, approximately twenty-five minutes had elapsed from the initial stop till when the evidence was found (Suppression Tr. 24-25), and the defendant was told he was free to go before giving consent. The defendant had his girlfriend en route to pick him up. These intervening circumstances break the causal chain. The officer's conduct was not flagrant—there were no threats, weapons drawn, or physical coercion.

## VI. THE PROSECUTION'S RELIANCE ON *FLORIDA v. ROYER* IS MISPLACED

The Prosecution cites *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), for the proposition that "consent to search obtained during an unlawful detention is not voluntary." However, *Royer* is distinguishable.

In *Royer*, the defendant was detained in an airport terminal by detectives who held his luggage and asked him to accompany them to a police room. The Court found the detention unlawful because the officers did not have probable cause to believe the defendant was engaged in criminal activity. Here, by contrast, the defendant was told he was free to go before the consent was obtained. The defendant was not detained in any meaningful sense when he offered consent.

Moreover, in *Royer*, the Court emphasized that the "totality of the circumstances" must be examined. Here, the totality demonstrates voluntary consent: the defendant was not handcuffed, the officer's weapon was not drawn, the defendant was fully informed he could leave, and the defendant voluntarily engaged the officer and offered to allow the search.

## VII. EVEN IF SOME MINOR PROCEDURAL ERROR OCCURRED, THE EVIDENCE SHOULD NOT BE SUPPRESSED UNDER THE GOOD FAITH EXCEPTION

Finally, even if this Court were to find some technical deficiency in the officer's conduct, the evidence should not be suppressed under the good faith exception established in *United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897 (1984).

The officer acted in objectively reasonable reliance on apparent consent. The defendant explicitly offered to allow the search. The officer had years of experience. There is no evidence that the officer knew his conduct was unconstitutional at the time—indeed, he testified he had "reasonable suspicion" for the search. Under *Leon*, suppression is not required when the officer's conduct falls within the bounds of reasonable professional judgment.

The discovery that the defendant's license was suspended provided probable cause to arrest or cite him for driving under suspension. Pennsylvania v. Labron, 518 U.S. 938, 940 (1996) (per curiam). This citation was fully justified and does not constitute an unlawful seizure.

### C. The Seizure Ended When the Officer Told the Defendant He Was "Free to Go"

The prosecution's most legally erroneous argument concerns the effect of the officer's statement that the defendant was "free to go." The prosecution contends that this statement was ineffective because the defendant could not actually drive due to his suspended license.

This argument misreads Ohio v. Robinette, 519 U.S. 33, 41-42 (1996), which held that a detention ends when the defendant is told they are free to leave. The Supreme Court has not required that a defendant have the physical ability to leave in order for a seizure to end—only that they understand they are free to go. Here, the defendant understood he was free to go because he had arranged for his girlfriend to pick him up. The officer testified:

"After I explained to her where the car was located, where he was located at, she said that she was going to come pick him up and [I] handed the phone back to [defendant]. I told him he was free to go. Obviously he had a ride coming. He was not intending to drive anywhere. After that, our time was pretty — we were finished. I started walking back to my car." Suppression Tr. 21:5-12.

The prosecution's reliance on United States v. Drayton, 536 U.S. 194, 206 (2002), is misplaced. In Drayton, the Court found no seizure where police did not coerce suspects and suspects were free to leave. Here, similarly, there was no coercion—the defendant was told he was free to go, he had a ride coming, and he was not handcuffed or restrained in any way. The prosecution's attempt to create a "coercive environment" based solely on activated lights fails because the defendant had already voluntarily stopped and was aware a police officer was present.

The prosecution's argument that the defendant "had no realistic option to decline further questioning" is directly contradicted by the officer's testimony that the defendant re-engaged him by asking for clarification on the ID procedures. Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25. The defendant initiated this conversation, not the officer. This demonstrates the defendant understood he was free to go and chose to remain and engage with the officer.

### D. The Consent to Search Was Voluntary

The prosecution's argument that consent was invalid because it was obtained during an unlawful detention fails because the detention had ended before consent was given. Once the defendant was told he was free to go, the Fourth Amendment seizure terminated. The defendant then voluntarily re-engaged the officer.

Under the totality of the circumstances, the consent was clearly voluntary. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 226-27 (1973). The defendant knew he was free to leave—he had just been told so. He initiated the conversation that led to the request for consent. There were no coercive factors present: the officer used no physical force, made no threats, and did not display a weapon. Suppression Tr. 24:3-5. The defendant's "agreeable" demeanor and proactive offer to be searched further support voluntariness. United States v. Drayton, 536 U.S. at 204.

The prosecution's reliance on Florida v. Royer is misplaced because that case is distinguishable. In Royer, the defendant was detained in a small room with his luggage while police investigated. 460 U.S. at 507. Here, the defendant was standing beside his vehicle on the shoulder of the road, had been told he was free to go, and had a ride en route. The coercive environment that invalidated consent in Royer is simply not present here.

### E. Alternatively, the Automobile Exception Justifies the Search

Even if this Court were to find some prior illegality, the automobile exception provides an independent basis for the search. The officer had probable cause to search the vehicle based on the totality of the circumstances: the defendant was driving at 3:42 a.m., was coming from Philadelphia (an area the officer testified was known for drug trafficking), claimed to be lost despite having been to the area before, and engaged in behavior the officer found unusual. Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983).

The prosecution argues the officer lacked probable cause because his suspicions were "subjective." However, the probable cause standard is evaluated under an objective reasonable officer standard. The facts known to the officer—late night driving, coming from a drug source area, being lost despite prior familiarity with the area—would cause a reasonable officer to suspect criminal activity. Probable cause does not require certainty, only a fair probability. Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. at 238.

Under United States v. Ross, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982), probable cause to believe a vehicle contains evidence of a crime justifies a warrantless search of the entire vehicle, including the trunk.

### F. The Fruit of the Poisonous Tree Doctrine Does Not Apply

The prosecution's reliance on Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963), is misplaced because there was no poisonous tree. Even assuming arguendo that some prior illegality occurred, the causal chain was broken by the intervening circumstance of the defendant being told he was free to go. Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975). Because the defendant was no longer seized when he consented, the consent cannot be attributed to any alleged coercion.

The prosecution argues that the "free to go" statement was ineffective because the defendant couldn't drive. However, this misreads the intervening act doctrine. The question is not whether the defendant could physically leave, but whether the defendant understood he was free to leave. Here, the defendant clearly understood he was free to go—he arranged for his girlfriend to pick him up and was waiting for her. The "free to go" statement was an effective intervening act that broke any causal chain.

**FINAL RULING**

The motion to suppress the evidence discovered in the defendant's vehicle is denied. Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by both the prosecution and the defense, and upon a careful analysis of the factual record and applicable precedent, the Court finds that the search of the defendant's vehicle was constitutional. The encounter evolved from a lawful detention into a consensual interaction, and the defendant's consent to search the vehicle was voluntary and not tainted by any prior illegality.

**I. The Initial Detention Was Lawful**

The prosecution's argument that the initial encounter was an unlawful seizure is unpersuasive. The facts establish that the defendant's vehicle was already stopped at the intersection before the officer activated his lights. (Suppression Tr. 13). The officer's activation of lights was for safety purposes, not to effect a stop. Under the community caretaking doctrine, an officer may stop a vehicle to investigate a potential hazard or provide assistance when the circumstances warrant such concern. *Cady v. Dombrowski*, 413 U.S. 433, 441 (1973). A vehicle stopped in an intersection at 3:42 a.m. objectively justifies a welfare check.

Furthermore, once the officer approached the vehicle, he was entitled to request the defendant's driver's license and registration. *Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648, 653 (1979). The discovery that the defendant's license was suspended provided probable cause to issue a citation and seize the license, thereby validating the continued detention for the purpose of processing that traffic violation. *Pennsylvania v. Labron*, 518 U.S. 938, 940 (1996) (per curiam). Consequently, the initial seizure was lawful.

**II. The Detention Terminated Prior to the Consent**

The prosecution contends that the defendant was never "free to go" because he was stranded without a valid license and the officer's lights remained activated. This argument misinterprets the legal standard for the termination of a seizure. A seizure ends when a reasonable person would understand that they are free to leave the officer's presence and terminate the encounter. *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 41-42 (1996).

Here, the officer explicitly told the defendant, "I told him he was free to go," and physically demonstrated this by "walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). The fact that the defendant could not legally drive his vehicle due to a suspended license does not mean he was seized; he was free to wait for his ride, call a friend, or walk away. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable seizures of the person, not against the inconvenience of being stranded due to one's own legal infraction. The officer's use of emergency lights for safety, and the presence of a backup officer standing on the curb, do not negate the clear verbal and physical indication that the detention had concluded. See *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. 194, 204 (2002) (police are not required to inform suspects they are free to go, but when they do, it is strong evidence of a consensual encounter).

**III. The Defendant Voluntarily Re-Engaged the Officer**

The critical fact distinguishing this case from *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), upon which the prosecution relies, is that the defendant voluntarily re-initiated the interaction after being told he was free to leave. The officer was walking away when the defendant "engaged me again in conversation" to ask for a second explanation of the ID procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The officer then asked if he could ask a question, and the defendant agreed, turning around and coming back to the officer. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25).

This sequence of events confirms that the encounter had transformed into a consensual conversation. A consensual encounter does not implicate the Fourth Amendment because no seizure occurs when a person voluntarily cooperates with an officer. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991). The defendant's decision to continue speaking with the officer cannot be attributed to coercion from the prior detention, as the causal chain was broken by the officer's termination of the seizure and the defendant's own initiative. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975).

**IV. The Consent to Search Was Valid**

The prosecution argues that the consent to search was involuntary because it was obtained during an unlawful detention. However, because the detention had ended and the encounter was consensual, this argument fails. The totality of the circumstances demonstrates that the consent was voluntary. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 226-27 (1973).

The defendant was not handcuffed, was not placed under arrest, and the officer's weapon was never drawn. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5). The officer did not use threats or physical force. Most importantly, the defendant initiated the renewed contact and offered to be searched before the officer asked anything other than "do you have anything that would concern me." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The defendant's "agreeable" demeanor and proactive offer to be searched further support the finding of voluntariness. See *United States v. Drayton*, 536 U.S. at 204 (finding consent voluntary where suspects were cooperative and officer's conduct was not coercive).

**V. The Automobile Exception Provides an Alternative Basis**

Alternatively, the search was justified under the automobile exception. The officer had probable cause to search the vehicle based on the totality of the circumstances. The defendant was driving at 3:42 a.m., coming from Philadelphia, claimed to be lost despite having been to the area before, and engaged in behavior the officer found suspiciously "overly cooperative." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). While the officer's suspicion was based in part on subjective intuition, the objective facts—the time of night, the route from a known drug source area, and the inconsistent story—support a finding of probable cause. Under *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982), probable cause to believe a vehicle contains evidence of a crime justifies a warrantless search of the entire vehicle, including the trunk.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the defendant's motion to suppress is denied. The search of the vehicle was conducted pursuant to valid consent given after the defendant had been informed he was free to leave and had voluntarily re-engaged the officer. The evidence discovered is admissible.

# CLOSING STATEMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## In the Matter of the Suppression of Evidence

---

### INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, the defense respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's arguments urging this Court to suppress the evidence discovered in the defendant's vehicle. The prosecution's position rests upon a fundamental mischaracterization of the facts and a misapplication of Fourth Amendment jurisprudence. As the Court correctly determined in its Final Ruling, the motion to suppress should be denied because the search of the defendant's vehicle was constitutional. The defense elaborates on the Court's analysis and addresses the prosecution's specific arguments below.

---

### I. THE ENCOUNTER WAS CONSENSUAL FROM ITS INCEPTION, OR IN THE ALTERNATIVE, ANY INITIAL DETENTION WAS LAWFUL

The prosecution argues that the officer effected an unlawful seizure at the outset of the encounter. This argument fails because the facts establish that the defendant was not seized until he voluntarily chose to engage with the officer.

The record demonstrates that the defendant's vehicle was already stopped at the intersection before the officer activated his emergency lights. (Suppression Tr. 13). The defendant voluntarily pulled his vehicle over to the shoulder of the road, independent of any police directive. (Suppression Tr. 12-13). The officer did not initiate a traffic stop; rather, "the car pulled over on its own." (Suppression Tr. 13, 26, 30, 46, 59-60). This is a critical factual distinction that the prosecution overlooks.

Even assuming arguendo that a seizure occurred, it was justified under the community caretaking doctrine. As the Supreme Court held in Cady v. Dombrowski, 413 U.S. 433, 441 (1973), "the detention of a motorist may be justified on less than probable cause for the purpose of checking his driver's license and registration when the circumstances warrant such concern." A vehicle stopped in an intersection at 3:42 a.m. objectively justifies a welfare check. The officer's activation of lights was for safety purposes, not to effect an investigatory stop.

Furthermore, once the officer approached the vehicle and engaged the defendant in conversation, he was entitled to request the defendant's driver's license and registration. Delaware v. Prouse, 440 U.S. 648, 653 (1979). The discovery that the defendant's license was suspended provided probable cause to issue a citation and seize the license, thereby validating any continued detention for the purpose of processing that traffic violation. Pennsylvania v. Labron, 518 U.S. 938, 940 (1996) (per curiam).

---

### II. THE DETENTION TERMINATED PRIOR TO THE CONSENT, AND THE DEFENDANT WAS FREE TO LEAVE

The prosecution's central argument—that the defendant was never "free to go"—misinterprets the legal standard for the termination of a seizure. A seizure ends when a reasonable person would understand that they are free to leave the officer's presence and terminate the encounter. Ohio v. Robinette, 519 U.S. 33, 41-42 (1996).

The record contains overwhelming evidence that the officer explicitly terminated the seizure:

1. The officer testified: "I told him he was free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10).

2. The officer physically demonstrated this by "walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12).

3. The defendant himself stated that "he was just going to wait for [his girlfriend] to come pick him up" and "was not intending to drive anywhere." (Suppression Tr. 19:12-18).

The prosecution contends that the defendant could not legally drive due to his suspended license, and therefore was not "free to go." This argument is legally erroneous. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable seizures of the person, not against the inconvenience of being stranded due to one's own legal infraction. The defendant was free to wait for his ride, call a friend, walk away, or otherwise terminate the encounter. The fact that he chose to remain and converse with the officer was a voluntary decision.

The prosecution's reliance on the officer's lights remaining activated and the presence of a backup officer does not negate the clear verbal and physical indication that the detention had concluded. As the Supreme Court held in United States v. Drayton, 536 U.S. 194, 204 (2002), "police are not required to inform suspects they are free to go, but when they do, it is strong evidence of a consensual encounter."

---

### III. THE DEFENDANT VOLUNTARILY RE-ENGAGED THE OFFICER, TRANSFORMING THE ENCOUNTER INTO A CONSENSUAL INTERACTION

The critical fact distinguishing this case from Florida v. Royer, 460 U.S. 491 (1983), upon which the prosecution relies, is that the defendant voluntarily re-initiated the interaction after being told he was free to leave.

The record establishes the following sequence:

1. The officer told the defendant "he was free to go" and "started walking back to my car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12).

2. At that point, "[defendant] engaged me again in conversation. He asked me again what he is supposed to do with the license[-]seizure form." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25).

3. The officer then asked if he could ask a question, and "he turned around and came back to me. And he said, yes, I can—he would answer my questions." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25).

This sequence of events confirms that the encounter had transformed into a consensual conversation. A consensual encounter does not implicate the Fourth Amendment because no seizure occurs when a person voluntarily cooperates with an officer. Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991). The defendant's decision to continue speaking with the officer cannot be attributed to coercion from the prior detention, as the causal chain was broken by the officer's termination of the seizure and the defendant's own initiative. Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975).

The prosecution's attempt to characterize this as a continuing seizure ignores the defendant's own voluntary conduct. The defendant was not detained; he was engaging in conversation with an officer out of his own free will.

---

### IV. THE CONSENT TO SEARCH WAS VALIDLY OBTAINED

The prosecution argues that the consent to search was involuntary because it was obtained during an unlawful detention. However, because the detention had ended and the encounter was consensual, this argument fails.

The totality of the circumstances demonstrates that the consent was voluntary. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 U.S. 218, 226-27 (1973). Multiple factors support this finding:

1. **The defendant was not handcuffed or placed under arrest.** (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5).

2. **The officer's weapon was never drawn.** (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5). The officer testified this was because he had "no safety concerns." (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4).

3. **No threats or physical force were used.** The officer simply asked if he could ask a question.

4. **The defendant initiated the renewed contact.** After being told he was free to go, the defendant "engaged me again in conversation." (Suppression Tr. 21:15).

5. **The defendant offered to be searched before the officer asked.** The officer testified: "He—without any kind of probing, he offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2).

6. **The defendant was described as "agreeable."** (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18).

The prosecution argues that the officer's request for consent was itself improper because it was based on "reasonable suspicion." However, even assuming the officer had reasonable suspicion, that does not invalidate the subsequent consent. An officer is permitted to request consent to search from a person who is free to refuse. Florida v. Bostick, 501 U.S. at 434-35. The key question is whether the consent was voluntary, not whether the officer had suspicion.

The prosecution's reliance on the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine is misplaced because there was no "poisonous tree." The detention was lawful, and even if it was not, the consent was sufficiently attenuated from any alleged illegality. Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. at 603-04.

---

### V. THE AUTOMOBILE EXCEPTION PROVIDES AN ALTERNATIVE BASIS FOR THE SEARCH

Even if this Court were to find that the consent was invalid, the search was independently justified under the automobile exception to the Fourth Amendment's warrant requirement.

The Supreme Court has held that "probable cause to believe that a vehicle contains evidence of a crime justifies a warrantless search of the vehicle." United States v. Ross, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982). Probable cause is determined by the totality of the circumstances. Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983).

Here, the officer had probable cause based on the following objective facts:

1. **The defendant was driving at 3:42 a.m.** — a late hour associated with criminal activity.

2. **The defendant was coming from Philadelphia** — a known drug source area.

3. **The defendant claimed to be lost** despite having been to the area before, which the officer reasonably found inconsistent.

4. **The defendant engaged in behavior the officer found suspiciously "overly cooperative"** — a factor that, combined with other circumstances, can support probable cause.

While the prosecution argues that the officer's suspicion was based on "subjective intuition," the totality of these objective circumstances, viewed together, support a finding of probable cause. The prosecution's attempt to parse each individual fact in isolation misappplies the probable cause standard, which requires considering all relevant circumstances together. Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. at 230-31 ("the totality of the circumstances approach is sufficiently flexible to apply in all cases").

---

### REBUTTAL OF PROSECUTION'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

The prosecution's closing statement makes several specific arguments that warrant rebuttal:

**First**, the prosecution argues that the pat-down was unlawful because the officer lacked reasonable suspicion. However, the prosecution conflates two separate issues. The officer did not conduct a pat-down of the defendant's person. The officer asked if he could search the vehicle, and the defendant offered to be searched by placing his hands on the vehicle. (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). The prosecution's argument about the pat-down is simply factually incorrect.

**Second**, the prosecution cites Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), for the proposition that a frisk requires specific, articulable facts. However, this case does not involve a Terry frisk. The defendant offered to be searched; he did not submit to a pat-down.

**Third**, the prosecution argues that the officer's subjective belief that the defendant was "suspicious" is insufficient. The defense agrees—but the prosecution ignores that the officer had objective facts beyond his subjective belief, including the late hour, the route from Philadelphia, and the inconsistent story.

**Fourth**, the prosecution argues that the defendant was "stranded" and therefore not free to leave. This argument finds no support in the law. The defendant could have waited for his ride, walked away, or called another person for assistance. The inability to drive a vehicle does not equate to being seized.

---

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, and for the reasons stated in this Court's Final Ruling, the defense respectfully requests that this Court deny the prosecution's request to suppress the evidence discovered in the defendant's vehicle.

The search was conducted pursuant to valid consent given after the defendant had been informed he was free to leave and had voluntarily re-engaged the officer. Alternatively, the search was justified under the automobile exception. The evidence discovered is admissible.

The prosecution has failed to meet its burden of proving that the search was unconstitutional. The motion to suppress should be denied.

**FINAL RULING**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense, and upon a thorough review of the factual record and applicable legal standards, the Court affirms its prior preliminary ruling. The motion to suppress the evidence discovered in the defendant's vehicle is denied. The prosecution’s arguments regarding an alleged illegal pat-down are unpersuasive in light of the factual findings, and the defense has sufficiently demonstrated that the search was conducted pursuant to a valid consensual encounter.

**I. THE PROSECUTION HAS FAILED TO ESTABLISH AN ILLEGAL SEIZURE OR PAT-DOWN**

The prosecution’s primary contention is that the search of the vehicle was unconstitutional because it stemmed from an illegal pat-down conducted without reasonable suspicion. This argument mischaracterizes the record and misapplies Fourth Amendment jurisprudence.

To justify a pat-down or "frisk" for weapons, an officer must have reasonable, articulable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). The prosecution correctly notes that the officer testified he had "no fear for his safety" and observed no furtive movements or threatening behavior. (Suppression Tr. 32:2-4, 33:3-19). However, the prosecution’s argument collapses because the record does not establish that the officer conducted a non-consensual *Terry* frisk.

The testimony indicates that the defendant himself initiated the physical aspect of the search. The officer testified that "without any kind of probing, [the defendant] offered me to search him by placing his hands on the side of the vehicle." (Suppression Tr. 23:18-24:2). Because the defendant voluntarily offered to be searched, and the officer had not subjected him to a coercive pat-down for weapons prior to this offer, there was no *Terry* violation. The officer’s lack of subjective safety concerns further supports the conclusion that he was not exercising a *Terry* justification for a search, but rather engaging in a consensual interaction. Consequently, the "fruit of the poisonous tree" doctrine, *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484 (1963), does not apply because there was no initial "poisonous tree" in the form of an unconstitutional seizure or frisk.

**II. THE DETENTION LAWFULLY TERMINATED PRIOR TO THE CONSENT**

The Court agrees with the defense that the seizure terminated before the defendant consented to the search. A seizure ends when a reasonable person would feel free to leave or terminate the encounter. *Ohio v. Robinette*, 519 U.S. 33, 41-42 (1996).

The factual findings are clear: the officer completed the paperwork for the suspended license citation, explained the procedures to the defendant, and explicitly told him, "he was free to go." (Suppression Tr. 19:8-10). The officer then physically demonstrated the termination of the encounter by "walking back to [his] car." (Suppression Tr. 21:5-12). The prosecution’s argument that the defendant was not "free to go" because he was stranded without a valid license is legally misplaced. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable seizures of the person, not against the logistical inconvenience of being unable to operate a vehicle due to a suspended license. The defendant was free to wait for his ride, call a friend, or walk away. His choice to remain did not transform the consensual encounter into a seizure.

**III. THE DEFENDANT VOLUNTARILY RE-ENGAGED AND CONSENTED**

The critical sequence of events confirms that the search was consensual. After the officer walked away, the defendant "engaged me again in conversation" to ask a second time about the ID procedures. (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25). The officer returned, answered the question, and then asked if he could ask a question of his own. The defendant agreed, "turn[ed] around and came back to [the officer]." (Suppression Tr. 21:15-22:25).

This re-initiation of contact by the defendant breaks any causal chain between the prior lawful detention and the subsequent consent. *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603-04 (1975). Once the defendant voluntarily re-engaged, the interaction became a consensual encounter implicating no Fourth Amendment protections. *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429, 434 (1991).

The validity of the consent is further supported by the totality of the circumstances. *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218, 226-27 (1973). The defendant was not handcuffed, was not placed under arrest, and the officer’s weapon was never drawn. (Suppression Tr. 24:3-5). The defendant was described as "agreeable" and, crucially, offered to be searched before the officer made any specific request to do so. (Suppression Tr. 23:4-18, 23:18-24:2). Under these facts, the consent was voluntary and not the product of police coercion.

**IV. ALTERNATIVE GROUNDS**

While the Court finds the consent to be valid, the defense also correctly identifies that the automobile exception may provide an alternative basis for the search. Under the totality of the circumstances—the late hour (3:42 a.m.), the travel from a known drug source city (Philadelphia), the defendant’s inconsistent claim of being lost despite prior familiarity with the area, and his unusually cooperative behavior—the officer possessed probable cause to believe the vehicle contained contraband. *United States v. Ross*, 456 U.S. 798, 825 (1982); *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). However, given the clear evidence of voluntary consent, the Court need not rest its decision solely on this ground.

**CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has not met its burden of proving that the search violated the defendant's Fourth Amendment rights. The interaction was consensual, the defendant was free to leave, and he voluntarily re-initiated contact and offered to be searched. The motion to suppress is denied.

**SO ORDERED.**
