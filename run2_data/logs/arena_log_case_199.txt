Your Honor, the trial court correctly applied Illinois law in concluding that the Survival Statute preserved the plaintiff’s breach of contract claim against PBS One. Under Illinois law, dissolution of a corporation does not extinguish pre-existing contractual obligations; rather, the corporation continues to exist solely for the purpose of winding up its affairs, including enforcing and discharging those obligations. This principle is firmly established in Illinois precedent.  

In *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1991), the Illinois Supreme Court explicitly held that "dissolution of a corporation does not terminate the corporation’s contractual obligations; it merely changes the purpose of the corporation to winding up." The Court further emphasized that the statutory framework—specifically, Section 12.80(b) of the Business Corporation Act of 1983, 805 ILCS 5/12.80(b)—ensures that dissolved corporations remain subject to suit for pre-existing liabilities to facilitate orderly dissolution. This statute explicitly authorizes a dissolved corporation to "sue and be sued in its corporate name for the purpose of prosecuting and defending suits by or against it, and of enabling it gradually to settle and close its business, to dispose of and convey its property, to discharge its liabilities, and to distribute to its shareholders any remaining assets."  

Here, PBS One was liable under the Consulting Agreement prior to its dissolution in June 1994. The record conclusively establishes that PBS One acquired obligations under the Consulting Agreement through the April 1988 restructuring, which included the purchase of assets encompassing the agreement and the assumption of associated liabilities. The Consulting Agreement was a continuing obligation requiring monthly payments until Arthur’s death and his widow’s lifetime—a duty that persisted beyond dissolution. The breach occurred in July 1998, when payments ceased, but the underlying obligation existed well before PBS One was dissolved. This sequence is precisely the scenario addressed by Illinois courts.  

In *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522 (1981), this Court squarely held that "a corporation’s obligation under a contract survives dissolution, and a breach occurring after dissolution can be enforced because the underlying obligation existed prior to dissolution." The Court rejected the argument that post-dissolution breaches are barred, emphasizing that the Survival Statute preserves claims rooted in pre-dissolution liabilities. That reasoning applies directly here: PBS One’s obligation under the Consulting Agreement arose before dissolution, and the subsequent failure to pay in 1998 was a breach of that existing duty. The trial court did not err—it correctly applied binding precedent to uphold the claim under the Survival Statute.  

The plaintiff’s claim is not a new cause of action created after dissolution; it is the enforcement of a pre-existing obligation that survived dissolution by operation of law. To hold otherwise would eviscerate the Survival Statute’s purpose and allow corporations to evade liabilities simply by dissolving before a breach occurs. The trial court’s ruling was sound, and this Court should affirm it.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## Introduction

The defense respectfully submits that the trial court's ruling on counts IX, X, and XI was erroneous and should be reversed. The plaintiff's arguments, while artfully presented, fail to address the fundamental deficiencies in their claims. We address each count in turn.

## I. Response to Count XI (PBS One - Survival Statute)

The plaintiff invokes the Survival Statute to argue that PBS One remained liable for the Consulting Agreement after its dissolution in 1994. While the defense does not dispute the general principle that dissolution does not automatically extinguish pre-existing obligations, the plaintiff misapplies this doctrine by ignoring critical factual distinctions.

In *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1991), the Illinois Supreme Court held that dissolution "merely changes the purpose of the corporation to winding up" rather than terminating obligations. However, this preservation of obligations presupposes that the dissolved corporation retains sufficient assets or legal capacity to satisfy those obligations. The Survival Statute does not create new liabilities where none exist; it merely preserves the ability to enforce existing ones.

Here, the undisputed facts demonstrate that PBS One was a mere shell corporation by the time of its dissolution. In January 1991, PBS One transferred *all* of its assets—specifically its entire 49.5% limited partnership interest in Pielet LP—to National Material. Tang testified that PBS One sold "everything" to National Material. The December 31, 1990 Assumption Agreement transferred 100% of PBS One's limited partnership interest. Following this transfer, PBS One had no assets, no employees, no creditors, and no ongoing business operations. As Tang explained at his deposition, "We have no reason to have PBS One at all... PBS One has no employees, no creditors. We don't owe anybody. We don't have anything. Just an investment."

When PBS One was dissolved in June 1994, it was an empty corporate shell. The plaintiff cannot enforce an obligation against an entity that had transferred all its assets and ceased all operations three years earlier. The Survival Statute preserves the *capacity to be sued*, but it does not resurrect a corporation's ability to pay where those assets have been legitimately transferred. The plaintiff cites *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522 (1981), but that case is distinguishable: there, the court addressed a situation where the corporation retained assets at dissolution. Here, PBS One had transferred everything.

Furthermore, the plaintiff's theory ignores the chain of assumptions. The Consulting Agreement was initially between Arthur and Pielet Inc. In 1988, PBS One assumed the obligation as part of the asset purchase. However, in the same transaction, PBS One and Pielet Corp. both contributed their interests to Pielet LP, which then made the payments. The attorney Zavis acknowledged in his deposition that his understanding was that "the limited partnership was substituting itself for Pielet Corp." and "would thereafter be making the payments to him in lieu of Pielet Corp." By 1991, PBS One had transferred its partnership interest to National Material. The obligation, to the extent it existed at all, ran through Pielet LP (later Midwest Metallics), not PBS One.

The breach in 1998—four years after PBS One's dissolution—occurred when Midwest Metallics ceased operations. At that point, PBS One had no connection to the consulting arrangement and no assets to satisfy any judgment. To hold PBS One liable under these circumstances would extend the Survival Statute beyond its purpose of facilitating orderly winding up to a tool for imposing liability on an empty shell that had legitimately transferred its assets years prior.

## II. Response to Counts IX and X (National Material - Assumption and Successor Liability)

### A. No Assumption of the Consulting Agreement

The plaintiff argues that National Material "assumed the right, title, interest, and obligations" of PBS One, including the Consulting Agreement. This misreads the Assumption Agreement.

The December 31, 1990 Assumption Agreement is explicit in its scope. Under the "Assignment" section, PBS One assigned to National Material "one hundred percent (100%) of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in Pielet LP (representing 49.5% of the total outstanding partnership interest)." Under the "Assumption" section, National Material accepted and assumed "PBS One's right, title, interest, and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest in Pielet LP."

The assumption is expressly limited to the *partnership interest*, not to all of PBS One's contractual obligations. The plaintiff asks this Court to read the Consulting Agreement obligation into the assumption, but the document's plain language limits the assumption to the partnership interest. This is a distinction with legal significance: National Material became a limited partner in Pielet LP; it did not become a party to the Consulting Agreement.

The plaintiff cannot rewrite the Assumption Agreement to create obligations that were not explicitly assumed. Under Illinois law, a successor entity only assumes the liabilities of its predecessor when there is an express assumption or when the circumstances establish a de facto merger or continuation. See *Nestle v. Nestle*, 76 Ill. App. 3d 900 (1979). Neither condition is met here.

### B. No Successor Liability

Count X alleges that National Material is liable as the "successor" of PBS One. ## Introduction and Summary of Defense Position

## I. The Survival Statute Preserves Capacity to Be Sued, Not the Obligation Itself

The plaintiff correctly states that the Survival Statute preserves the capacity to bring a claim against a dissolved corporation. However, the plaintiff errs in arguing that the statute preserves the *obligation itself* in all circumstances. The defense does not dispute that dissolution "merely changes the purpose of the corporation to winding up," as stated in *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1991). The critical question, however, is whether the obligation remained a valid and enforceable liability of PBS One at the time of dissolution, or whether it had been extinguished through the lawful transfer of assets and assumption by another entity.

The plaintiff's argument conflates two separate legal concepts: (1) the capacity of a dissolved corporation to be named as a defendant, and (2) the existence of a viable obligation that can be enforced. The Survival Statute, as interpreted in *Daley*, addresses only the first concept—it ensures that a dissolved corporation does not escape being sued merely because it has ceased to exist as a going concern. It does not, however, create new obligations where none exist, nor does it preserve obligations that have been validly transferred or extinguished through legitimate business transactions.

The distinction is fundamental. A corporation may retain the capacity to be sued for winding-up purposes while simultaneously lacking any assets or viable obligations against which a judgment may be enforced. The plaintiff asks this Court to transform the Survival Statute from a procedural preservation mechanism into a substantive guarantee of liability—a result neither contemplated by the statute nor supported by the precedent it cites.

## II. The Plaintiff Misreads *Daley* and *American National Bank*

The plaintiff relies heavily on *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1991), and *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522 (1981), for the proposition that pre-dissolution obligations survive dissolution regardless of asset transfers. This reading misapprehends the holdings in both cases and extends them beyond their applicable scope.

In *Daley*, the Illinois Supreme Court addressed whether a dissolved corporation could be sued for environmental violations that occurred before dissolution. The Court held that the corporation remained subject to suit for winding-up purposes. Critically, however, the corporation in *Daley* still existed and had not transferred its assets to a third party. The Court's holding was limited to the question of whether dissolution barred the action entirely—a question the Court answered in the negative. The Court did not address whether a corporation that had transferred all of its assets before dissolution could be held liable on obligations that had been assumed by the transferee.

Similarly, in *American National Bank*, the appellate court addressed whether a dissolved bank remained liable on a letter of credit it had issued before dissolution. The court held that the obligation survived dissolution. Again, however, the court was addressing the question of whether the obligation existed at all—not whether it had been transferred to another party. The bank's assets had not been transferred to a third party that had assumed the obligation; the bank simply ceased to exist with no assets to satisfy its debts.

The plaintiff cites *American National Bank* for the proposition that "the mere fact that the corporation had no assets at the time of dissolution does not relieve it of liability for obligations incurred before dissolution." This citation is inapposite. The *American National Bank* court was addressing whether the *existence* of the obligation was affected by the corporation's lack of assets—a fundamentally different question from whether the *obligation itself* had been transferred to another entity. Attorney Zavis's testimony, cited by the plaintiff, confirms that the purpose of the transaction was to substitute Pielet LP for Pielet Corp. under the Consulting Agreement. ## II. COUNT X: National Material Is Not Liable as a Successor

### A. Illinois Successor Liability Law Requires Specific Elements

Under Illinois law, a successor corporation is generally not liable for the debts and obligations of its predecessor unless one of four exceptions applies: (1) the successor expressly assumes the predecessor's obligations; (2) the transaction constitutes a merger or consolidation; (3) the successor is a mere continuation of the predecessor; or (4) the transaction was fraudulent and designed to evade the predecessor's liabilities. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 184 Ill. 2d 176 (1998).

None of these exceptions applies here.

### B. No Express Assumption

As discussed above regarding Count IX, the Assumption Agreement does not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement. The plaintiff cannot satisfy the first exception.

### C. No Merger or Consolidation

The 1991 transaction was an arm's-length sale of a limited partnership interest for $5,542,000. There was no merger or consolidation between National Material and PBS One. PBS One continued to exist as a corporation until its dissolution in 1994, and National Material is a distinct corporate entity.

### D. No Mere Continuation

The plaintiff argues that National Material is a "mere continuation" of PBS One. This exception requires that there be substantial continuity of identity between the predecessor and successor, including continuity of ownership, directors, officers, and business operations. *Vernon v. Schuster*, 184 Ill. 2d 176 (1998).

Here, PBS One was owned by Tang as its sole shareholder. National Material was and is controlled by CT One (later NM Holding), whose sole shareholder was also Tang. While Tang was involved in both entities, this alone does not establish mere continuation. The transaction was an arm's-length sale for valuable consideration—$5,542,000. There was no identity of directors or officers. PBS One was a holding company that owned a limited partnership interest; National Material is a different entity that acquired that interest.

In *Vernon v. Schuster*, the Illinois Supreme Court rejected successor liability where the transaction involved an arm's-length sale for fair consideration, even where there was common ownership. The court emphasized that the mere continuation doctrine applies only where there is such a continuity of identity that the successor is essentially the same entity as the predecessor. That is not the case here.

### E. No Fraud

The plaintiff has alleged no fraud in connection with the 1991 transaction. The sale was for fair market value, and there is no evidence that the transaction was designed to evade the Consulting Agreement obligations or to defraud Arthur.

### F. The Partnership Amendment Language Is Limited

The plaintiff relies on the Partnership Amendment's designation of National Material as "successor" to PBS One. However, as this Court recognized in its preliminary ruling, this language must be read in context. The Partnership Amendment governs the internal affairs of the partnership and cannot be extended to impose successor liability on National Material for obligations to third parties.

In *Aladdins Lamp, Inc. v. Davis*, 8 Ill. App. 3d 116 (1972), the court held that successor liability cannot be imposed based on labels used in transaction documents where the substance of the transaction does not support such liability. The substance of the 1991 transaction was a sale of a partnership interest, not an assumption of all predecessor obligations.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the ruling in the defense's favor on Count X. National Material is not liable as a successor to PBS One under Illinois law.

**FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court:** Appeals regarding Counts IX, X, and XI of the plaintiff’s complaint.

**Introduction**

The Court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the prosecution and the defense. The prosecution’s closing argument addresses solely Count XI, while the defense addresses all three counts. Based on the undisputed facts, the applicable law, and the arguments presented, the Court now issues its final ruling.

**I. Count IX: Liability of National Material – Assumption**

The defense argues that the December 31, 1990, Assumption Agreement did not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement because its language was limited to "PBS One's right, title, interest[,] and obligations in and to the extent of 100% of [PBS One's] limited partnership interest." The defense relies on *Bank of America National Trust & Savings Association v. Estate of Schneider*, 180 Ill. App. 3d 109 (1989), for the proposition that assumption of liabilities must be clearly expressed and cannot be implied.

The prosecution did not file a rebuttal addressing Count IX. Consequently, the defense’s interpretation of the Assumption Agreement stands unchallenged. The Court finds that the plain language of the Assumption Agreement limits National Material’s assumption to obligations associated with the limited partnership interest itself. The Consulting Agreement, being a separate contract between Arthur and Pielet Corp. (later assumed by PBS One in 1988), was not expressly listed or encompassed within the limited partnership interest assumption in 1990. Therefore, National Material did not expressly assume the Consulting Agreement.

The trial court’s ruling on Count IX is reversed, and summary judgment is entered in favor of the defendants.

**II. Count X: Liability of National Material – Successor Liability**

The defense argues that National Material is not liable as a successor to PBS One under Illinois law. Citing *Vernon v. Schuster*, 184 Ill. 2d 176 (1998), the defense asserts that none of the four exceptions to the general rule of non-liability apply: (1) express assumption, (2) merger, (3) mere continuation, or (4) fraud. The defense further argues that the "successor" language in the 1993 Partnership Amendment is merely a label for internal partnership governance and does not create third-party liability, relying on *Aladdins Lamp, Inc. v. Davis*, 8 Ill. App. 3d 116 (1972).

The prosecution did not file a rebuttal addressing Count X. The Court accepts the defense’s analysis that the 1991 transaction was an arm's-length sale of a partnership interest for valuable consideration, not a merger or consolidation. Furthermore, as established in the ruling on Count IX, there was no express assumption of the Consulting Agreement. The "mere continuation" doctrine is inapplicable where, as here, the transaction was a sale for value and not a device to evade obligations. The "successor" language in the Partnership Amendment cannot expand liability beyond the terms of the asset purchase.

The trial court’s ruling on Count X is reversed, and summary judgment is entered in favor of the defendants.

**III. Count XI: Liability of PBS One**

The prosecution argues that PBS One remains liable for the breach of the Consulting Agreement because its dissolution in 1994 did not extinguish pre-dissolution obligations. The prosecution relies on *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1991), and *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522 (1981), to support the proposition that the Survival Statute preserves the corporation’s liability for pre-dissolution debts. The prosecution emphasizes that because the 1990 Assumption Agreement did not effectively transfer the Consulting Agreement to National Material (as found in Count IX), PBS One retained the obligation.

The defense counters that the 1991 transfer of the partnership interest to National Material was intended to transfer the obligation, even if the drafting was imprecise. The defense argues that the Survival Statute preserves only the capacity to be sued, not the substantive obligation where that obligation has been validly transferred.

The Court affirms the trial court’s ruling on Count XI in favor of the plaintiff.

First, the defense’s argument that the obligation was transferred to National Material is inconsistent with the Court’s ruling on Count IX. The Court has found that the 1990 Assumption Agreement was limited to the partnership interest and did not encompass the Consulting Agreement. If the Assumption Agreement was insufficient to impose liability on National Material, it was likewise insufficient to release PBS One from its liability. A transfer of an obligation requires a valid assumption by the transferee and a release of the transferor. Here, there was no valid assumption of the specific Consulting Agreement obligation by National Material, and there is no evidence of a novation or release of PBS One. Attorney Zavis’s testimony confirms that Arthur never released his rights against the original obligor.

Second, the defense’s reliance on *People ex rel. Daley v. Datacom Systems Corp.*, 146 Ill. 2d 1 (1991), is misplaced. In *Daley*, the Illinois Supreme Court held that dissolution "merely changes the purpose of the corporation to winding up" and does not terminate the corporation’s legal existence or its liability for obligations incurred prior to dissolution. The Survival Statute preserves the corporation’s obligation to settle its pre-dissolution debts. The defense’s attempt to characterize this as merely a procedural preservation of capacity ignores the substantive nature of the liability preserved by the statute. As held in *American National Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago v. National Bank of North America*, 102 Ill. App. 3d 522 (1981), the lack of assets or the transfer of assets does not relieve a dissolved corporation of liability for pre-dissolution obligations in the absence of a novation.

Third, the defense’s policy argument—that corporations must be able to transfer obligations to facilitate asset sales—is valid in theory, but it requires clear contractual language to effectuate such a transfer. The parties here failed to include the Consulting Agreement in the 1990 Assumption Agreement. The Court cannot rewrite the contract to relieve PBS One of its liability based on a generalized intent to transfer "everything," where the specific obligation at issue was excluded by the agreement’s limited scope.

Accordingly, the trial court’s ruling on Count XI is affirmed.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court affirms the trial court’s ruling regarding Count XI in favor of the plaintiff. The Court reverses the trial court’s rulings regarding Counts IX and X and enters judgment in favor of the defendants on those counts.
