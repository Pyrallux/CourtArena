As appellate prosecution counsel, I submit that joinder of the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges was unquestionably proper under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The statute permits joinder when charges are "based on the same conduct or on a series of acts connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶16, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499. Here, the charges shared critical common features that established a single scheme of control and intimidation within the household—specifically, the defendant's consistent use of threats, violence, and coercion against both victims to maintain dominance and suppress reporting of criminal conduct.  

The evidence demonstrates a clear pattern of interconnected criminal acts. The sexual assault charges arose from three years of abuse against V.G., characterized by threats to take her brother away or send her away to Mexico or California if she disclosed the assaults. State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 985, 471 N.W.2d 24 (Ct. App. 1991) (holding that charges involving "interconnected criminal acts" with consistent patterns of threats against victims are properly joined). Similarly, the intimidation charges stemmed from the defendant’s jail calls seeking to pressure M.S. and V.G. to alter their statements to police and testify favorably at sentencing—directly invoking the same threats: he told M.S. "you better start thinking that one day I'm coming out... you are going to be sorry," and warned V.G. during a call that he would "kill the boy" if police were called. These threats mirror the sexual assault threats, demonstrating a unified scheme to control both victims through fear of harm to children.  

The connection is further cemented by the defendant’s conduct on October 26, 2009—the day of the domestic violence incident. V.G. testified that the sexual assaults ceased after police arrested the defendant that day, and M.S. confirmed the defendant’s threats during the altercation: "He told V.G. to hang up the phone... or he was going to kill the boy and he was going to kill himself." This incident directly precipitated the intimidation campaign, as the defendant immediately began calling M.S. from jail to suppress reporting of both the domestic violence and the sexual assaults. State v. Doss, 116 Wis. 2d 374, 385, 342 N.W.2d 43 (1984) (recognizing that "evidence of other acts is admissible to show motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake," including explaining why a victim delayed reporting). Here, the intimidation evidence was essential to explain V.G.’s prolonged silence about the sexual assaults—she feared the defendant would take her brother away or report her to police, as he had threatened during both the assaults and the jail calls.  

Crucially, V.G. is a victim in both sets of charges: the sexual assault counts and one intimidation count specifically named her as the victim. State v. Kienzle, 2007 WI App 159, ¶18, 304 Wis. 2d 155, 736 N.W.2d 741 (joinder proper when "charges involved the same victim and were part of a continuous course of conduct"). The circuit court correctly noted that joining the charges was "logical" because "one of the intimidation counts involves the same victim of the sexual assaults—V.G.—making it logical to connect those two for purposes of trial." This shared victimhood alone satisfies the statutory requirement for joinder under Williams.  

Moreover, the State’s argument that the intimidation evidence provided necessary context for the sexual assault charges was entirely sound. As the circuit court recognized, the intimidation calls directly related to the defendant’s efforts to manipulate the domestic violence case, which was the immediate catalyst for V.G.’s delayed reporting of the sexual assaults. State v. Martin, 1998 WI App 153, ¶12, 221 Wis. 2d 232, 584 N.W.2d 397 (evidence of prior threats is relevant to "show why a victim delayed reporting"). The defendant’s threats to kill himself and take the children if M.S. did not attend sentencing—coupled with his promises to "change" if she supported him—explains why V.G. only disclosed the sexual assaults two days after the sentencing hearing. Without this context, the jury would have lacked a complete understanding of the timeline and motivations behind the reporting delay, which is a critical element of the sexual assault case.  

Finally, the circuit court properly mitigated any risk of prejudice by instructing the jury that the defendant’s prior convictions could only be used for credibility and not as proof of guilt in the current charges. This instruction aligns with the precedent that joinder is proper when the trial court gives limiting instructions to prevent jury confusion. State v. Lomax, 2012 WI App 35, ¶21, 339 Wis. 2d 614, 811 N.W.2d 407 (joinder upheld where "context evidence was properly admitted and jury received clear instructions"). The defense had ample opportunity to cross-examine on the domestic violence incident, and the circuit court expressly permitted such cross-examination.  

In sum, the charges were properly joined because they arose from a single scheme of intimidation and control within the household, involved common victims, and the intimidation evidence was intrinsically relevant to explaining the context of the sexual assault reporting delay. The circuit court’s ruling was fully consistent with Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) and controlling precedent.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Defendant-Respondent Luis Salinas respectfully submits this defense opening response in opposition to the State's petition for review. The Court of Appeals correctly reversed the circuit court's joinder ruling because the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were improperly joined under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The prosecution's arguments to the contrary mischaracterize the record and ignore controlling precedent establishing that joinder requires more than the mere presence of overlapping victims or generalized "patterns" of conduct. The charges here arose from fundamentally separate criminal episodes occurring at different times, involving different victims, and addressing different criminal objectives. The circuit court's error in joining these charges was not harmless, as the introduction of highly inflammatory domestic violence evidence prejudiced Salinas's right to a fair trial on the sexual assault charges.

---

## II. ARGUMENT

### A. The Charges Did Not Satisfy the Statutory Requirements for Joinder Under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1)

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) permits joinder of charges only when they are "based on the same conduct or on a series of acts connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." The prosecution bears the burden of establishing that joinder is proper, and this Court has consistently interpreted the statute narrowly to prevent the unfair prejudice that arises when jurors hear evidence of multiple unrelated crimes. *State v. Williams*, 2010 WI 7, ¶16, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499.

The prosecution argues that the charges share a "single scheme or plan" of "control and intimidation within the household." This characterization fundamentally distorts the record. The sexual assault charges arise from criminal conduct that occurred between 2006 and October 26, 2009—the date Salinas was arrested for domestic violence. The intimidation charges, by contrast, arise from jail telephone calls made between October 2009 and May 2010, after Salinas was already incarcerated. These are not a "series of acts connected together" but rather two entirely separate criminal episodes separated by the defendant's incarceration.

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Higginbotham*, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (Ct. App. 1991), is misplaced. In *Higginbotham*, the court upheld joinder where the defendant robbed multiple victims in a single evening using a consistent method—a true "series of acts connected together." Here, by contrast, the sexual assaults occurred over three years in private, while the intimidation occurred after arrest and concerned entirely different conduct. The intimidation calls never mentioned the sexual assaults; they concerned only the domestic violence case.

Critically, the prosecution cannot establish that the intimidation charges were "based on the same conduct" as the sexual assaults. The intimidation charges arose from Salinas's attempts to influence M.S. and V.G.'s statements regarding the October 26, 2009 domestic violence incident and to encourage them to attend his sentencing hearing. As Salinas's lawyer explicitly stated during the pre-trial conference, the intimidation charges arose from Salinas "trying to get them to consider a better sentencing recommendation" in the domestic violence case and "were completely separate from any sort of sexual assault allegation." The prosecution never disputed this characterization below—it explicitly agreed that the intimidation was about "getting them to consider a better sentencing recommendation."

The prosecution's argument that V.G. was a "common victim" in both sets of charges fails to establish proper joinder. While V.G. was named as the victim in one intimidation count, the intimidation alleged was not related to the sexual assaults. The intimidation count concerning V.G. alleged that Salinas attempted to pressure her to change her statement about being slapped on October 26, 2009—not about any sexual conduct. *See* *State v. Kienzle*, 2007 WI App 159, ¶18, 304 Wis. 2d 155, 736 N.W.2d 741 (joinder proper when charges involve same victim and "continuous course of conduct"). Here, there was no continuous course of conduct linking the sexual assaults to the intimidation—the intimidation concerned an entirely separate incident and time period.

### B. The "Context" Argument Fails Because the Intimidation Evidence Was Not Necessary to Explain V.G.'s Delayed Reporting

The prosecution argues that the intimidation evidence was necessary to explain why V.G. delayed reporting the sexual assaults until May 13, 2010. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

First, the record establishes that V.G. did not delay reporting the sexual assaults because of the intimidation calls. V.G. testified that she did not report the sexual assaults "because I just wanted him gone because I thought that with him gone it just wouldn't be going through all the things that I was going through, and I thought that was just—I was afraid and just embarrassed and ashamed of everything." She was also worried her mother "would be ashamed" and "blame herself." These reasons for non-disclosure have nothing to do with the intimidation calls, which occurred after Salinas's arrest and after V.G. had already decided not to report the sexual assaults.

Second, the prosecution's own statements to the circuit court undermine its "context" argument. At the pre-trial conference, the State explicitly stated that the intimidation evidence was relevant because "it starts with a domestic violence situation between the victim's mother and the defendant and evolves until we get to the disclosure in this case." This is not a claim that the intimidation explains delayed reporting—it is a claim that the domestic violence incident itself led to the disclosure. But this argument proves too much: if the domestic violence arrest caused V.G. to finally feel safe enough to disclose, then the intimidation calls—which occurred after the arrest—cannot explain the delay.

Third, even if some contextual evidence were relevant, the prosecution could have introduced such evidence through a limiting instruction without joining the charges. *See* *State v. Doss*, 116 Wis. 2d 374, 385, 342 N.W.2d 43 (1984) (other acts evidence admissible for limited purposes with proper jury instruction). The circuit court could have admitted the relevant portions of the jail calls as other-acts evidence while maintaining separate trials. The prosecution's decision to join the charges was driven by convenience, not necessity.

### C. The Joinder Was Fundamentally Unfair and Prejudicial

Even if joinder were technically permissible, the circuit court's decision to join the charges was fundamentally unfair given the nature of the evidence introduced. The prosecution's opening statement described a "pattern of violence, intimidation, threats, and most disturbingly, repeated sexual assault of a child." It painted Salinas as a domestic abuser who strangled his girlfriend in front of her daughter and held a knife to his own child's throat. This inflammatory evidence overwhelmed the actual evidence of sexual assault and made it impossible for the jury to fairly evaluate the sexual assault charges.

The defense specifically warned the circuit court about this prejudice. Salinas's lawyer told the jury in opening: "Just because he might be a bad guy is really irrelevant." The lawyer emphasized that "the intimidation counts relate to a prior conviction for hitting M.S. and V.G. The intimidation is not about a sexual assault" and urged the jury to "parse out the difference between Mr. Salinas having a jaded past, perhaps having done a bad thing to these two people who are going to testify and whether the charges that he's here for today actually happened or not."

Despite these warnings, the prosecution devoted substantial closing argument to the domestic violence incident, describing how Salinas "takes a knife and he takes his little boy" and threatens to kill himself and the child. This was not evidence relevant to the sexual assault charges—it was character assassination designed to prejudice the jury.

The prosecution argues that jury instructions cured any prejudice. This argument fails. The instruction that prior convictions could only be used for credibility, not as proof of guilt, did nothing to address the massive prejudice from the domestic violence evidence introduced through the testimony of V.G. and M.S. about the October 26, 2009 incident. The jury heard detailed testimony about Salinas attempting to strangle M.S. and threatening to kill his son with a knife. No limiting instruction could cure the prejudice from this evidence.

This Court has recognized that joinder is improper when "the jury would be unable to keep separate the evidence relevant to each charge and would consider evidence of one charge as proof of the other." *State v. Williams*, 2010 WI 7, ¶23, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499. Here, the prosecution's entire strategy was to use the domestic violence evidence to prove Salinas was capable of committing sexual assault—a classic "propensity" theory that Wisconsin law expressly prohibits. The jury instructions could not overcome the tidal wave of prejudice from the joined evidence.

### D. The Error Was Not Harmless

Given the overwhelming prejudice from the joined evidence, the circuit court's joinder error was not harmless. The jury convicted Salinas on all charges, including the sexual assault charges, but the evidence on those charges was far from overwhelming. V.G. admitted on cross-examination that she had "very little recall of the sexual assaults" and that her memory was "very vivid" regarding the domestic violence incident. Salinas's lawyer argued that the "glossing over" of the sexual assault allegations should convince the jury they never happened.

The prosecution's closing argument succeeded in part because it distracted the jury with the inflammatory domestic violence evidence. A reasonable jury, considering only the sexual assault evidence, might have had reasonable doubt. But the joined evidence made Salinas appear so dangerous that the jury likely convicted him on the sexual assault charges as punishment for the domestic violence, not based on proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the sexual assaults actually occurred.

The Court of Appeals correctly found that the joinder error was not harmless. This Court should affirm.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**STATE OF WISCONSIN v. LUIS SALINAS**

**CASE NO.** [INSERT CASE NO.]

**DECISION**

This matter comes before the court on the petition for review by the State of Wisconsin following the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the circuit court’s order joining the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges. The State argues that joinder was proper under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) because the charges were part of a single scheme of control and intimidation involving common victims. The Defendant, Luis Salinas, argues that the charges arose from separate criminal episodes with distinct objectives and that joinder resulted in prejudicial error. Based on the facts and arguments provided, the court affirms the decision of the Court of Appeals.

**I. STANDARD FOR JOINDER**

Wisconsin Stat. § 971.12(1) provides that joinder of offenses is permitted when the charges "are based on the same conduct or on a series of acts connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." The statute is to be construed narrowly to prevent the prejudice that results from trying a defendant on multiple unrelated charges. State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶16, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499. The burden is on the State to establish that the statutory requirements for joinder are met. Id.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Absence of a Single Scheme or Plan**

The State contends that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges constitute a "single scheme of control and intimidation within the household." While the State correctly identifies that both sets of charges involve threats against family members, the legal requirements for joinder demand a more specific connection than a general pattern of behavior.

The sexual assault charges stem from conduct occurring between 2006 and October 26, 2009, involving repeated sexual contact with V.G. The intimidation charges, however, arise from conduct occurring between October 2009 and May 2010, specifically involving phone calls made from jail following the defendant's arrest for domestic violence. As the defense argues, these are distinct criminal episodes separated by the defendant's incarceration. The intimidation calls were directed specifically at influencing the sentencing proceedings for the domestic violence convictions, not at suppressing the sexual assault allegations—which had not yet been reported. The State's own admission at the pre-trial conference that the intimidation was about "getting them to consider a better sentencing recommendation" underscores the distinct objective of these acts.

The State relies on State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (Ct. App. 1991), to argue for joinder based on interconnected acts. However, Higginbotham involved a series of robberies committed in a single evening using a similar modus operandi. Here, the acts are separated by significant time and a change in the defendant's status from free perpetrator to incarcerated inmate attempting to manipulate a separate legal proceeding. The connection asserted by the State—a "scheme of control"—is too attenuated to satisfy the statutory requirement of a "series of acts connected together." The intimidation was a discrete reaction to the arrest for domestic violence, not a continuation of the sexual assault plan.

**B. Lack of a Continuous Course of Conduct**

The State further argues that joinder is proper because V.G. is a common victim. While V.G. is named as a victim in one of the intimidation counts and the sexual assault counts, joinder based solely on a common victim requires a "continuous course of conduct." State v. Kienzle, 2007 WI App 159, ¶18, 304 Wis. 2d 155, 736 N.W.2d 741. The intimidation of V.G. regarding the domestic violence incident (the slap) does not constitute a continuous course of conduct with the sexual assaults that ended months prior. The intimidation count involving V.G. was predicated on her witnessing the domestic violence and her potential testimony regarding that event, not her status as a victim of sexual assault. Therefore, the common victim factor does not salvage the joinder in this context.

**C. Prejudice and the Failure of Limiting Instructions**

Even if joinder were arguably permissible under the statute, the circuit court's decision resulted in substantial prejudice that was not harmless. The trial involved the admission of highly inflammatory evidence regarding the domestic violence incident, including testimony that Salinas strangled M.S. and threatened to kill his four-year-old son with a knife. This evidence was irrelevant to the elements of the sexual assault charges and served primarily to paint the defendant as a violent individual, thereby encouraging a conviction based on propensity rather than proof of the specific sexual assault charges.

The defense correctly notes that the prosecution's opening and closing arguments emphasized this "pattern of violence," urging the jury to view the sexual assault charges as part of a broader narrative of brutality. While the circuit court provided a limiting instruction regarding prior convictions, such instructions are often insufficient to cure the prejudice of trying joined charges where the evidence of one crime is vastly more inflammatory than the other. See State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶23, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499 (recognizing the risk that juries will consider evidence of one charge as proof of another). The State's reliance on State v. Doss, 116 Wis. 2d 374, 385, 342 N.W.2d 43 (1984), to justify the evidence as "context" for the delay in reporting is unavailing; V.G. testified she delayed reporting due to shame and fear, not specifically because of the jail calls. The probative value of the intimidation evidence was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The circuit court erred in joining the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges. The charges did not arise from the same conduct or a single scheme or plan as required by Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). Furthermore, the joinder resulted in significant prejudice that deprived the defendant of a fair trial on the sexual assault charges. The decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the circuit court is affirmed.

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) permits joinder when charges are "based on the same conduct or on a series of acts connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶16, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499. This requirement is satisfied where the offenses share a "common factual nexus" or are "logically connected" through the defendant’s overarching pattern of coercive control. State v. This is not a "separate episode" but a coordinated effort to maintain control: the sexual assaults created the conditions for the domestic violence incident, and the intimidation calls were designed to prevent exposure of the entire pattern of abuse.

The defense’s reliance on State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (Ct. App. 1991), is misplaced. In Higginbotham, the court upheld joinder of robbery charges committed in a single evening using a consistent modus operandi. Here, by contrast, the acts are separated by significant time and a fundamental change in Salinas's circumstances—from a free perpetrator living in the household to an incarcerated inmate attempting to manipulate a separate legal proceeding. The connection the State asserts—a "scheme of control"—is too attenuated to satisfy the statutory requirement of acts "connected together" or constituting "parts of a single scheme or plan."

### B. The Intimidation Was Not Designed to Suppress Sexual Assault Allegations

The prosecution claims the intimidation calls were "an attempt to suppress the sexual assault allegations" by ensuring V.G. and M.S. did not "disclose the full scope of his abuse." This argument is directly contradicted by the record.

The transcripts of the phone calls (¶¶6-7) show that Salinas pressured M.S. to change her statement about the domestic violence incident—specifically, to say he did not try to strangle her and did not threaten to kill her. Salinas also asked M.S. to convince V.G. to change her statement about the domestic violence incident—to say Salinas did not hit her. At no point in the recorded calls did Salinas reference the sexual assaults. The sexual assaults had not been reported at the time of these calls, and there is no evidence that Salinas was aware that V.G. had not disclosed the full extent of his abuse.

As the defense argued below, the intimidation charges arose from Salinas "trying to get them to consider a better sentencing recommendation" in the domestic violence case and were "completely separate from any sort of sexual assault allegation." The prosecution's attempt to relabel these calls as part of a unified scheme to conceal sexual abuse finds no support in the evidentiary record.

## II. THE COMMON VICTIM FACTOR DOES NOT SUPPORT JOINDER

### A. V.G. Was Not a Victim of the Intimidation in the Same Way She Was a Victim of Sexual Assault

The prosecution argues that joinder is proper because V.G. is a common victim in both sets of charges. However, as the circuit court correctly recognized, the intimidation count involving V.G. was "predicated on her testimony about the domestic violence incident"—not on her status as a victim of sexual assault. The intimidation charge related to V.G.'s potential testimony about the slap and the domestic violence that occurred on October 26, 2009, not to the sexual assaults that had occurred over the preceding three years.

In State v. Kienzle, 2007 WI App 159, ¶18, 304 Wis. 2d 155, 736 N.W.2d 741, this Court held that joinder based on a common victim requires a "continuous course of conduct." Here, the intimidation of V.G. regarding the domestic violence incident does not constitute a continuous course of conduct with the sexual assaults that ended months prior. The sexual assaults occurred when V.G.'s mother was not home and when her brothers were asleep or outside. The intimidation involved V.G.'s potential testimony about an incident she witnessed. These are distinct forms of victimization arising from different criminal episodes.

### B. The Prosecution Misapplies State v. Doss

The prosecution invokes State v. Doss, 116 Wis. 2d 374, 385, 342 N.W.2d 43 (1984), to argue that the intimidation evidence was relevant to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting the sexual assaults. However, Doss is distinguishable.

In Doss, the court held that evidence of prior threats was admissible to explain why a victim delayed reporting a sexual assault when the defendant had threatened to kill the victim if she told anyone. Here, by contrast, V.G. testified she delayed reporting because she was "afraid and ashamed" and worried her mother "would be ashamed" and "blame herself" (¶19). V.G. did not testify that she delayed reporting because of the jail phone calls. The prosecution cannot manufacture relevance by arguing that the intimidation evidence "could have" affected V.G.'s decision not to report when the victim herself never cited this as a reason for the delay.

Moreover, even if some relevance existed, the probative value of the intimidation evidence was substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. The domestic violence evidence—including testimony that Salinas strangled M.S. and threatened to kill his four-year-old son with a knife—was far more inflammatory than the underlying sexual assault charges. This Court recognized in State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶23, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499, that limiting instructions are often insufficient to cure the prejudice of trying joined charges where the evidence of one crime is vastly more inflammatory than the other.

## III. THE PROSECUTION'S EVIDENCE WAS UNFAIRLY PREJUDICIAL

### A. The Domestic Violence Evidence Was Irrelevant to the Sexual Assault Charges

The prosecution claims the domestic violence evidence was "necessary to explain why V.G. delayed reporting." This argument fails because V.G. herself testified about why she delayed reporting, and her explanation did not reference the domestic violence incident or the jail phone calls. V.G. testified she did not report the sexual assaults "because I just wanted him gone" and "I was afraid and just embarrassed and ashamed of everything" (¶19). The prosecution cannot rely on the victim's own testimony to establish relevance and then contradict that testimony to justify admission of inflammatory evidence.

### B. The Prosecution's Opening and Closing Arguments Compound the Prejudice

The prosecution's opening statement described the case as showing a "pattern of violence, intimidation, threats, and most disturbingly, repeated sexual assault of a child occurred within a family environment." This narrative was not a neutral presentation of evidence but a deliberate attempt to convince the jury that Salinas was a violent person who must have committed the sexual assaults. The prosecution told the jury that October 26, 2009, was "a day of horror" that "stopped what was happening to V.G." and "the last day he assaulted her"—statements that conflated the domestic violence incident with the sexual assaults and invited the jury to convict based on propensity.

In closing, the prosecution argued that Salinas used a knife to "desperately" keep M.S. from calling police—a scenario designed to paint Salinas as a dangerous individual. While the defense acknowledged that Salinas had been "punished for" the domestic violence, the prosecution insisted on retrying those facts before the jury deciding the sexual assault charges.

The circuit court's limiting instruction regarding prior convictions (¶27) was insufficient to cure this prejudice. As this Court recognized in State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶23, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499, jury instructions cautioning that attorneys' remarks are not evidence and that closing arguments are opinion cannot undo the damage of admitting highly inflammatory evidence that serves no legitimate purpose in proving the sexual assault charges.

## IV. THE PROSECUTION CANNOT ESTABLISH HARMLESS ERROR

The prosecution bears the burden of proving that the joinder error was harmless. The prosecution has not met this burden because the evidence of the domestic violence incident was so inflammatory that it likely affected the jury's decision on the sexual assault charges. The prosecution's own arguments demonstrate the centrality of this evidence to its case: the prosecution devoted substantial portions of its opening and closing to describing the domestic violence, knowing it could not prove the sexual assault charges without first persuading the jury that Salinas was capable of such acts.

The prosecution's claim that "the jury still convicted on those charges, proving they evaluated the evidence separately" is逻辑ically flawed. The jury's verdict demonstrates only that the jury found the sexual assault charges proven beyond a reasonable doubt—it does not demonstrate that the inflammatory domestic violence evidence did not improperly influence that finding. The prosecution's argument conflates the sufficiency of the evidence with the absence of prejudice, which this Court should reject.

The prosecution has failed to establish that the charges arose from the same conduct or a single scheme or plan as required by Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The joinder resulted in substantial prejudice that was not harmless, and the defendant was deprived of a fair trial on the sexual assault charges.

**STATE OF WISCONSIN v. LUIS SALINAS**

**CASE NO.** [INSERT CASE NO.]

**DECISION ON REBUTTALS**

This matter comes before the court for a final ruling following the submission of rebuttal arguments by the State of Wisconsin and the Defendant, Luis Salinas. The court has reviewed the rebuttals, the underlying facts, and the applicable legal standards regarding the joinder of offenses under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The primary issue remains whether the circuit court erred in joining the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges and, if so, whether such error was harmless.

For the reasons set forth below, the court adheres to its preliminary ruling affirming the decision of the Court of Appeals. The State has not demonstrated that the charges constitute parts of a single scheme or plan as required by statute, and the prejudicial effect of the joinder was not harmless.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The question of whether joinder is proper under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) is a matter of law that this court reviews de novo. State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶16, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499. The statute provides that joinder is permitted when the charges "are based on the same conduct or on a series of acts connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." Id. This provision must be construed narrowly to prevent the prejudice resulting from trying a defendant on multiple unrelated charges. Id.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Joinder Was Improper as the Charges Do Not Constitute a Single Scheme or Plan**

The State argues that the intimidation charges and the sexual assault charges are part of a "single scheme of domestic control and intimidation." The State contends that the jail calls were a direct continuation of the threats Salinas used during the sexual assaults, specifically the threats to take V.G.'s brother away. However, this argument stretches the concept of a "single scheme" beyond its recognized limits.

While the State correctly identifies a general pattern of coercive behavior, the legal requirement for joinder demands a more specific connection than a shared modus operandi or a general "scheme of control." As noted in State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶16, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499, the statute requires that the acts be "connected together" or constitute parts of a "single scheme or plan." Here, the sexual assault charges stem from conduct occurring between 2006 and October 26, 2009. The intimidation charges arise from conduct occurring between October 2009 and May 2010, while Salinas was incarcerated.

Crucially, the record shows that the objective of the intimidation calls was distinct from the objective of the sexual assaults. At the pre-trial conference, the State admitted that the intimidation calls were about "getting them to consider a better sentencing recommendation" in the domestic violence case. The transcripts of the calls confirm this, as Salinas pressured M.S. and V.G. to change their statements regarding the domestic violence incident—the strangulation and the slap—not regarding any sexual assault. The sexual assaults had not been reported at the time of the calls, and there is no evidence Salinas knew they might be reported. Therefore, the calls cannot be viewed as part of a scheme to suppress the sexual assault allegations; rather, they were a discrete attempt to manipulate the sentencing for a separate crime.

The State's reliance on State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (Ct. App. 1991), is unpersuasive. In Higginbotham, the court upheld joinder of robberies committed in a single evening with a consistent modus operandi. Id. at 985. Here, the acts are separated by months, involve different victims (M.S. vs. V.G. for the intimidation counts), and occur in fundamentally different contexts—one in the home, the other from a jail cell. The connection asserted by the State—a "scheme of control"—is too attenuated to satisfy the statutory requirement. The intimidation was a reaction to the arrest for domestic violence, not a continuation of the plan to commit sexual assault.

**B. The Common Victim Factor Does Not Save the Joinder**

The State further argues that joinder is proper because V.G. is a common victim. However, joinder based solely on a common victim requires a "continuous course of conduct." State v. Kienzle, 2007 WI App 159, ¶18, 304 Wis. 2d 155, 736 N.W.2d 741. The conduct here was not continuous. The sexual assaults ended on October 26, 2009, when Salinas was arrested. The intimidation of V.G. occurred months later, via jail calls, regarding her testimony in the domestic violence case. The fact that V.G. was a victim of both does not bridge the gap between the distinct criminal episodes. The intimidation count involving V.G. was predicated on her witnessing the domestic violence, not on her status as a victim of sexual assault. Thus, the common victim factor does not establish the necessary logical nexus for joinder.

**C. The Error Was Not Harmless**

Even if joinder were arguably permissible, the defense has demonstrated that the error was not harmless. The trial involved the admission of extensive evidence regarding the domestic violence incident, including testimony that Salinas strangled M.S. and threatened to kill his four-year-old son with a knife. This evidence was highly inflammatory and irrelevant to the elements of the sexual assault charges.

The State argues that this evidence was admissible under State v. Doss, 116 Wis. 2d 374, 385, 342 N.W.2d 43 (1984), to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting. However, this argument fails because V.G. herself testified that she delayed reporting because she was "afraid and just embarrassed and ashamed," and because she did not want her mother to blame herself. She did not cite the jail calls or the domestic violence incident as the cause of her delay. The State cannot rely on *Doss* to admit evidence for a purpose the victim's own testimony does not support.

The prosecution's opening and closing arguments compounded the prejudice by weaving the domestic violence incident into a narrative of "pattern of violence" designed to persuade the jury that Salinas was a violent man who must have committed the sexual assaults. As recognized in State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶23, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499, limiting instructions are often insufficient to cure the prejudice of trying joined charges where the evidence of one crime is vastly more inflammatory than the other. Given the vivid detail of the domestic violence testimony compared to V.G.'s "very little recall" of the specific sexual assaults, there is a significant probability that the jury convicted Salinas of the sexual assaults based on the inflammatory evidence of the domestic violence and intimidation, rather than on proof beyond a reasonable doubt of the sexual assault charges.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The State has not met its burden to show that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were part of the same conduct or a single scheme or plan under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The charges were distinct criminal episodes separated by time and circumstance. Furthermore, the joinder resulted in substantial prejudice that was not harmless. The decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the circuit court is affirmed.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR THE DEFENSE

## INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits that this Court should affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals, which reversed the circuit court's joinder of the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges. The State has failed to demonstrate that the charges constitute "parts of a single scheme or plan" as required by Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1), and the prejudicial effect of the improper joinder was not harmless. The State's closing argument mischaracterizes the record and stretches the concept of a "single scheme" beyond its recognized legal limits. For the reasons set forth below, the Court should affirm the Court of Appeals decision.

## I. THE JOINDER WAS IMPROPER UNDER WISCONSIN STATUTE § 971.12(1)

Wisconsin Statute § 971.12(1) permits joinder of offenses only when the charges "are based on the same conduct or on a series of acts connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." This provision must be construed narrowly to prevent the prejudice resulting from trying a defendant on multiple unrelated charges. State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶16, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499.

The record demonstrates that the sexual assault charges and the intimidation charges are distinct criminal episodes that do not satisfy the statutory requirements for joinder. The sexual assaults occurred between 2006 and October 26, 2009, when V.G. was 13 years old and older. The intimidation charges arise from conduct occurring between October 2009 and May 2010, while Salinas was incarcerated in jail following his arrest for domestic violence.

Critically, the objective of the intimidation calls was distinct from the objective of the sexual assaults. At the pre-trial conference, the State explicitly admitted that the intimidation calls were about "getting them to consider a better sentencing recommendation" in the domestic violence case. The transcripts of the calls confirm this, as Salinas pressured M.S. and V.G. to change their statements regarding the domestic violence incident—the strangulation and the slap—not regarding any sexual assault. The sexual assaults had not been reported at the time of the calls, and there is no evidence Salinas knew they might be reported.

## II. REBUTTAL OF THE STATE'S "SINGLE SCHEME" ARGUMENT

The State argues that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges are part of a "single scheme of domestic control and intimidation." This argument stretches the concept of a "single scheme" beyond its recognized limits as established in State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶16, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499.

While the State correctly identifies a general pattern of coercive behavior, the legal requirement for joinder demands a more specific connection than a shared modus operandi or a general "scheme of control." The connection asserted by the State—a "scheme of control"—is too attenuated to satisfy the statutory requirement.

The State's reliance on State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 985, 471 N.W.2d 24 (Ct. App. 1991), is unpersuasive. In Higginbotham, the court upheld joinder of robberies committed in a single evening with a consistent modus operandi. Here, the acts are separated by months, involve different victims (M.S. vs. V.G. for the intimidation counts), and occur in fundamentally different contexts—one in the home, the other from a jail cell.

The State's argument that Salinas used the same threats during the sexual assaults and the phone calls fails because the threats during the sexual assaults (to take the brother away) were qualitatively different from the threats in the jail calls (to kill himself and the child). More importantly, the purpose was different: during the sexual assaults, the threats were to force compliance with sexual acts; during the jail calls, the threats were to manipulate testimony regarding the domestic violence incident.

## III. REBUTTAL OF THE "LOGICAL NEXUS" ARGUMENT

The State argues that there is a "logical nexus" between the offenses. However, the record shows that the intimidation calls were specifically designed to influence the domestic violence sentencing, not to conceal sexual assaults that had not yet been reported.

The State claims Salinas "knew that if M.S. and V.G. testified truthfully about the domestic violence, it would expose his pattern of violence—including the sexual assaults." This is speculative and unsupported by the record. The sexual assaults had not been reported at the time of the calls. V.G. did not disclose the sexual assaults until May 13, 2010, which was after the sentencing and after the intimidation calls had ended. There is no evidence in the record that Salinas was aware that V.G. was contemplating disclosing sexual assault allegations.

The State's argument that the calls were "about preventing disclosure of all his abuse" ignores the explicit content of the calls, which focused exclusively on the domestic violence incident. Salinas asked M.S. to change her statement to say he did not try to strangle her and did not threaten to kill her. Salinas asked M.S. to convince V.G. to change her statement to say Salinas did not hit her. These requests relate entirely to the domestic violence charges, not to any sexual assault allegations.

## IV. REBUTTAL OF THE COMMON VICTIM ARGUMENT

The State argues that joinder is proper because V.G. is a common victim. However, joinder based solely on a common victim requires a "continuous course of conduct." State v. Kienzle, 2007 WI App 159, ¶18, 304 Wis. 2d 155, 736 N.W.2d 741.

The conduct here was not continuous. The sexual assaults ended on October 26, 2009, when Salinas was arrested. The intimidation of V.G. occurred months later, via jail calls, regarding her testimony in the domestic violence case. The fact that V.G. was a victim of both does not bridge the gap between the distinct criminal episodes.

The intimidation count involving V.G. was predicated on her witnessing the domestic violence, not on her status as a victim of sexual assault. The record reflects that V.G. was targeted in the intimidation because she was a witness to the domestic violence incident, not because she was a victim of sexual assault. Thus, the common victim factor does not establish the necessary logical nexus for joinder.

## V. THE ERROR WAS NOT HARMLESS

Even if joinder were arguably permissible, the defense has demonstrated that the error was not harmless. The trial involved the admission of extensive evidence regarding the domestic violence incident, including testimony that Salinas strangled M.S. and threatened to kill his four-year-old son with a knife. This evidence was highly inflammatory and irrelevant to the elements of the sexual assault charges.

The State argues that this evidence was admissible under State v. Doss, 116 Wis. 2d 374, 385, 342 N.W.2d 43 (1984), to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting. However, this argument fails because V.G. herself testified that she delayed reporting because she was "afraid and just embarrassed and ashamed," and because she did not want her mother to blame herself. She did not cite the jail calls or the domestic violence incident as the cause of her delay. The State cannot rely on Doss to admit evidence for a purpose the victim's own testimony does not support.

The prosecution's opening and closing arguments compounded the prejudice by weaving the domestic violence incident into a narrative of "pattern of violence" designed to persuade the jury that Salinas was a violent man who must have committed the sexual assaults. As recognized in State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶23, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499, limiting instructions are often insufficient to cure the prejudice of trying joined charges where the evidence of one crime is vastly more inflammatory than the other.

Given the vivid detail of the domestic violence testimony compared to V.G.'s "very little recall" of the specific sexual assaults, there is a significant probability that the jury convicted Salinas of the sexual assaults based on the inflammatory evidence of the domestic violence and intimidation, rather than on proof beyond a reasonable doubt of the sexual assault charges.

## CONCLUSION

The State has not met its burden to show that the intimidation charges and sexual assault charges were part of the same conduct or a single scheme or plan under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1). The charges were distinct criminal episodes separated by time and circumstance. The intimidation was a reaction to the arrest for domestic violence, not a continuation of a plan to commit sexual assault. Furthermore, the joinder resulted in substantial prejudice that was not harmless.

The defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals reversing the circuit court's joinder of the charges.

**STATE OF WISCONSIN v. LUIS SALINAS**

**CASE NO.** [INSERT CASE NO.]

**DECISION**

This matter is before the court on a petition for review of a court of appeals decision reversing the circuit court’s judgment of conviction. The State petitioned for review after the court of appeals held that the circuit court erred in joining the intimidation charges with the sexual assault charges and that the error was not harmless. See State v. Salinas, No. 2013AP2686, unpublished slip op. (Wis. Ct. App. Apr. 21, 2015). After considering the parties’ closing arguments and the record provided, we affirm the court of appeals.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

Whether joinder of charges is proper under Wis. Stat. § 971.12(1) presents a question of law that we review de novo. State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶16, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499. We also review de novo the application of the harmless error rule to the improper joinder. Id.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Joinder Was Improper**

Wisconsin Stat. § 971.12(1) provides that joinder is permitted when the charges "are based on the same conduct or on a series of acts connected together or constituting parts of a single scheme or plan." This provision must be narrowly construed to prevent the prejudice that results from trying a defendant on multiple, unrelated charges. State v. Williams, 2010 WI 7, ¶16, 322 Wis. 2d 755, 779 N.W.2d 499.

The State argues that the intimidation charges and the sexual assault charges constitute a "single scheme of coercive control." While the State identifies a general pattern of abusive behavior, the legal requirement for joinder demands a more specific connection than a shared modus operandi or a generalized "scheme of control." The record demonstrates that the sexual assault charges and the intimidation charges are distinct criminal episodes separated by time, location, and specific objective.

The sexual assaults occurred between 2006 and October 26, 2009, when Salinas was arrested for domestic violence. The intimidation charges stem from conduct occurring between October 2009 and May 2010, while Salinas was incarcerated. The objective of the intimidation calls was distinct from the objective of the sexual assaults. At the pre-trial conference, the State admitted that the intimidation calls were about "getting them to consider a better sentencing recommendation" in the domestic violence case. The transcripts confirm this; Salinas pressured M.S. and V.G. to change their statements regarding the domestic violence incident—the strangulation and the slap—not regarding any sexual assault. The sexual assaults had not been reported at the time of the calls, and there is no evidence Salinas knew they might be reported. Therefore, the calls cannot be viewed as part of a scheme to suppress the sexual assault allegations; rather, they were a discrete attempt to manipulate the sentencing for a separate crime.

The State’s reliance on State v. Higginbotham, 162 Wis. 2d 978, 471 N.W.2d 24 (Ct. App. 1991), is misplaced. In Higginbotham, the court upheld joinder of robberies committed in a single evening with a consistent modus operandi. Id. at 985. The connection asserted by the State—a "scheme of control"—is too attenuated to satisfy the statutory requirement.

The State further argues that joinder is proper because V.G. is a common victim. However, joinder based solely on a common victim requires a "continuous course of conduct." State v. Kienzle, 2007 WI App 159, ¶18, 304 Wis. 2d 155, 736 N.W.2d 741. The conduct here was not continuous. The sexual assaults ended on October 26, 2009, when Salinas was arrested. The intimidation of V.G. occurred months later, via jail calls, regarding her testimony in the domestic violence case. The intimidation count involving V.G. was predicated on her witnessing the domestic violence, not on her status as a victim of sexual assault. Thus, the common victim factor does not establish the necessary logical nexus for joinder.

Having determined that joinder was improper, we must consider whether the error was harmless. The State argues that the evidence of the domestic violence incident was admissible under State v. Doss, 116 Wis. 2d 374, 385, 342 N.W.2d 43 (1984), to explain V.G.'s delay in reporting the sexual assaults.
