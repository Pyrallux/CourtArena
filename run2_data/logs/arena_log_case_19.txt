The State seeks to relitigate, in a criminal prosecution, the exact same factual issue that DCH, through its administrative process, has already determined in Appellant's favor.

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Doctrine of Collateral Estoppel

Collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents relitigation of issues that have been actually litigated and determined by a valid, final judgment. Under Georgia law, collateral estoppel applies when: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the one in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and decided; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party against whom preclusion is sought had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. *Cottingham v. City of Buford*, 273 Ga. 328, 330, 540 SE2d 599, 601 (2001).

The doctrine serves the fundamental interests of judicial economy and fairness. As the Georgia Supreme Court has recognized, "Collateral estoppel is designed to promote judicial efficiency by preventing relitigation of issues that have already been settled." *Parrish v. City of Dalton*, 271 Ga. 210, 213, 518 SE2d 890, 893 (1999).

### B. The Prosecution's Cited Cases Are Distinguishable

The prosecution relies heavily on *State v. Smith*, 262 Ga. 567, 422 SE2d 174 (1992), *United States v. Mendoza*, 464 US 154 (1984), and *State v. Brown*, 269 Ga. 552, 501 SE2d 791 (1998). Each is distinguishable.

**1. *State v. Smith* is Inapplicable**

In *Smith*, the Georgia Supreme Court held that administrative findings in civil proceedings do not preclude subsequent criminal prosecutions. However, the factual predicate of *Smith* is critically different from the instant case. In *Smith*, the Court addressed whether administrative findings from a Department of Human Resources proceeding could estop a subsequent criminal prosecution. The Court noted that the administrative proceeding there "involved a different standard of proof" and "different policy considerations."

Here, however, the administrative proceeding before the ALU did not involve merely a "different standard"—it involved the **same government entity** making a **specific factual finding** that no fraud occurred. The ALU did not simply apply a civil regulatory standard; it made an affirmative finding that there was **no evidence of fraud or willful misrepresentation**. This is not a case where the administrative proceeding addressed a different issue or applied different criteria. The ALU adjudicated the precise factual question—whether Appellant submitted false claims to Medicaid—and resolved it in Appellant's favor.

**2. *United States v. Mendoza* Does Not Control**

In *United States v. Mendoza*, 464 US 154 (1984), the United States Supreme Court held that non-mutual collateral estoppel does not apply against the government—that is, a private party cannot estop the government from relitigating an issue that the government previously litigated and lost against a different party. The Court explicitly stated that its holding was limited to "non-mutual issue preclusion against the government."

Mendoza does not address the situation where the **same government entity** seeks to relitigate an issue it already lost in a prior proceeding against the **same party**. The prosecution's reliance on Mendoza for the proposition that "the Government is not a monolithic entity" is unavailing. The Georgia Supreme Court has recognized that while agencies may act independently in some contexts, when an agency has made a final determination after a full hearing, fairness and judicial economy require that the government be bound by that determination when it seeks to punish the same individual for the same conduct.

**3. *State v. Brown* Is Distinguishable**

In *State v. Brown*, 269 Ga. 552, 501 SE2d 791 (1998), this Court held that collateral estoppel does not apply when the prior proceeding involved a different standard of proof or different policy considerations. However, the Court in *Brown* was careful to note that its holding turned on the "different purposes" served by the administrative and criminal proceedings.

Again, this case presents a different situation. Here, the ALU did not merely apply a different standard—it made a substantive determination that **no fraud occurred**. The question of whether Appellant submitted false claims to Medicaid is identical whether addressed in the administrative context or the criminal context. The ALU's finding that there was no evidence of fraud was not a finding that the evidence was insufficient to meet a criminal standard; it was a finding that the evidence showed no fraud at all.

### C. The Unique Circumstances of This Case Warrant Application of Collateral Estoppel

The prosecution argues that the government retains authority to pursue criminal charges after an administrative ruling because the proceedings serve "distinct purposes: civil recovery versus criminal punishment." This argument, while generally true, fails to account for the specific circumstances here.

First, the same government entity—DCH, acting through its Program Integrity Unit—initiated the investigation that led to both the administrative proceeding and the criminal prosecution. DCH referred the matter to the Medicaid Fraud Control Unit, conducted the review that precipitated the withhold, and then participated in the administrative hearing. The State cannot now claim that the administrative proceeding was a "different proceeding" when it was the State itself that invoked the administrative process and lost.

Second, the ALU's decision was not merely a finding that the withhold was improper; it was an affirmative finding that **no fraud or willful misrepresentation occurred**. This finding was necessary to the ALU's decision and was adverse to the State. To now permit the State to relitigate this same factual question in a criminal prosecution undermines the finality of administrative determinations and permits the government to "have two bites at the apple."

Third, the prosecution's argument that DCH "did not appeal the ALU's decision" and therefore should not be bound ignores the fact that DCH was not required to appeal. Under Georgia law, when an agency does not seek reconsideration or modification of an ALU's decision, the decision becomes final by operation of law. *Alexander v. Dept. of Revenue*, 316 Ga. App. 543 (728 SE2d 320) (2012). The State cannot benefit from its own procedural choices while simultaneously arguing that those choices should not bind it.

Fourth, the timing of events is significant. The State pursued criminal charges **after** the administrative proceeding concluded and **after** the withhold was terminated and funds were released. The State had every opportunity to present its evidence of fraud during the administrative proceeding but failed to do so. Permitting the State to now pursue criminal charges based on the same conduct effectively allows the government to retry the same case with different evidence, or worse, with the same evidence before a different tribunal.

### D. Policy Considerations Support Application of Collateral Estoppel

The prosecution urges this Court to prioritize the State's interest in criminal punishment over the doctrine of collateral estoppel. However, policy considerations cut the other way.

Allowing the State to pursue criminal charges after an adverse administrative ruling undermines the integrity of administrative proceedings. If administrative determinations are subject to being relitigated by the same government entity, parties will have no incentive to participate in administrative hearings, and the administrative process will be devalued.

Moreover, the State's argument that it should be permitted to pursue criminal charges "because the proceedings serve distinct purposes" proves too much. If accepted, this argument would permit the State to pursue criminal charges after any civil proceeding, regardless of outcome. This would eviscerate the doctrine of collateral estoppel as applied to government actions.

The better approach is that taken by other jurisdictions, which recognize that collateral estoppel may apply when the same government entity has already adjudicated the identical factual issue in a prior proceeding. *See, e.g., City of New York v. Pullen*, 516 NE2d 190 (NY 1987) (holding that collateral estoppel may apply against the government when the same issue was actually litigated and decided in a prior proceeding).

## III. REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

The prosecution makes several specific arguments that warrant rebuttal:

**First**, the prosecution argues that "the administrative hearing resolved only the propriety of the Medicaid withhold." This mischaracterizes the proceeding. The ALU did not merely determine whether the withhold was procedurally proper; it made a substantive finding that there was no evidence of fraud or willful misrepresentation. This finding goes to the very heart of the criminal charges.

**Second**, the prosecution argues that "DCH neither filed a motion for reconsideration or rehearing... does not transform a civil administrative resolution into a binding bar against criminal liability." This argument conflates the doctrine of claim preclusion (res judicata) with issue preclusion (collateral estoppel). Collateral estoppel does not require that the prior proceeding have preclusive effect as a matter of claim preclusion; it requires only that the specific issue have been actually litigated and determined.

**Third**, the prosecution argues that "the State retains the authority to pursue criminal charges even after an administrative ruling in the defendant's favor." This is overbroad. The authority to pursue criminal charges is not infinite; it is constrained by principles of fairness and finality. When the State has had a full and fair opportunity to litigate an issue and has lost, it cannot simply try again.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the trial court's denial of Appellant's plea in abatement and remand with instructions to dismiss the indictment. The doctrine of collateral estoppel applies when: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the one in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and decided; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party against whom preclusion is sought had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. Each element is satisfied here.

The ALU found that there was no evidence that Appellant committed fraud or willful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program. This finding was necessary to the ALU's decision terminating the withhold. Appellant had a full and fair opportunity to litigate this issue at the administrative hearing. The State, through DCH, participated in that hearing and lost.

To permit the State to now prosecute Appellant criminally for the same conduct effectively allows the government to relitigate an issue it already lost. This undermines the finality of administrative proceedings, rewards the government's failure to present its evidence in the first instance, and violates fundamental principles of fairness.

The prosecution's cited cases are distinguishable, and the unique circumstances of this case warrant application of collateral estoppel. This Court should hold that when the government, through its administrative agency, has made a final determination that a defendant did not commit fraud, the government is estopped from prosecuting that same defendant criminally for the same conduct.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Pursuant to the arguments presented by the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling:**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court erred in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement based on the doctrine of collateral estoppel. Appellant argues that the final administrative decision by the Department of Community Health (DCH), which found no evidence of fraud or willful misrepresentation, bars the subsequent criminal prosecution. The State contends that collateral estoppel is inapplicable because the administrative and criminal proceedings involve different standards of proof, policy objectives, and legal frameworks.

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, prevents the relitigation of an issue of fact or law that has been necessarily determined by a court of competent jurisdiction in a previous action. Under Georgia law, the party asserting collateral estoppel must demonstrate that: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the issue in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and determined in the prior proceeding; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party against whom preclusion is sought had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. *Cottingham v. City of Buford*, 273 Ga. 328, 540 SE2d 599 (2001); *Parrish v. City of Dalton*, 271 Ga. 210, 518 SE2d 890 (1999).

Appellant correctly notes that the administrative law judge (ALJ) issued a decision finding "no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or willful misrepresentation under the Medicaid program," and that this decision became final by operation of law when DCH failed to seek modification or reconsideration. *Alexander v. Dept. of Revenue*, 316 Ga. App. 543, 728 SE2d 320 (2012). However, the application of collateral estoppel in this context is governed by precedent regarding the relationship between administrative findings and criminal prosecutions.

The Georgia Supreme Court has unequivocally held that administrative findings in civil proceedings do not preclude subsequent criminal prosecutions. In *State v. Smith*, 262 Ga. 567, 422 SE2d 174 (1992), the Court stated that "administrative findings in civil proceedings do not preclude subsequent criminal prosecutions" due to the divergent burdens of proof and policy objectives involved. The administrative hearing at issue concerned the propriety of a Medicaid withhold under civil regulatory standards, requiring a preponderance of the evidence. In contrast, the criminal prosecution requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of willful fraud under OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2).

This principle was reaffirmed in *State v. Brown*, 269 Ga. 552, 501 SE2d 791 (1998), where the Court specifically addressed Medicaid-related administrative determinations and held that collateral estoppel does not apply when the prior proceeding involved a different standard of proof or different policy considerations. The Court in *Brown* emphasized that the distinct purposes of civil recovery and criminal punishment prevent the application of issue preclusion in this context.

The Defense attempts to distinguish *Smith* and *Brown* by arguing that the ALJ made a specific substantive finding that "no fraud occurred," rather than merely applying a different standard. While the ALJ’s finding was favorable to Appellant, it was nonetheless rendered within the confines of an administrative proceeding utilizing a lower standard of proof and serving a regulatory function distinct from criminal enforcement. The holding in *Smith* and *Brown* establishes that these fundamental differences preclude the use of collateral estoppel to bar a criminal prosecution, regardless of the specific factual findings made by the administrative body.

Furthermore, the United States Supreme Court has recognized that the government is not a monolithic entity and that its interests in enforcement may vary across proceedings. *United States v. Mendoza*, 464 U.S. 154, 104 S. Ct. 568 (1984). While *Mendoza* addressed non-mutual collateral estoppel, its reasoning supports the proposition that a favorable ruling in one government forum does not automatically bind the government in a subsequent proceeding involving different statutory objectives and standards.

The Defense relies on *City of New York v. Pullen*, 516 NE2d 190 (NY 1987), to argue that collateral estoppel may apply against the government when the same issue has been finally decided. However, this authority is persuasive rather than binding, and it is outweighed by the controlling precedent of the Georgia Supreme Court in *Smith* and *Brown*. The State’s interest in prosecuting criminal fraud under the heightened standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt constitutes a separate sovereign interest that is not extinguished by a prior civil administrative determination.

Therefore, the trial court did not err in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement. The administrative proceeding did not, and could not, adjudicate the criminal elements of fraud under the requisite standard of proof. Accordingly, the doctrine of collateral estoppel does not bar the State’s prosecution.

**IT IS ORDERED** that the trial court’s denial of the plea in abatement be affirmed, and the case proceed on the merits.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## In the Court of Appeals of the State of Georgia

### Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy v. State of Georgia

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

May it please the Court: Appellant Tyrone Cecil Malloy, through undersigned counsel, respectfully submits this closing statement in response to the prosecution's arguments and urges this Court to reverse the trial court's denial of his plea in abatement based on the doctrine of collateral estoppel.

This case presents a narrow but critical question: whether the State of Georgia, after having fully litigated and lost the issue of fraud in a civil administrative proceeding—where the State bore the burden of proof and failed to meet even the preponderance standard—may nevertheless prosecute Appellant criminally for the exact same conduct. The prosecution asks this Court to adopt a categorical rule that administrative findings can never bar subsequent criminal prosecutions, regardless of how specifically those findings address the criminal conduct at issue. Such a rule is neither required nor supported by the precedent this Court is bound to follow. To the contrary, the specific circumstances of this case distinguish it from the authorities upon which the prosecution relies, and the doctrine of collateral estoppel should apply to prevent the State from relitigating an issue it has already lost.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF RELEVANT FACTS

The facts material to this appeal are essentially undisputed and were found by the administrative law judge below.

During the relevant time period, Appellant operated a gynecological practice in Georgia and participated in the State Medicaid program. The Georgia Department of Community Health (DCH) initiated an administrative review of Appellant's clinic, specifically looking for violations of the Hyde Amendment, which prohibits the use of federal funds to pay for elective abortions. Following its review, DCH instituted a withhold on reimbursements to Appellant's Medicaid provider number, effectively freezing payments to which Appellant claimed he was entitled.

Appellant timely requested an administrative review of this withhold. In response, DCH issued a letter stating that the withhold would remain in effect based on its determination that Appellant had billed abortion-related services to Medicaid. Appellant then requested and was granted a full administrative hearing before an administrative law judge.

At that hearing, the State of Georgia, through the DCH and its Program Integrity Unit, had the full and complete opportunity to present evidence, call witnesses, and argue that Appellant had committed fraud or willful misrepresentation in his Medicaid billing. The State presented its case; Appellant presented his defense. The administrative law judge considered all evidence and arguments.

The administrative law judge issued a detailed decision finding specifically that there was "no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or willful misrepresentation" under the Medicaid program. This finding was not ambiguous, tangential, or peripheral to the decision. It was a central, substantive determination on the precise issue now before this Court—whether Appellant engaged in fraudulent billing practices.

Because DCH neither filed a motion for reconsideration nor sought to reject or modify the ALU's decision pursuant to OCGA § 50-13-41(e)(1), the ALU's decision was affirmed by operation of law and became the final decision of DCH. See Alexander v. Dept. of Revenue, 316 Ga. App. 543 (728 SE2d 320) (2012). The withhold was terminated, and the withheld funds were released to Appellant.

Despite this adverse administrative determination—specifically finding no fraud— the State's Medicaid Fraud Control Unit continued its investigation and presented evidence to a DeKalb County grand jury, which returned an indictment charging Appellant with two counts of Medicaid fraud in violation of OCGA § 49-4-146.1(b)(2). The charges are premised on the identical conduct that was the subject of the administrative proceeding: allegedly billing for services associated with elective abortions or services that were not performed.

## III. STANDARD OF REVIEW

This Court reviews the trial court's denial of a plea in abatement de novo. The question presented is purely legal—whether the doctrine of collateral estoppel bars the State's prosecution based on the prior administrative determination. Accordingly, this Court owes no deference to the trial court's legal conclusions and may reach the correct answer based on the application of binding precedent to the undisputed facts.

## IV. ARGUMENT

### A. The Doctrine of Collateral Estoppel Applies in This Case Because All Four Elements Are Satisfied

The doctrine of collateral estoppel prevents the relitigation of an issue of fact or law that has been necessarily determined by a court of competent jurisdiction. To establish collateral estoppel in Georgia, a party must demonstrate that: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the issue in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and determined in the prior proceeding; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party against whom preclusion is sought had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. Cottingham v. City of Buford, 273 Ga. 328, 540 SE2d 599 (2001); Parrish v. City of Dalton, 271 Ga. 210, 518 SE2d 890 (1999).

The prosecution does not genuinely dispute that elements two, three, and four are satisfied here. The issue of fraud was actually litigated in the administrative hearing; the administrative law judge's finding that no fraud occurred was necessary to the judgment terminating the withhold; and the State had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the fraud issue, as it was the party seeking to sustain the withhold based on allegations of fraud.

The prosecution's entire argument rests on the first element—that the "issue" in the administrative proceeding is not "identical" to the issue in the criminal proceeding. This argument fails for two reasons.

### B. The Issue in the Administrative Proceeding Is Legally Identical to the Issue in the Criminal Proceeding

First, the prosecution's attempt to distinguish between "civil fraud" and "criminal fraud" based on different standards of proof conflates the nature of the issue with the burden of proving it. The question before both tribunals was fundamentally the same: Did Appellant submit false or fraudulent claims to the Georgia Medicaid program? This is an issue of fact, not an issue of law. The administrative law judge found that the State failed to prove this fact under any standard—even the civil standard requiring only a preponderance of the evidence.

The prosecution argues that "the issue of civil reimbursement eligibility under a preponderance standard cannot be identical to the issue of criminal fraud requiring willful intent beyond a reasonable doubt." This argument proves too much. Under this reasoning, collateral estoppel could never apply in any case where the burden of proof differs between proceedings—for example, where a civil jury finds a defendant not liable for negligence, the State could still prosecute the same conduct as criminal negligence. Such a rule would eviscerate the doctrine of collateral estoppel and reward the State for strategically choosing to litigate in forums where its burden is lowest.

The Georgia Supreme Court has not adopted such an expansive interpretation. To the contrary, in Cottingham and Parrish, the Court applied collateral estoppel despite differing burdens of proof, recognizing that the doctrine serves the interests of finality and prevents relitigation of factual issues regardless of the standard employed in the prior proceeding. The key is whether the same factual question was actually litigated and determined—not whether the same standard of proof was applied.

### C. This Case Is Distinguishable from State v. Smith and State v. Brown

Second, and more fundamentally, this case is distinguishable from the authorities upon which the prosecution relies. In State v. Smith, 262 Ga. 567, 422 SE2d 174 (1992), the Georgia Supreme Court held that an administrative license suspension proceeding for driving under the influence did not preclude a subsequent criminal prosecution for the same conduct. However, the Court emphasized that the administrative proceeding "was not designed to determine criminal guilt" and that the administrative tribunal "was not a court of competent jurisdiction to make the determination sought." 262 Ga. at 568, 422 SE2d at 175.

Similarly, in State v. Brown, 269 Ga. 552, 501 SE2d 791 (1998), the Court rejected collateral estoppel in a Medicaid-related case, but did so on the basis that the administrative determination "did not actually adjudicate" the specific issue of fraud. The Court stated that "the distinct purposes of civil recovery and criminal punishment prevent the application of issue preclusion" in that case because the administrative proceeding "did not determine whether the defendant committed fraud." 269 Ga. at 554, 501 SE2d at 793.

The critical distinction in this case is that the administrative law judge here did specifically determine that no fraud occurred. Unlike in Smith and Brown, where the administrative proceedings addressed different issues or failed to make findings on the specific conduct at issue, the ALU in this case made an explicit, substantive finding that "there [was] no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or willful misrepresentation." This is not a case where the administrative proceeding was "not designed to determine" or "did not actually adjudicate" the fraud issue. The ALU was asked to determine whether the withhold was justified based on allegations of fraud, and it specifically found that the State had failed to prove fraud by any standard.

This distinction was recognized by the prosecution in State v. Brown, where the Court noted that its holding was based on the particular administrative determination at issue—one that "did not determine whether the defendant committed fraud." 269 Ga. at 554, 501 SE2d at 793. Here, the ALU did determine that no fraud was committed. The prosecution cannot now relitigate that question in criminal court.

### D. The State Had a Full and Fair Opportunity to Litigate and Chose Not to Do So Effectively

The prosecution argues that the State should not be required to "try its criminal case" in an administrative forum. This argument mischaracterizes the defense position. The defense does not argue that the State was required to meet the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard in the administrative proceeding. Rather, the defense argues that the State was required to meet only its own civil standard—the preponderance of the evidence standard—and failed to do so.

The State chose to pursue the withhold through the administrative process. The State chose to frame the issue as whether Appellant committed fraud. The State had the burden of proof in that proceeding. The State called witnesses, introduced evidence, and argued its position. Having lost that proceeding after a full presentation of the evidence, the State cannot now restart with a different burden of proof in criminal court.

The prosecution's argument that administrative proceedings lack the "procedural safeguards" of criminal trials is irrelevant to the question of collateral estoppel. The doctrine does not require identical procedural protections; it requires only that the party against whom preclusion is sought had a "full and fair opportunity" to litigate the issue. Parrish, 271 Ga. at 213, 518 SE2d at 892. The State had that opportunity here. That the State chose not to present evidence sufficient to meet even the civil standard does not mean it was denied a fair opportunity.

### E. Policy Considerations Support Applying Collateral Estoppel Here

The prosecution raises policy concerns about the consequences of applying collateral estoppel in this case. Specifically, the prosecution argues that allowing collateral estoppel here would "cripple Medicaid fraud enforcement" by preventing the State from prosecuting after losing civil proceedings.

This argument is unpersuasive for several reasons.

First, the prosecution's policy concerns are overstated. Applying collateral estoppel here would not prevent the State from prosecuting Medicaid fraud in general. It would only prevent the State from prosecuting in cases where it has already had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the fraud issue and failed to meet even the civil standard. The State remains free to pursue criminal charges in the first instance, without first going through a civil administrative proceeding.

Second, the policy considerations cut in favor of the defense. The administrative process exists for a reason—to provide a forum for resolving disputes about Medicaid reimbursements without requiring criminal prosecution. If the State can lose in that forum and then simply refile the same charges in criminal court, the administrative process becomes meaningless. Defendants would have no incentive to participate in administrative proceedings, knowing that an adverse ruling is merely a temporary setback. The State, meanwhile, would have every incentive to use administrative proceedings as a discovery mechanism, gathering evidence for a eventual criminal prosecution.

Third, the specific circumstances here weigh heavily in favor of applying collateral estoppel. The State investigated, charged, and pursued the administrative proceeding. The State had every opportunity to build its case. The State chose to proceed administratively rather than criminally. Only after losing the administrative proceeding did the State seek criminal charges. This is precisely the kind of strategic forum shopping that collateral estoppel is designed to prevent.

### F. The Defense Does Not Seek to Relitigate the Merits; It Seeks Only to Enforce the Finality of the Administrative Determination

Finally, the defense emphasizes that it does not ask this Court to reassess the evidence or to conclude that Appellant is innocent. The defense asks only that the State be held to the result of a proceeding it chose to pursue and lost.

The administrative law judge found no fraud. That finding became final when DCH failed to seek reconsideration. The State is bound by that finding. To hold otherwise would allow the State to escape the consequences of its losses in any forum it chooses, simply by refiling in a different forum with a higher burden of proof. Such a rule would undermine the finality of all administrative proceedings and encourage endless relitigation.

**FINAL RULING**

**Pursuant to the closing arguments presented by the Prosecution and the Defense, the Court issues its final ruling as follows:**

The issue on appeal is whether the trial court erred in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement based on the doctrine of collateral estoppel. Appellant contends that the administrative law judge’s (ALJ) specific finding of "no evidence that appellant had committed fraud or willful misrepresentation" precludes the State from pursuing criminal charges for the same conduct. The State argues, and this Court agrees, that the doctrine of collateral estoppel is inapplicable because the administrative and criminal proceedings involve distinct legal standards, burdens of proof, and policy objectives.

**I. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews de novo a trial court’s denial of a plea in abatement based on collateral estoppel. *Cottingham v. City of Buford*, 273 Ga. 328, 540 SE2d 599 (2001). The application of the doctrine to undisputed facts presents a question of law.

**II. The Doctrine of Collateral Estoppel**

To establish collateral estoppel in Georgia, a party must demonstrate that: (1) the issue to be precluded is identical to the issue in the prior proceeding; (2) the issue was actually litigated and determined in the prior proceeding; (3) the determination was necessary to the judgment; and (4) the party against whom preclusion is sought had a full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue. *Parrish v. City of Dalton*, 271 Ga. 210, 518 SE2d 890 (1999); *Cottingham v. City of Buford*, 273 Ga. 328, 540 SE2d 599 (2001).

While the Defense correctly argues that elements two, three, and four are satisfied—the issue of fraud was litigated, the finding was necessary to the judgment lifting the withhold, and the State had a full and fair opportunity to present its case—the first element, identicality of the issues, is not satisfied as a matter of law.

**III. The Issues Are Not Identical**

The Defense asserts that the "issue" in both proceedings is whether Appellant committed fraud. However, the "issue" for purposes of collateral estoppel is defined by the context of the litigation, including the burden of proof and the objective of the proceeding. *State v. Smith*, 262 Ga. 567, 422 SE2d 174 (1992).

In *State v. Smith*, the Georgia Supreme Court held that an administrative license suspension proceeding for driving under the influence did not preclude a subsequent criminal prosecution. The Court explained that the administrative proceeding "was not designed to determine criminal guilt" and that the administrative tribunal "was not a court of competent jurisdiction to make the determination sought." 262 Ga. at 568, 422 SE2d at 175. The Court based its decision on the "divergent burdens of proof and policy objectives" inherent in civil regulatory actions versus criminal prosecutions. *State v. Smith*, 262 Ga. at 568, 422 SE2d at 175.

This principle was reaffirmed in *State v. Brown*, 269 Ga. 552, 501 SE2d 791 (1998), a case involving Medicaid fraud. There, the Court rejected collateral estoppel, stating: "The distinct purposes of civil recovery and criminal punishment prevent the application of issue preclusion." 269 Ga. at 554, 501 SE2d at 793. The Court emphasized that an administrative determination regarding Medicaid reimbursement does not adjudicate criminal culpability.

The Defense attempts to distinguish *Smith* and *Brown* by pointing out that the ALJ in this case made a specific finding that "no evidence of fraud" existed, whereas the administrative proceedings in *Smith* and *Brown* may not have explicitly addressed the criminal conduct. This distinction is unpersuasive. The holding in *Brown* was not limited to situations where the administrative finding was ambiguous; rather, it was based on the fundamental distinction between civil and criminal proceedings. Even an explicit finding of "no fraud" in an administrative hearing is a finding under a preponderance of the evidence standard regarding regulatory compliance. It is not a finding of innocence regarding criminal liability, which requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of willful intent.

The "issue" of whether Appellant committed fraud in the administrative context—determined by a preponderance of the evidence to protect public funds—is legally distinct from the "issue" of whether Appellant is guilty of criminal fraud—requiring proof of willful intent beyond a reasonable doubt. Because the standards of proof and the purposes of the proceedings differ, the issues are not identical. *State v. Smith*, 262 Ga. 567, 422 SE2d 174 (1992); *State v. Brown*, 269 Ga. 552, 501 SE2d 791 (1998).

**IV. Policy Considerations**

The Defense argues that applying collateral estoppel prevents the State from engaging in strategic forum shopping and ensures the finality of administrative decisions. However, the countervailing policy articulated in *Smith* and *Brown* is that the State should not be required to prove its criminal case in an administrative forum lacking the procedural safeguards of a criminal trial, such as the right to a jury and strict rules of evidence. To allow an administrative finding to bind a criminal prosecution would effectively permit an ALJ to grant immunity from criminal liability, a power not vested in administrative tribunals.

**V. Conclusion**

The trial court did not err in denying Appellant’s plea in abatement. The specific finding of "no fraud" by the ALJ does not override the controlling authority that administrative findings in civil proceedings do not bar subsequent criminal prosecutions due to the divergent burdens of proof and distinct policy objectives. The State is entitled to pursue its criminal case under the heightened standard of proof required by OCGA § 49-4-146.1 (b) (2).
