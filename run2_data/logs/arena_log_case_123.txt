# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Washington Supreme Court

### State of Washington, Respondent v. John George and Tommy George, Appellants

---

## INTRODUCTION

Appellants John George and Tommy George respectfully submit this opening response to rebut the State's arguments and demonstrate that the Court of Appeals erred in affirming their convictions for attempted first-degree theft by deception. The defense contends that: (1) the evidence was insufficient to establish the market value of the truck beyond a reasonable doubt; (2) the State's reliance on purchase price as conclusive evidence of market value misapprehends the applicable legal standard; and (3) the theft statute, as applied, fails to provide fair notice of what conduct is prohibited. The convictions should be reversed.

---

## I. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH MARKET VALUE

### A. The Legal Standard for Sufficiency of Evidence

It is well-established that the State bears the burden of proving every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. In **State v. Green**, 89 Wash. 2d 554, 558, 574 P.2d 1171 (1978), this Court held that "the due process clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects an accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged." The evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the prosecution, but the prosecution cannot rely on mere speculation or inference to establish essential elements. **State v. Randhawa**, 133 Wash. 2d 67, 79, 958 P.2d 327 (1998).

### B. The State Failed to Prove Market Value

The critical deficiency in the State's case lies in its failure to establish the actual market value of the 1974 Chevrolet truck. The prosecution's entire case rested on the $1,800 purchase price, which the State contends is sufficient to establish market value. This reasoning is fundamentally flawed.

As this Court recognized in **State v. Charley**, 54 Wash. 2d 422, 426, 341 P.2d 476 (1959), "market value" is not synonymous with "sale price." The market value of an item is the price that a willing buyer would pay a willing seller, neither being under compulsion to buy or sell. The mere fact that a transaction occurred does not establish that the price reflected true market value, particularly where, as here, the buyer may have been deceived about the property's condition.

The facts demonstrate that the Georges purchased the truck from Jerome Potter after Potter disclosed significant problems: the rear wheel differential was inoperable, the truck had sat outside for two years, and Potter had replaced the original engine. These disclosures establish that the $1,800 price reflected Potter's assessment of a vehicle with substantial defects—not its fair market value in working condition. The State presented no independent evidence of what a willing buyer would have paid for the truck in its actual condition.

### C. The State v. Lui Case Does Not Support the Prosecution's Position

The State misreads **State v. Lui**, 179 Wash. 2d 457, 315 P.3d 493 (2013). In that case, this Court held that sale price is "relevant evidence" of market value—not that it is conclusive proof. The Court in Lui specifically noted that the sale price "may be considered" alongside other factors. Here, however, the State presented no other evidence of value and asked the Court to treat the purchase price as dispositive.

Moreover, the circumstances in Lui are distinguishable. In Lui, there was no claim that the seller had misrepresented the property's condition. Here, by contrast, the very basis of the theft charge is that the Georges allegedly deceived Potter about the truck's condition to pay less than its value. If the Georges are correct that they deceived Potter, then the purchase price was artificially low—not reflective of market value.

---

## II. THE PROSECUTION FAILED TO ESTABLISH THE ELEMENTS OF FIRST-DEGREE THEFT

### A. The Degree of Theft Depends on Actual Market Value

Under RCW 9A.56.010(21), "value" means "the market value of the property or services at the time and place of the crime." For first-degree theft, the property must exceed $1,000 in value. RCW 9A.56.030. For second-degree theft, the property must exceed $250 but be worth $1,000 or less. RCW 9A.56.040.

The State presented no evidence of the truck's market value at the time of the theft. The fact that the Georges paid $1,800 establishes only what they paid—not what the truck was worth. Given the extensive evidence of the truck's poor condition—mechanical problems, two years of exposure to the elements, engine replacement, and actual mileage of 185,000 miles—there is no basis for concluding that $1,800 reflected fair market value.

### B. The Georges' Improvements Do Not Establish Value

The State argues that the Georges' post-purchase improvements are irrelevant. This is correct, but the State's reasoning proves too much. If post-purchase improvements are irrelevant to determining value at the time of the theft, then the purchase price itself is equally irrelevant unless the State establishes that the price reflected fair market value.

The record establishes that the Georges made improvements to the truck after purchasing it. These improvements do not establish the truck's value at the time of the theft, but they do establish that the truck required significant work—further supporting the conclusion that the purchase price did not reflect fair market value.

---

## III. THE THEFT STATUTE IS VAGUE AS APPLIED

### A. The Constitutional Requirement

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires that criminal statutes give fair notice of prohibited conduct. A statute is void for vagueness if it "fails to provide a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited, or is so standardless that it invites arbitrary enforcement." **State v. Hennings**, 129 Wash. 2d 512, 527, 919 P.2d 580 (1996).

### B. The Statute Fails Here

The Georges were charged with attempted first-degree theft based on allegations that they deceived Potter about the truck's value. Yet the State never established what the truck's actual market value was. The prosecution's theory—that the purchase price equals market value—creates an impossible standard for defendants to understand.

A person of ordinary intelligence cannot determine whether their conduct constitutes first-degree theft, second-degree theft, or no theft at all, when the determination depends on establishing market value through evidence that the State itself acknowledges is merely "relevant" rather than conclusive. The Georges had no way of knowing whether they had committed a felony or a misdemeanor, because the statute provides no guidance on how value should be calculated when the purchase price is the only evidence available.

In **State v. Brown**, 121 Wash. 2d 283, 850 P.2d 310 (1993), this Court held that "market value" is not vague because it is a "well-established legal concept." However, the State's application of that concept here renders the statute impermissibly vague. The State cannot simultaneously argue that market value is established by the purchase price (making it easy to prove) while also claiming that the statute provides fair notice (requiring defendants to know what a willing buyer would pay). These positions are inconsistent.

---

## IV. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED

The Court of Appeals erred in affirming the convictions because the evidence was insufficient to establish the market value of the truck. The State's reliance on the purchase price, without more, failed to prove that the truck was worth more than $1,000—the threshold for first-degree theft.

In **State v. Salinas**, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 829 P.2d 1068 (1992), this Court held that evidence is sufficient if any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt. However, the Salinas standard requires some evidence from which the trier of fact could make that finding. Here, the State presented no evidence from which a rational jury could determine the truck's market value—the only evidence was the purchase price, which does not establish market value as a matter of law.

The trial court erred in denying the Georges' motion for dismissal, and the Court of Appeals compounded that error by applying the wrong legal standard.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the Court of Appeals' decision and dismiss the attempted first-degree theft convictions. The evidence was insufficient to establish the market value of the truck, the State's reliance on purchase price as conclusive evidence of market value misapprehends the applicable legal standard, and the statute, as applied, fails to provide fair notice of prohibited conduct.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**State of Washington, Respondent v. John George and Tommy George, Appellants.**

**PENDING BEFORE THE COURT** is the appeal of Appellants John George and Tommy George following their convictions for attempted first degree theft by deception. The Court has reviewed the opening arguments of the Prosecution and the Defense, the record provided, and the applicable legal standards. Based solely on the facts and arguments presented, the Court issues the following preliminary ruling.

**I. ANALYSIS OF SUFFICIENCY OF EVIDENCE**

The primary issue on appeal is whether the State presented sufficient evidence to support the element of value required for first degree theft. The Appellants contend that the State failed to prove the market value of the truck at the time and place of the crime, relying instead on the purchase price paid for the truck in a different condition.

The legal standard for sufficiency of evidence is well established. In *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 829 P.2d 1068 (1992), this Court held that evidence is sufficient if any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. Furthermore, the State bears the burden of proving every element of the crime, including the value of the property, beyond a reasonable doubt. *State v. Green*, 89 Wash. 2d 554, 574 P.2d 1171 (1978).

The value of property for theft purposes is defined as the market value at the time and place of the crime. RCW 9A.56.010(21). In *State v. Lui*, 179 Wash. 2d 457, 315 P.3d 493 (2013), this Court confirmed that the sale price of property is relevant evidence of its market value, particularly when no contrary evidence is presented. The State presented evidence that the Appellants purchased the truck for $1,800. While the Appellants argue, citing *State v. Charley*, 54 Wash. 2d 422, 341 P.2d 476 (1959), that sale price is not synonymous with market value, *Charley* does not preclude the jury from considering sale price as evidence of value. It merely defines market value as the price a willing buyer would pay a willing seller.

Here, the evidence established that the Appellants purchased the truck for $1,800. The Appellants argue that this price reflected a truck with substantial defects and therefore did not represent the "fair market value" of the truck in its improved condition at the time of the attempted sale to the undercover officers. However, this argument inadvertently supports the conviction. The Appellants themselves alleged that they made improvements to the truck after purchasing it. If the market value of the truck in its defective state was $1,800, the value of the truck in its improved condition at the time of the attempted sale must have been equal to or greater than $1,800.

The State was required to prove that the value of the property exceeded $1,000 to sustain a conviction for first degree theft. RCW 9A.56.030. By establishing that the truck was worth $1,800 in its broken, unimproved state, the State necessarily established that the value exceeded the $1,000 threshold. The Appellants presented no evidence to rebut the $1,800 figure or to suggest that the value of the improved truck had somehow fallen below the statutory threshold. Therefore, a rational trier of fact could conclude that the value of the truck at the time of the crime exceeded $1,000. *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 829 P.2d 1068 (1992).

**II. ANALYSIS OF STATUTORY VAGUENESS**

The Appellants argue that the theft statute is unconstitutionally vague as applied because the State's theory—that purchase price equals market value—fails to provide fair notice of prohibited conduct. This argument is unpersuasive.

In *State v. Brown*, 121 Wash. 2d 283, 850 P.2d 310 (1993), this Court held that the term "market value" is a well-established legal concept that provides fair notice of prohibited conduct and is not unconstitutionally vague. The fact that the State may use purchase price as evidence of market value does not render the statute vague; it simply speaks to the method of proof. The statute clearly defines the prohibited conduct (theft) and the element of value (market value). The Appellants' inability to predict the exact evidentiary path the State would use to prove that element does not render the statute void for vagueness under the standard set forth in *State v. Hennings*, 129 Wash. 2d 512, 919 P.2d 580 (1996).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The State presented sufficient evidence for a rational jury to find that the value of the truck exceeded $1,000. The purchase price of $1,800 was admissible evidence of market value, and the Appellants' admission of improvements precludes any argument that the value fell below the statutory threshold for first degree theft. The theft statute is not unconstitutionally vague.

Accordingly, the Court affirms the decision of the Court of Appeals.

# DEFENSE REBUTTAL

## IN THE SUPREME COURT OF WASHINGTON

### State of Washington, Respondent v. John George and Tommy George, Appellants

---

**DEFENSE REBUTTAL TO PROSECUTION'S REBUTTAL**

---

## I. INTRODUCTION

The prosecution's rebuttal mischaracterizes the applicable legal standards and ignores fundamental flaws in the State's theory of value. The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the convictions because the State failed to present sufficient evidence that the truck's market value exceeded $1,000 at the time of the attempted sale, and because the theft statute as applied failed to provide fair notice of the conduct prohibited.

---

## II. THE PROSECUTION'S REBUTTAL FAILS TO ESTABLISH SUFFICIENT EVIDENCE OF VALUE

### A. The Purchase Price of the Defective Truck Does Not Establish the Value of the Improved Truck at the Time of the Crime

The prosecution erroneously conflates the purchase price of a defective vehicle with the market value of an improved vehicle at the time of the attempted sale. This conflation violates the fundamental principle established in *State v. Charley*, 54 Wash. 2d 422, 341 P.2d 476 (1959), that market value must be determined "at the time and place of the crime."

The facts demonstrate the fatal flaw in the prosecution's theory. The Georges purchased the truck in June 2003 for $1,800 in its defective condition: the rear wheel differential was inoperable, the truck had sat outside for two years, and the actual mileage was 185,000 miles. The attempted sale to the undercover officers occurred sometime thereafter. The prosecution presented no evidence whatsoever regarding the condition of the truck at the time of the attempted sale, the nature or extent of the improvements made, or the market value of the truck in its improved condition.

The prosecution's argument that "the $1,800 price for the defective truck alone exceeds the $1,000 threshold" fundamentally misapprehends the crime of theft. The Georges were not charged with attempting to sell the truck in its defective condition for $1,800—they were charged with attempting to sell the truck to the undercover officers based on false representations about the truck's condition and mileage. The relevant inquiry is the market value of the truck *as it was represented and as it existed at the time of the attempted sale*, not the price paid for a different, defective version of the truck months earlier.

### B. The Prosecution's Misreading of Lui Does Not Support the Conviction

The prosecution relies heavily on *State v. Lui*, 179 Wash. 2d 457, 315 P.3d 493 (2013), for the proposition that "sale price is relevant evidence of market value." However, the prosecution ignores the critical distinction between *relevant evidence* and *sufficient evidence*. While sale price may be considered as evidence of market value, it is not *dispositive* of market value, particularly when the property's condition has materially changed between the sale and the crime.

In *Lui*, the court permitted the jury to consider sale price as evidence of market value because "no contrary evidence was presented." However, *Lui* does not stand for the proposition that sale price alone, without any evidence connecting the sale price to the condition of the property at the time of the crime, is sufficient to establish market value beyond a reasonable doubt. Here, the prosecution presented absolutely no evidence regarding the condition of the truck at the time of the attempted sale or the relationship between the $1,800 purchase price and the truck's value in its improved condition.

### C. The Georges' Alleged Improvements Do Not Establish Value—They Raise Questions About Value That the State Failed to Answer

The prosecution's assertion that "the Georges' admission of improvements...confirms the truck had value exceeding $1,000" represents a logical fallacy. The admission that improvements were made does not establish the *value* of those improvements or the resulting market value of the improved truck. Improvements may add value, but they may also add less value than their cost, or the truck may have deteriorated despite the improvements.

The burden of proof rests entirely on the State, not on the defendants. *State v. Green*, 89 Wash. 2d 554, 574 P.2d 1171 (1978). The Georges had no obligation to present evidence regarding the value of the truck in its improved condition. The prosecution's attempt to shift this burden by noting that "the Georges presented no evidence to rebut the purchase price" misapprehends the State's constitutional burden to prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970).

The prosecution's theory effectively requires defendants to prove their innocence by presenting evidence that their property is worth less than the statutory threshold—a burden-shifting mechanism expressly rejected by Washington law. The State was required to prove, and failed to prove, that the truck as it existed at the time of the attempted sale had a market value exceeding $1,000.

### D. The Prosecution Ignores the Distinction Between Purchase Price and Market Value

As this Court recognized in *State v. Charley*, 54 Wash. 2d 422, 341 P.2d 476 (1959), "market value" is defined as "the price a willing buyer would pay a willing seller" at the time and place of the crime. The prosecution conflates sale price with market value, but these are distinct concepts. The sale price of $1,800 reflected a specific transaction between a willing buyer and a willing seller *for a truck in its defective condition*. The prosecution presented no evidence of what a willing buyer would pay a willing seller for the truck *in its improved condition* at the time of the attempted sale.

The prosecution's reliance on the absence of contrary evidence presented by the defense is misplaced. The State bears the burden of affirmatively establishing every element of the crime. The absence of defense evidence does not relieve the State of this burden or convert insufficient evidence into sufficient evidence.

---

## III. THE THEFT STATUTE AS APPLIED IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE

### A. The Prosecution's Reliance on Brown Is Misplaced

The prosecution cites *State v. Brown*, 121 Wash. 2d 283, 850 P.2d 310 (1993), for the proposition that "market value" is a well-established legal concept that provides fair notice. However, the prosecution conflates the *definition* of market value with the *method of proof* employed by the State.

While "market value" as a term may be sufficiently definite, the State's theory in this case—using the purchase price of a defective truck to establish the value of an improved truck at a later time—creates an impossible standard for defendants to understand. A defendant cannot know whether his conduct constitutes first-degree theft, second-degree theft, or no theft at all when the State is permitted to rely on a purchase price from a prior transaction involving a materially different version of the property.

### B. The Prosecution Misapplies Hennings

The prosecution's reliance on *State v. Hennings*, 129 Wash. 2d 512, 919 P.2d 580 (1996), is similarly misplaced. In *Hennings*, the Court held that a statute is not void for vagueness merely because "the State uses a particular method of proof." However, the instant case presents a different situation: the State has not merely used a particular method of proof, but has used a method of proof that bears no rational relationship to the element it purports to prove.

The prosecution's theory permits the State to satisfy the value element by presenting evidence of a prior sale price without any evidence connecting that price to the condition of the property at the time of the crime. This creates an arbitrary and unpredictable standard that fails to provide fair notice of what conduct is prohibited.

---

## IV. THE PROSECUTION'S REBUTTAL FAILS TO ADDRESS THE FUNDAMENTAL INSUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE

The prosecution's rebuttal entirely fails to address the core deficiency in the State's case: the complete absence of evidence regarding the market value of the truck *at the time and place of the crime*. The prosecution presented evidence only of the purchase price of a defective truck months earlier. The prosecution presented no evidence of:

1. The condition of the truck at the time of the attempted sale;
2. The nature or extent of any improvements made;
3. The market value of the truck in its improved condition;
4. Any expert testimony regarding the value of the truck; or
5. Any comparable sales of similar trucks in similar condition.

Under *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 829 P.2d 1068 (1992), evidence is sufficient only if "any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." A rational trier of fact could not find that the market value of the truck exceeded $1,000 based solely on evidence that the defendants purchased a defective truck for $1,800 months earlier, when the prosecution presented no evidence regarding the truck's value at the time of the crime.

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the convictions for attempted first degree theft by deception and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**State of Washington, Respondent v. John George and Tommy George, Appellants.**

**FINAL RULING**

**PENDING BEFORE THE COURT** is the appeal of Appellants John George and Tommy George following their convictions for attempted first degree theft by deception. The Court has reviewed the rebuttals of the prosecution and defense, the record provided, and the applicable legal standards. Based solely on the facts and arguments presented, the Court issues the following final ruling.

**I. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The Appellants contend that the State failed to present sufficient evidence to establish the value of the truck at the time and place of the crime, a necessary element for first degree theft. Specifically, the Appellants argue that the State’s reliance on the $1,800 purchase price was erroneous because that price reflected the truck’s value in a defective condition, whereas the crime involved the truck in an allegedly improved condition.

The legal standard for reviewing a sufficiency challenge is well settled. In *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 829 P.2d 1068 (1992), this Court held that evidence is sufficient if, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The State bears the burden of proving every element of the offense, including the value of the property. *State v. Green*, 89 Wash. 2d 554, 574 P.2d 1171 (1978).

The Appellants rely on *State v. Charley*, 54 Wash. 2d 422, 341 P.2d 476 (1959), for the proposition that market value must be determined "at the time and place of the crime." While the Court agrees with this definition, the Appellants’ application of it to the facts is unpersuasive. The State presented evidence that the Appellants purchased the truck for $1,800 in June 2003. At that time, the truck was inoperable due to a rear wheel differential problem, had sat outside for two years, and had high mileage. This $1,800 figure represents the market value of the truck in a significantly depreciated, defective state.

The Appellants argue that because they made improvements to the truck prior to the attempted sale, the State was required to present new evidence of the truck’s value in its improved condition. However, this argument ignores the logical inference arising from the evidence presented. If the market value of the truck was $1,800 while it was inoperable and neglected, it follows that the value of the truck in a repaired, "perfect" condition—as represented by the Appellants to the undercover officers—was equal to or greater than $1,800.

The State was required to prove that the value of the property exceeded $1,000 to sustain a conviction for first degree theft under RCW 9A.56.030. By establishing that the truck was worth $1,800 in its defective state, the State necessarily established that the value exceeded the statutory threshold. The Appellants’ speculation that the improvements might not have added value, or that the truck might have deteriorated, does not negate the sufficiency of the State's evidence. The State is not required to disprove every hypothetical scenario that might lower the value; it need only produce evidence from which a rational jury can find the value exceeded the threshold. *State v. Lui*, 179 Wash. 2d 457, 315 P.3d 493 (2013), confirms that sale price is relevant evidence of market value, particularly when no contrary evidence is presented. Here, the $1,800 purchase price provides a sufficient evidentiary basis for the jury to conclude the value exceeded $1,000.

**II. STATUTORY VAGUENESS**

The Appellants argue that the theft statute is unconstitutionally vague as applied because the State’s use of the purchase price of a defective truck to prove the value of an improved truck fails to provide fair notice. This argument is without merit.

In *State v. Brown*, 121 Wash. 2d 283, 850 P.2d 310 (1993), this Court held that the term "market value" is a well-established legal concept that provides fair notice of prohibited conduct. The statute clearly defines the prohibited act (theft) and the element of value (market value at the time and place of the crime). The fact that the State utilized the purchase price of the truck in a prior transaction as evidence of that value does not render the statute vague. As stated in *State v. Hennings*, 129 Wash. 2d 512, 919 P.2d 580 (1996), a statute is not void for vagueness merely because the State employs a particular method of proof. The Appellants’ inability to predict the precise evidentiary strategy the State would employ does not equate to a lack of notice regarding the criminality of their conduct.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The State presented sufficient evidence for a rational jury to find that the value of the truck exceeded $1,000. The purchase price of $1,800 for the truck in a defective condition established a baseline value that satisfied the statutory threshold for first degree theft, particularly where the Appellants admitted to making improvements prior to the attempted sale. The theft statute is not unconstitutionally vague.

Accordingly, the Court affirms the decision of the Court of Appeals.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## IN THE WASHINGTON STATE SUPREME COURT

### State of Washington, Respondent v. John George and Tommy George, Appellants

---

## INTRODUCTION

Your Honors, the defense respectfully submits this closing statement in support of our appeal and in rebuttal to the State's position. The defense maintains that the Court of Appeals erred in affirming the convictions for attempted first degree theft by deception, and this Court should reverse for two fundamental reasons: (1) the State failed to present sufficient evidence to establish the market value of the truck at the time and place of the crime as required by Washington law, and (2) the application of the theft statute to these circumstances renders it unconstitutionally vague.

---

## I. STATEMENT OF FACTS ESSENTIAL TO DEFENSE

The defense emphasizes the following critical facts, which the State has conveniently minimized:

The Georges did not obtain the truck through deceptive means; rather, they purchased the truck in its defective condition from Jerome Potter for $1,800 in June 2003. At the time of this purchase, the truck was inoperable due to a rear wheel differential problem, had sat uncovered outside for two years, and carried approximately 185,000 miles. The odometer read 70,000 miles due to its five-digit limitation. Potter explicitly disclosed these mechanical problems to Tommy George, including the engine replacement from a "350" to a "400."

The evidence establishes that the Georges made improvements to the truck after purchasing it. They invested labor and resources to transform the inoperable, deteriorated vehicle into something they believed had value. This improvement process is a critical fact that distinguishes the condition of the truck at the time of purchase from its condition at the time of the attempted sale.

The misrepresentations made to the undercover officers—describing the truck as in "perfect condition," always garaged, and having only 70,000 miles—occurred after the Georges had invested in the truck. The State's theory conflates the value of the truck in its defective state at the time of purchase with its value after improvements, but this conflations violates the established legal standard for determining market value.

---

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS: SUFFICIENCY OF EVIDENCE

### A. The Legal Standard for Market Value

The State bears the burden of proving every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, including the value of the property at the time and place of the crime. This Court held in **State v. Charley**, 54 Wash. 2d 422, 341 P.2d 476 (1959), that market value must be determined "at the time and place of the crime." This is a well-settled principle that the State cannot circumvent.

The time of the crime here is not June 2003 when the Georges purchased the defective truck from Potter. The crime of attempted first degree theft by deception occurred when the Georges attempted to sell the truck to the undercover officers based on their false representations. The place of the crime is similarly distinct from the prior sale transaction.

### B. The State's Evidence Failed to Establish Value at the Relevant Time

The State's only evidence of value was the $1,800 purchase price from June 2003. This price reflected the market value of the truck in its defective, inoperable condition—sitting outside for two years with a malfunctioning differential and high mileage. The State presented no evidence whatsoever regarding the value of the truck at the time of the attempted sale.

As this Court recognized in **State v. Green**, 89 Wash. 2d 554, 574 P.2d 1171 (1978), the State bears the burden of proving every element of the offense. The value element requires proof of the property's worth at the time of the criminal conduct, not at some prior point in time. The State's reliance on the purchase price constitutes an fundamental error in temporal analysis.

The trial court acknowledged this problem by giving lesser included instructions on attempted second and third degree theft, implicitly recognizing that the value element was not firmly established for first degree theft. The Court of Appeals erred in affirming the conviction despite this evidentiary deficiency.

### C. The Logical Inference Argument Fails

The State's argument that the value of the improved truck "must be equal to or greater than" the value of the defective truck rests on faulty logic. This Court should reject the proposition that improvements automatically increase market value. A rational trier of fact cannot simply assume that repairs and improvements to a thirty-year-old vehicle necessarily add value. The vehicle could have deteriorated further, the improvements could have been performed improperly, or the market could have valued the truck at less than the purchase price plus improvement costs.

In **State v. Lui**, 179 Wash. 2d 457, 315 P.3d 493 (2013), this Court held that sale price is relevant evidence of market value "particularly when no contrary evidence is presented." However, the State cannot rely on this precedent to bootstrap evidence from a prior transaction when the temporal element is fundamentally misaligned. The sale price of the defective truck is relevant to the value of that defective truck at that time—it is not relevant to the value of an improved truck at a later time.

### D. The Defense Did Not Bear the Burden of Production

The State argues that the defense's failure to present evidence about the truck's value in its improved condition relieves the State of its burden. This represents a fundamental misunderstanding of our legal system. Under **In re Winship**, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), the burden of proof rests squarely on the prosecution. The defense has no obligation to prove innocence or to present evidence undermining the prosecution's case.

However, the defense did raise this issue in its motion for dismissal, arguing that the State failed to establish a prima facie case. The trial court denied this motion, and the Court of Appeals erred in affirming this denial. The State's evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the prosecution, simply does not establish the essential element of value at the time and place of the crime.

---

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS: CONSTITUTIONAL VAGUENESS

### A. The Due Process Clause Requires Fair Notice

The defense maintains that applying the theft statute to these circumstances, where the State uses the purchase price of a defective item to establish the value of an allegedly improved item, violates due process. The Fourteenth Amendment requires that criminal statutes provide fair notice of what conduct is prohibited.

In **State v. Brown**, 121 Wash. 2d 283, 850 P.2d 310 (1993), this Court acknowledged that "market value" is a well-established legal concept. However, the concept of market value must be applied in a manner that provides actual notice to defendants. The State's approach here—using a prior sale price from a different point in time to establish value at the time of the crime—creates an impossible standard for defendants to anticipate.

### B. The Statute as Applied Fails to Provide Fair Notice

A defendant seeking to comply with the law must understand what facts determine criminal liability. Under the State's theory, a defendant cannot know whether his or her conduct constitutes first degree theft because the value determination depends on evidence the defendant cannot possibly anticipate—the purchase price of an item in a prior transaction, regardless of what the item looked like at the time of the alleged crime.

The defense in **State v. Hennings**, 129 Wash. 2d 512, 919 P.2d 580 (1996), argued that a statute is not void for vagueness merely because the State employs a particular method of proof. However, the defense does not challenge the method of proof in the abstract. Rather, the defense argues that the specific application of the statute here—using a prior sale price to establish value when the property's condition has changed—provides no notice to defendants regarding what evidence the State will rely upon.

The State conflates statutory clarity with evidentiary sufficiency. Even if the theft statute clearly defines the prohibited conduct, its application in this case fails to provide the fair notice required by due process. The Georges could not have known, when they made improvements to the truck, that the State would later rely on the purchase price of the defective truck to establish the value element of first degree theft.

---

## IV. REBUTTAL TO STATE'S ARGUMENTS

### A. Rebuttal to the "Baseline Value" Argument

The State argues that the $1,800 purchase price established a "baseline value" that satisfies the statutory threshold. This argument misstates the legal requirement. The statute requires proof of value at the time and place of the crime—not at some prior transaction. The State's "baseline value" theory effectively reads the temporal requirement out of the statute.

The State cites **State v. Salinas**, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 829 P.2d 1068 (1992), for the proposition that evidence is sufficient if any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt. However, **Salinas** does not stand for the proposition that any evidence, regardless of its temporal relevance, can establish the value element. The evidence must still be capable of supporting a finding regarding the value at the relevant time.

### B. Rebuttal to the Application of *Charley*

The State argues that *Charley* does not require separate evidence for each stage of the property's condition. This misreads *Charley*. The holding in *Charley* establishes that market value must be determined at the time and place of the crime—this is not a flexible standard that permits the State to rely on evidence from different points in time.

The crime here was the attempted sale based on false representations. The value relevant to this crime is the value of the truck at the time of the attempted sale, not the value at the time of the prior purchase. The State's evidence simply fails to establish this element.

### C. Rebuttal to the Vagueness Argument

The State argues that the defense's vagueness argument conflates evidentiary challenges with constitutional concerns. This is incorrect. The defense's argument recognizes that the statute, as applied, fails to provide fair notice. The defendant cannot know, in advance, what evidence the State will use to establish value.

The State's reliance on **Brown** and **Hennings** is misplaced. In **Brown**, the term "market value" was found sufficiently clear because it is a well-established legal concept. However, the concept of market value, when applied to circumstances where the property's condition has changed, must still provide notice regarding what evidence will be relevant. The State's approach here provides no such notice.

---

## V. CONCLUSION AND RELIEF REQUESTED

The defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the Court of Appeals and dismiss the convictions for attempted first degree theft by deception. The State failed to present sufficient evidence to establish the market value of the truck at the time and place of the crime. The State's reliance on the $1,800 purchase price from a prior transaction, when the truck was in a defective condition, does not satisfy the statutory requirement.

Alternatively, the defense requests that this Court reverse and remand for a new trial, during which the State should be required to present evidence regarding the truck's value at the time of the attempted sale rather than relying on the prior purchase price.

The defense further requests that this Court hold, consistent with due process requirements, that the theft statute, as applied in these circumstances, fails to provide fair notice of the conduct prohibited and is therefore unconstitutionally vague.

The defense has maintained throughout these proceedings that the State cannot meet its burden by conflating the value of the truck at the time of purchase with its value at the time of the crime. The Court of Appeals erred in affirming the convictions despite this fundamental evidentiary deficiency. This Court should correct that error.

**Respectfully submitted,**

**DEFENSE**

**State of Washington, Respondent v. John George and Tommy George, Appellants.**

**FINAL RULING**

**PENDING BEFORE THE COURT** is the appeal of Appellants John George and Tommy George following their convictions for attempted first degree theft by deception. The Court has reviewed the closing arguments of the prosecution and defense, the record provided, and the applicable legal standards. Based solely on the facts and arguments presented, the Court issues the following final ruling.

**I. SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE**

The Appellants contend that the State failed to present sufficient evidence to establish the value of the truck at the time and place of the crime, a necessary element for first degree theft. The State argues that the $1,800 purchase price paid by the Appellants for the truck in its defective condition established a "baseline value" that necessarily exceeded the $1,000 threshold for first degree theft, regardless of the truck's condition at the time of the attempted sale.

The legal standard for reviewing a sufficiency challenge is well settled. In *State v. Salinas*, 119 Wash. 2d 192, 829 P.2d 1068 (1992), this Court held that evidence is sufficient if, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The State bears the burden of proving every element of the offense, including the value of the property. *State v. Green*, 89 Wash. 2d 554, 574 P.2d 1171 (1978).

The central issue is whether the State's evidence—the $1,800 purchase price of a defective truck—was sufficient to prove the market value of the truck at the time and place of the crime. The crime occurred when the Appellants attempted to sell the truck to undercover officers, at which time they represented the truck to be in "perfect condition" and "great shape." In contrast, the $1,800 purchase price reflected the value of the truck in June 2003, when it was inoperable due to a rear wheel differential problem, had sat outside for two years, and had high mileage.

This Court has consistently held that market value must be determined "at the time and place of the crime." *State v. Charley*, 54 Wash. 2d 422, 341 P.2d 476 (1959). The State's evidence addressed the value of the truck at a distinct point in time (June 2003) and in a distinct condition (defective). The State presented no evidence regarding the value of the truck in its improved condition at the time of the attempted sale.

The State argues that it is logical to infer that a truck worth $1,800 in a defective state must be worth at least $1,000 in a repaired state. While this may be a common-sense inference, it is not evidence. The State bears the burden of proving the value of the property *as it existed at the time of the crime*. *State v. Green*, 89 Wash. 2d 554, 574 P.2d 1171 (1978). The State cannot meet this burden by proving the value of the property in a prior, different condition and asking the jury to speculate that the value remained above the statutory threshold after the Appellants' alleged improvements.

The Appellants correctly note that improvements do not automatically increase market value, and the State presented no expert testimony, appraisal, or other evidence to establish the value of the truck in its improved condition. Without such evidence, the jury was left to speculate as to whether the repairs were effective, whether they added value, or whether the market value of a 1974 truck with 185,000 miles exceeded $1,000. The State's reliance on the purchase price of the defective truck fails to satisfy the "time and place" requirement of *Charley*. Consequently, the evidence is insufficient to support a conviction for first degree theft.

**II. STATUTORY VAGUENESS**

The Appellants argue that the theft statute is unconstitutionally vague as applied because the State's use of the purchase price of a defective truck to prove the value of an improved truck fails to provide fair notice. This argument is without merit.

In *State v. Brown*, 121 Wash. 2d 283, 850 P.2d 310 (1993), this Court held that the term "market value" is a well-established legal concept that provides fair notice of prohibited conduct. The statute clearly defines the prohibited act (theft) and the element of value (market value at the time and place of the crime). The fact that the State failed to present sufficient evidence to meet this standard in this specific instance does not render the statute vague. As stated in *State v. Hennings*, 129 Wash. 2d 512, 919 P.2d 580 (1996), a statute is not void for vagueness merely because the State employs a particular method of proof or fails to prove its case adequately. The Appellants' challenge is rooted in the sufficiency of the evidence, not a lack of notice regarding the criminality of their conduct.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The State presented sufficient evidence to establish that the Appellants attempted to sell the truck under false pretenses. However, the State failed to present sufficient evidence to establish the value of the truck at the time and place of the crime as required for a conviction of first degree theft. The $1,800 purchase price of the truck in its defective condition was not sufficient to prove the value of the truck in its improved condition exceeded $1,000. The theft statute is not unconstitutionally vague.

Accordingly, the Court reverses the decision of the Court of Appeals regarding the conviction for attempted first degree theft. The case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
