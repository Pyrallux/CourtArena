**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI) and the subsequent denial of the motion to suppress. The Appellant argues that the court of appeals correctly applied trial-level standards to a dispositive suppression hearing, asserting that the identity of the CI was necessary to challenge probable cause. The Appellee (State) contends that the court of appeals erred by conflating the limited scope of a suppression hearing with a trial on the merits, arguing that the parties’ agreement rendered the issue of guilt irrelevant and that informant disclosure is improper in such a proceeding.

After reviewing the record and the arguments of counsel, the Court finds the prosecution’s position to be the more persuasive interpretation of the applicable legal standards. The following analysis outlines the reasoning for this preliminary ruling.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Dispositive Nature of the Proceeding**

The facts clearly establish that the parties entered into a binding agreement that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive." As the prosecutor articulated, and defense counsel confirmed, the understanding was that if the court ruled in favor of the State on the motion to suppress, the Appellant would plead guilty, and the court would determine the sentence. If the court ruled in favor of the defense, the State would dismiss the case. Defense counsel explicitly stated, "It was. It is clear. I am not trying to—like I said, there is no trickery in what I am doing. It was a dispositive Motion to Suppress."

By agreeing to this structure, the defense effectively waived the right to a trial on the issue of guilt or innocence. The prosecution correctly argues that the defense cannot now seek to litigate issues that are properly reserved for trial—such as whether the CI "set him up" or entrapment occurred—under the guise of a probable cause challenge. The defense’s attempt to introduce "new information" regarding the CI’s belongings in the car and her presence with the Appellant goes to the factual narrative of the crime (i.e., ownership of the contraband and intent) rather than the validity of the search or seizure itself. While the defense frames this as a challenge to the CI’s reliability, the factual dispute they raise is fundamentally a substantive defense to the charge, which was contractually eliminated by the dispositive agreement.

**B. The Standard for Disclosure in Suppression Hearings**

The central legal question is whether the identity of a confidential informant must be disclosed during a hearing on a motion to suppress. The prosecution relies on *United States v. Martinez*, 901 F.2d 374 (5th Cir. 1990), which explicitly holds that "the issue of whether an informant should be disclosed is not relevant to a suppression hearing." This precedent aligns with the established principle that a suppression hearing is a limited proceeding designed to assess the objective legality of the police conduct, not the credibility of witnesses or the ultimate guilt of the defendant.

The defense attempts to distinguish *Martinez* and relies on *United States v. Mendoza*, 574 F.2d 1373 (5th Cir. 1978), arguing that the court must assess the informant’s reliability to determine probable cause. While it is true that probable cause is based in part on the reliability of the informant, the defense conflates the *court’s* assessment of reliability with the *defendant’s* right to disclosure. As noted in *United States v. Mendoza*, 574 F.2d 1373, 1378 (5th Cir. 1978), the court examines the totality of the circumstances to establish probable cause, but this does not grant the defendant an automatic right to cross-examine the informant or pierce their anonymity during that specific hearing. The trial judge here found the informant reliable based on the "effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them," which is a sufficient basis for the probable cause determination under the law.

**C. The Inapplicability of *Roviaro***

The defense places significant weight on *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), arguing that because the suppression hearing was dispositive and determined the Appellant’s fate, the "fair trial" standards of *Roviaro* should apply. *Roviaro* establishes that disclosure is proper when the informant’s testimony is "relevant and helpful to the defense" or "essential to a fair determination" of guilt or innocence.

However, the prosecution correctly notes that *Roviaro* is predicated on the existence of a trial where the defendant’s guilt is the ultimate issue. Here, there was no trial. The parties agreed to resolve the case based solely on the legality of the evidence. The defense’s liberty interest, while significant, does not transform a suppression hearing into a trial for the purposes of *Roviaro*. The procedural safeguards of a trial, including the broad right to compel witness testimony, were exchanged for the certainty of the dispositive motion. The defense cannot now invoke *Roviaro* to obtain the discovery tools of a trial after having agreed to forgo the trial itself.

**D. The "New Information" Argument**

The defense argues that the "new information" regarding the CI’s presence and belongings justified re-urging the motion to disclose. Even assuming this information was genuinely new, its relevance to the *suppression* of the evidence is tenuous. The fact that the CI’s belongings were found in the car suggests a defense of ownership or possession, which is a classic trial defense. It does not inherently negate the probable cause that led to the search in the first instance. The prosecution’s argument—that if the defense wished to attack the CI’s credibility regarding these facts, they should have proceeded to trial—is logically sound given the parameters of the agreement.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The court of appeals erred in applying trial-level standards to a proceeding that was explicitly limited to the dispositive issue of probable cause. The defense’s attempt to expand the scope of the hearing to include a full-fledged attack on the informant’s credibility and identity contravenes the parties' agreement and controlling precedent regarding suppression hearings. The identity of the informant was not relevant to the limited issue before the trial court, and the defense’s reliance on *Roviaro* is misplaced in this procedural context.

Accordingly, the preliminary ruling is in favor of the prosecution. The decision of the court of appeals is reversed, and the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is upheld.

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court for an updated ruling following the submission of rebuttal arguments by both the prosecution and the defense. The central issue remains whether the trial court erred in denying the motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI) during a hearing on a dispositive motion to suppress. The Appellant argues that the "new information" regarding the CI's potential involvement ("setting him up") and her belongings found in the car was critical to challenging the CI's reliability and, therefore, the probable cause justifying the search. After carefully considering the rebuttals and the applicable precedent, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling in favor of the prosecution. The defense's attempt to frame a substantive defense to the charge as a probable cause issue does not overcome the clear procedural boundaries established by the parties' agreement and controlling case law.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Scope of the Dispositive Agreement and the Waiver of Trial Rights**

The defense correctly notes in its rebuttal that a dispositive motion to suppress does not constitute a blanket waiver of all constitutional rights. However, the defense mischaracterizes the nature of the waiver effected by the agreement. The record reflects that both parties explicitly agreed that the motion to suppress would be "dispositive." As the prosecutor stated, and defense counsel confirmed, the agreement was that if the court ruled in favor of the State on the motion to suppress, the Appellant would plead guilty, and if the court ruled in favor of the defense, the State would dismiss the case.

By entering into this agreement, the defense strategically accepted the risk that the suppression hearing would be the sole forum for challenging the State's case. The prosecution's rebuttal accurately points out that the defense cannot now expand the scope of this limited hearing to litigate factual disputes regarding the Appellant's guilt—such as whether the CI "set him up" or who actually possessed the narcotics—simply because the outcome is severe. The defense's reliance on the sixteen-year sentence to argue for the application of trial standards is unpersuasive; the gravity of the sentence was a known risk assumed when the defense agreed to the dispositive nature of the hearing.

**B. The Distinction Between Reliability and Guilt**

The defense's primary contention is that the "new information"—specifically, that the CI's belongings were found in the car and that she was with the Appellant—goes to the CI's reliability, thereby making it relevant to the probable cause inquiry. The Court finds this argument to be a distinction without a difference in this context.

While probable cause does rely on the reliability of an informant, the defense's proffered evidence does not challenge the reliability of the *tip* provided to law enforcement; rather, it challenges the *narrative of the crime*. Alleging that the CI "set him up" or that the drugs belonged to the CI are classic defenses to the charge of possession, not challenges to the objective legality of the search or seizure. As the prosecution argues in its rebuttal, if the police had probable cause to search the vehicle based on the CI's information and corroboration, the fact that the CI may have been involved in the underlying criminal activity does not retroactively invalidate the probable cause.

The trial judge determined that the CI was reliable based on the "effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." Under *United States v. Mendoza*, 574 F.2d 1373 (5th Cir. 1978), this assessment by the court is sufficient to establish probable cause. The defense's "new information" does not contradict the accuracy of the CI's past tips; it merely suggests a different version of events regarding the possession of the contraband on the day of the arrest. This is a matter for the jury at trial, not the judge at a suppression hearing.

**C. The Inapplicability of *Roviaro* and *Martinez***

The defense urges the Court to apply *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), arguing that the dispositive nature of the hearing effectively makes it a trial for constitutional purposes. The Court rejects this expansion of *Roviaro*. As the prosecution correctly notes, *Roviaro* is predicated on the defendant's right to a fair trial where guilt or innocence is determined. Here, the defense agreed to forgo that trial in favor of a ruling on the motion to suppress. The defense cannot now invoke the protections of *Roviaro* to obtain discovery tools they contractually waived the right to use.

Furthermore, the prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Martinez*, 901 F.2d 374 (5th Cir. 1990), remains sound. In *Martinez*, the court held that "the issue of whether an informant should be disclosed is not relevant to a suppression hearing." The defense attempts to distinguish *Martinez* by claiming the issue here is reliability, not guilt. However, as established above, the defense's "reliability" argument is inextricably linked to the guilt/innocence defense of entrapment or misidentification. Because the defense is effectively seeking to litigate the merits of the possession charge, *Martinez* supports the denial of the motion to disclose.

**D. The "New Information" Does Not Warrant Reopening the Hearing**

The defense argues that the "new information" regarding the CI's belongings justified re-urging the motion. Even assuming this information was previously unknown, its relevance to the *suppression* of the evidence is tenuous. The prosecution's rebuttal logically demonstrates that such evidence concerns possession, ownership, and intent—issues that were explicitly taken off the table by the dispositive agreement. The defense cannot use the suppression hearing as a vehicle to introduce a defense that should have been reserved for a trial, especially when the defense explicitly agreed that the suppression hearing would determine the case's outcome.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The defense's rebuttal fails to demonstrate that the trial court abused its discretion in denying the motion to disclose. The "new information" proffered by the defense relates to the factual dispute of possession and intent, which are trial issues, not to the objective legality of the search or the informant's reliability in providing probable cause. The parties' agreement to a dispositive motion to suppress properly limited the scope of the hearing, and the defense cannot now circumvent that agreement by reframing trial defenses as probable cause challenges.

Accordingly, the ruling in favor of the prosecution is affirmed. The decision of the court of appeals is reversed, and the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is upheld.

## II. RELEVANT FACTS

The Defense submits that the following facts, as established in the record, are dispositive of this appeal:

**B. The "New Information" Discovery**

## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

**A. The Standard of Review**

This Court reviews the trial court's ruling on a motion to disclose a confidential informant's identity for an abuse of discretion. *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957). However, the legal questions underlying that discretion—particularly whether disclosure is required under the Constitution—are reviewed de novo.

**B. The Roviaro Doctrine Applies to This Proceeding**

The prosecution argues that *Roviaro* applies only to trials, not to suppression hearings. This reading is too narrow. While *Roviaro* emphasized the defendant's right to a fair trial, the Supreme Court grounded that right in the defendant's constitutional privilege to prepare and present a defense. The Court held:

> "We believe that the privilege is limited to the extent that it yields to the public interest in the law enforcement and the fair administration of justice and gives way when the informant's testimony is relevant and helpful to the defense." *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957).

The prosecution's argument that the dispositive agreement eliminates the need for disclosure fails for several reasons.

First, the agreement was expressly made "at the time when Judge Hill was here" and before the Defense received the "new information" regarding the confidential informant's alleged involvement in framing the appellant. The Defense cannot be deemed to have waived a right of which it was not aware.

Second, even assuming the agreement constitutes a waiver, such waiver cannot extend to constitutional rights that were not contemplated by the parties at the time of the agreement. The "new information" fundamentally altered the nature of the proceeding by suggesting that the confidential informant was not merely a tipster but an active participant in the crime.

Third, the prosecution's interpretation would allow the State to evade its constitutional obligations by simply agreeing to a dispositive suppression hearing. This result would eviscerate the *Roviaro* doctrine and incentivize gamesmanship.

**C. The "New Information" Was Relevant to Probable Cause**

The prosecution argues that the "new information" relates to guilt or innocence, not to probable cause. This characterization misconstrues the nature of the Defense's argument.

The Defense does not merely argue that the informant framed the appellant—it argues that this fact renders the informant unreliable for purposes of establishing probable cause. An informant who provides false information to law enforcement cannot constitute a reliable source for probable cause. As the Supreme Court explained in *Roviaro*:

> "Where the informant is a participant in the crime or where the informant has been involved in other criminal activities, disclosure may be necessary to enable the defendant to prepare his defense." *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 65 (1957) (Jackson, J., dissenting, articulating principles adopted by the majority).

Here, the Defense presented evidence that the confidential informant was present in the vehicle and that her belongings were found therein. This evidence directly supports an inference that the informant provided false information to law enforcement regarding the cocaine's ownership. If the informant lied about the ownership of the contraband, her tip cannot be deemed reliable for probable cause purposes.

The prosecution relies on *United States v. Mendoza*, 574 F.2d 1373 (5th Cir. 1978), for the proposition that the trial court's finding of reliability was sufficient. However, *Mendoza* is distinguishable because the defendant in that case did not present evidence that the informant was involved in the crime. Here, the Defense presented such evidence, which distinguishes this case from *Mendoza*.

**D. The Martinez Decision Does Not Control**

The prosecution's reliance on *United States v. Martinez*, 901 F.2d 374 (5th Cir. 1990), is misplaced. In *Martinez*, the Fifth Circuit held that "the issue of whether an informant should be disclosed is not relevant to a suppression hearing." However, the court in *Martinez* did not address the scenario presented here—where the defendant presents evidence that the informant was involved in the crime and where the informant's alleged involvement directly bears on her reliability for probable cause purposes.

The prosecution's attempt to distinguish *Roviaro* on the ground that it applies only to trials proves too much. If *Martinez* stands for the proposition that disclosure is never relevant to a suppression hearing, it conflicts with the Supreme Court's recognition in *Roviaro* that disclosure may be necessary when the informant's testimony is "relevant and helpful to the defense." *Roviaro*, 353 U.S. at 60-61.

Moreover, the prosecution's argument that the Defense is "reframing trial defenses as probable cause challenges" mischaracterizes the nature of the Defense's argument. The Defense does not seek to litigate the merits of the possession charge—it seeks to demonstrate that the informant's tip was unreliable, thus negating probable cause. This is a legitimate suppression issue.

**E. The Sixteen-Year Sentence Warrants Greater Scrutiny**

The prosecution dismisses the Defense's argument regarding the severity of the sentence as "circular reasoning." This characterization is incorrect.

The sixteen-year sentence is not merely a consequence of the plea—it is a factor that weighs in favor of requiring full disclosure. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Roviaro*:

> "The desire of the accused to show that an informant was a participant in the crime and not merely a tipster would normally be stronger when the stakes are higher." *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 64 (1957).

While the prosecution is correct that the appellant entered a guilty plea, that plea was entered pursuant to the dispositive agreement after the motion to suppress was denied. The appellant did not voluntarily forgo a trial; he was compelled to plead guilty because the trial court denied his motion to disclose the informant. Under these circumstances, the severity of the sentence is directly relevant to the question of whether the trial court abused its discretion.

## IV. REBUTTAL OF PROSECUTION'S ARGUMENTS

**A. The Dispositive Agreement Did Not Constitute a Blanket Waiver**

The prosecution argues that by agreeing to a dispositive motion to suppress, the Defense waived all constitutional rights. This argument fails for the reasons stated above. The agreement was entered before the Defense possessed the "new information" regarding the confidential informant's involvement in the crime. Moreover, the prosecution cannot point to any language in the agreement that expressly waives the defendant's right to disclosure under *Roviaro*.

**B. The Defense Did Not Seek to Transform the Hearing Into a Trial**

The prosecution accuses the Defense of improperly seeking to litigate guilt or innocence at a suppression hearing. This accusation mischaracterizes the Defense's arguments.

The Defense's motion to disclose was based on the informant's alleged involvement in framing the appellant—not on the merits of the possession charge. This distinction is critical. The Defense does not seek to prove that it did not possess the cocaine; it seeks to prove that the informant was unreliable, thus negating probable cause.

The prosecution's argument that "if the police had probable cause based on reliable information, the fact that the informant may have been present or that her belongings were later found in the car does not retroactively negate that probable cause" conflates two distinct issues. The question is not whether probable cause existed at the time of the search—it is whether the informant's tip was reliable. An informant who frames a defendant cannot be deemed reliable.

**C. The Prosecution's Reliance on Martinez Is Misplaced**

As stated above, *Martinez* does not address the scenario presented here. In *Martinez*, the defendant did not present evidence that the informant was involved in the crime. Here, the Defense presented such evidence. The Fifth Circuit's statement that "the issue of whether an informant should be disclosed is not relevant to a suppression hearing" must be read in context—and the context here is materially different.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal regarding the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose the identity of a confidential informant (CI). The Appellant contends that the trial court abused its discretion by refusing to order disclosure based on "new information" suggesting the CI was involved in the underlying offense. Specifically, the Appellant argues that because the CI’s belongings were found in the vehicle and she was present with the Appellant, her identity is material to challenging the reliability of the tip that established probable cause. The Appellee (State) argues that the proceeding was a dispositive motion to suppress, agreed upon by both parties, and that the defense’s arguments improperly introduce trial-level issues of guilt and innocence into a hearing limited to Fourth Amendment considerations.

After reviewing the record, the closing arguments, and the applicable precedent, the Court finds that the trial court did not err in denying the motion to disclose. The defense’s "new information" relates to the factual dispute of possession and intent—issues reserved for trial—and does not undermine the probable cause determination for the search. Furthermore, the dispositive nature of the agreement precludes the defense from expanding the scope of the hearing to litigate the merits of the possession charge.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Dispositive Agreement Defined the Scope of the Proceeding**

The record clearly establishes that the parties entered into an agreement that the motion to suppress would be dispositive. The prosecutor explicitly stated, "the agreement was that this was a dispositive Motion to Suppress; and after the court ruled on that Motion to Suppress, the court would then make a decision to sentencing." Crucially, defense counsel confirmed this understanding, stating, "It was. It is clear. I am not trying to—like I said, there is no trickery in what I am doing. It was a dispositive Motion to Suppress."

By agreeing to this framework, the defense accepted that the suppression hearing would be the sole forum for challenging the State's case. The agreement limited the inquiry to the legality of the search and seizure. The defense cannot now circumvent this agreement by introducing evidence that goes to the guilt or innocence of the Appellant, simply because the outcome of the suppression hearing was unfavorable. The "new information" regarding the CI’s belongings and presence does not alter the procedural posture of the case; the hearing remained a suppression hearing, not a trial on the merits.

**B. The Inapplicability of *Roviaro* to a Suppression Hearing**

The defense relies heavily on *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), arguing that the CI’s identity is necessary to a fair determination of the case. However, *Roviaro* is predicated on the defendant's right to a fair trial where guilt or innocence is determined. The Supreme Court held that the government’s privilege to withhold the identity of an informer "must give way where the public interest and the fair administration of justice require disclosure" in the context of a trial. *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957).

Here, there was no trial. The parties agreed to resolve the case solely on the motion to suppress. The defense attempts to argue that the severity of the sentence transforms the hearing into a "trial for constitutional purposes," but this argument is unavailing. The sentence was a consequence of the Appellant’s guilty plea, which was entered pursuant to the dispositive agreement. The gravity of the potential sentence does not convert a Fourth Amendment hearing into a trial on the merits, nor does it trigger the disclosure protections of *Roviaro* in a context where the defendant has agreed to forgo a trial.

**C. *Martinez* Controls the Relevance of Disclosure in Suppression Hearings**

The controlling precedent for this specific procedural context is *United States v. Martinez*, 901 F.2d 374 (5th Cir. 1990). In *Martinez*, the Fifth Circuit held that "the issue of whether an informant should be disclosed is not relevant to a suppression hearing." *United States v. Martinez*, 901 F.2d 374, 376 (5th Cir. 1990). The court reasoned that suppression hearings focus exclusively on the legality of the police conduct, not on factual disputes regarding the crime itself.

The defense attempts to distinguish *Martinez* by claiming the CI was a participant in the crime, thereby making her identity relevant to probable cause. However, the defense’s proffered evidence—that the CI’s belongings were found in the car and she was with the Appellant—does not challenge the legality of the search. Instead, it challenges the *ownership* of the contraband. Alleging that the CI "set him up" or that the drugs belonged to her are classic defenses to the charge of possession. These are issues of guilt or innocence, not issues regarding the objective probable cause the police possessed at the time of the search. As the prosecution correctly noted, if the police had probable cause based on the CI’s tip and corroboration, the subsequent discovery that the CI may have been involved in the crime does not retroactively invalidate the probable cause.

**D. The "New Information" Does Not Negate Probable Cause**

The trial judge determined that the CI was reliable based on the "effectiveness of her past tips, the accuracy of them." This assessment is consistent with the "totality of the circumstances" test outlined in *United States v. Mendoza*, 574 F.2d 1373 (5th Cir. 1978). Under *Mendoza*, a court may assess an informant’s reliability based on the veracity of past tips without revealing the informant’s identity.

The defense argues that the "new information" renders the CI unreliable because she may have lied about the Appellant’s possession. However, this argument conflates the reliability of the *tip* with the *defense to the crime*. The fact that the CI may have been involved in the criminal activity does not necessarily mean the information she provided to the police regarding the presence of drugs was false. The defense’s evidence speaks to who possessed the drugs, not whether the police had a reasonable basis to believe the drugs were there when they conducted the search. Because the "new information" does not undermine the factual basis for the search warrant or probable cause, it is not relevant to the motion to suppress.

**E. The Defense’s Waiver of Trial Issues**

The defense contends that the dispositive agreement could not have waived rights regarding "new information" that was discovered after the agreement was made. While the timing of the information is unfortunate, it does not change the legal nature of the hearing. The defense agreed to a dispositive motion to suppress, thereby waiving the right to litigate factual disputes regarding possession and intent at a trial. The defense cannot use the suppression hearing as a backdoor method to litigate these waived issues by framing them as "reliability" challenges. The trial court acted within its discretion by refusing to expand the scope of the hearing to accommodate a defense that should have been raised at trial, had the defense not agreed to the dispositive motion.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court properly denied the motion to disclose the identity of the confidential informant. The proceeding was limited to a dispositive motion to suppress, and the defense’s "new information" regarding the CI’s potential involvement in the crime relates to the Appellant’s guilt or innocence, not the legality of the search. Under *United States v. Martinez*, 901 F.2d 374 (5th Cir. 1990), the identity of the informant was not relevant to the suppression hearing. Furthermore, the protections of *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957), do not apply to a hearing where the defendant has agreed to forgo a trial on the merits.

Accordingly, the judgment of the court of appeals is affirmed, and the trial court’s denial of the motion to disclose is upheld.
