**FINAL RULING**

After reviewing the closing arguments from both the prosecution and the defense, the Court affirms its previous ruling. The central issue remains whether the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency (the Agency) is subject to the supermajority voter approval requirements of California Constitution, article XIII A, section 4 (section 4).

The prosecution argues that the Agency must be deemed a "special district" under section 4 to prevent the circumvention of Proposition 13, relying heavily on the dissenting opinion in *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197 [182 Cal.Rptr. 324, 643 P.2d 941]. While the dissent’s policy concerns regarding potential loopholes are noted, this Court is bound by the majority holding of that controlling precedent. The majority in *Richmond* explicitly held that "the two-thirds requirement of section 4 applies only to those local entities authorized to levy a property tax." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 206. The Court specifically acknowledged and rejected the argument that section 4 should be extended to entities without property tax power to close such a loophole, stating that "the language of section 4 does not support such an extension." *Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197, 207-08.

The facts clearly establish that the Agency lacks the requisite property tax authority. The enabling legislation states that "the Agency possesses no tax power other than the foregoing sales tax." (Gov. Code, § 26283.) Because the Agency is not authorized to levy a property tax, it falls outside the scope of section 4 under the *Richmond* holding.

The prosecution’s reliance on *Board of Supervisors v. County of Ventura* (1977) 19 Cal.3d 372 [139 Cal.Rptr. 650, 566 P.2d 241] to define the Agency as a special district based on its governmental purpose is unpersuasive in light of the specific constitutional limitation interpreted in *Richmond*. Even if the Agency serves a governmental purpose, the constitutional requirement for a supermajority vote is triggered only for entities with property tax powers. Furthermore, as the defense notes, the Agency lacks the independent corporate powers characteristic of the special districts contemplated in *Ventura*.

The prosecution’s citation of *People v. Price* (1991) 226 Cal.App.3d 1251 [277 Cal.Rptr. 114] does not alter this conclusion. *Price* confirms that "section 4 applies to any special tax, whether property or nonproperty, levied by a local entity authorized to levy a property tax." *People v. Price* (1991) 226 Cal.App.3d 1251, 1258. Since the Agency is not authorized to levy a property tax, the two-thirds requirement does not apply to its sales tax.

The defense correctly argues that policy arguments regarding loopholes cannot override the plain language of the Constitution as interpreted by the Supreme Court. If the electorate or the Legislature wishes to expand the supermajority requirement to entities without property tax authority, they must do so through constitutional amendment or new legislation, not through judicial reinterpretation of existing law.

Therefore, the Court finds that the Agency is not subject to the supermajority vote requirements of section 4. The tax ordinance, approved by a simple majority of the voters, is valid.

The judgment of the Court of Appeal is affirmed. The trial court’s judgment declaring the tax invalid is reversed. The sales tax imposed by the San Diego County Regional Justice Facility Financing Agency is valid.
