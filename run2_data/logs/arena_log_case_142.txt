# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Matter of the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians Motor Vehicle Registration Reciprocity

---

**MAY IT PLEASE THE COURT:**

The Defense respectfully submits this opening response on behalf of the State of Minnesota in opposition to the Plaintiff's petition for declaratory and injunctive relief. While the Defense acknowledges the important principles of tribal sovereignty at issue, the Plaintiff's argument overstates the applicable legal framework and ignores critical limitations on tribal authority that have been consistently recognized by this Court.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

This case presents a straightforward question of statutory interpretation and comity between governmental entities, not a fundamental challenge to tribal sovereignty. The Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians adopted a motor vehicle registration ordinance in 1974 and now seeks judicial intervention to compel the State of Minnesota to extend reciprocal exemptions to Band members under Minnesota's existing reciprocity statutes.

The Defense does not dispute that the Red Lake Band possesses authority to regulate motor vehicle registration on its reservation. The Band's ordinance was properly adopted and approved by the Secretary of the Interior. However, the question before this Court is whether Minnesota is constitutionally or statutorily required to extend reciprocity privileges to the Band when the Band's ordinance imposes substantial annual registration fees that differ materially from the fee structures of the other jurisdictions with whom Minnesota maintains reciprocity agreements.

The Plaintiff's argument conflates tribal sovereignty with a right to demand equal treatment from the state in all circumstances. This Court has never adopted such a broad principle. To the contrary, in *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946), this Court recognized that tribal sovereignty exists within a framework of federal supremacy, and tribal powers are "not absolute" but subject to congressional authority. The Plaintiff's attempt to transform the Band's internal regulatory authority into a constitutional right to state reciprocity ignores this foundational principle.

---

## II. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Framework for Analyzing Tribal Sovereignty and State Authority

The Plaintiff correctly notes that tribal governments possess attributes of sovereignty. However, the Plaintiff misapplies this principle by ignoring the critical distinction between a tribe's authority over its own members and internal affairs, and any claim to demand specific treatment from state governments.

In *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217, 220 (1959), this Court held that "the inherent sovereign powers of an Indian tribe do not extend to the activities of nonmembers of the tribe." While the Plaintiff cites *United States v. John*, 437 U.S. 634 (1978), for the proposition that tribes possess sovereignty over their members and territory, that case addressed tribal authority over criminal jurisdiction, not a tribe's right to demand state recognition of its regulatory schemes.

The critical flaw in the Plaintiff's argument is its assumption that tribal sovereignty automatically entitles a tribe to the same treatment that Minnesota extends to other states and foreign provinces. This Court has never held that tribal sovereignty creates a constitutional right to reciprocity. The Plaintiff cites *Arizona v. San Carlos Apache Tribe*, 463 U.S. 545 (1983), but that case addressed water rights and the allocation of scarce resources between tribes and states—a fundamentally different context from the present case.

As this Court explained in *New Mexico v. Mescalero Apache Tribe*, 462 U.S. 324, 334 (1983), "the tribe's interest in regulating wildlife on the reservation must be balanced against the state's legitimate interest in wildlife management outside the reservation." Similarly, here, Minnesota has a legitimate interest in managing its highway system and reciprocity relationships, and this Court has consistently held that states retain substantial authority over highway regulation.

### B. Minnesota's Reciprocity Statutes Do Not Create a Mandatory Obligation

The Plaintiff argues that Minnesota's enactment of Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187 creates an obligation to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, the statutes by their terms apply to "residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces which enter into reciprocal agreements with this state." The Red Lake Band is not a "state," "territory," "possession," or "foreign province." The Band is a federally recognized Indian tribe, and the Plaintiff identifies no authority for treating the Band as equivalent to these other governmental entities for purposes of the reciprocity statutes.

Second, the statutory scheme explicitly requires "reciprocal agreements"—meaning that the other jurisdiction must extend similar exemptions to Minnesota residents. The Band's ordinance imposes substantial annual registration fees on all motor vehicles using reservation highways. There is no evidence in the record that the Band would extend reciprocal exemptions to Minnesota residents who drive on the reservation. To the contrary, the Band's ordinance specifically requires such payments. Under these circumstances, the Band cannot claim entitlement to reciprocity under a statute that explicitly requires mutual agreement.

In *McClanahan v. Arizona State Tax Commission*, 411 U.S. 164, 175 (1973), this Court held that "state taxation of income derived from reservation sources" could be permissible in certain circumstances. Similarly, Minnesota's requirement that non-Band members pay registration fees when using reservation roads is consistent with the state's authority over highway use within its jurisdiction.

### C. The Plaintiff's Equal Treatment Argument Fails

The Plaintiff argues that Minnesota's refusal to extend reciprocity constitutes discriminatory treatment in violation of equal treatment principles. This argument fails because the Plaintiff cannot demonstrate that the Band is similarly situated to the jurisdictions with whom Minnesota maintains reciprocity.

The jurisdictions with whom Minnesota maintains reciprocity—Wisconsin, Ontario, the District of Columbia—all impose registration fee structures that are substantially similar to Minnesota's. When a Wisconsin resident drives in Minnesota, the reciprocal exemption makes sense because the fee structures are comparable and the administrative burden is minimal. The Band's ordinance, however, imposes "substantial annual registration fees" that may differ significantly from Minnesota's fees. Requiring Minnesota to honor the Band's registration system would require Minnesota to evaluate and accept a fee structure that differs from its own—an administrative burden the legislature specifically did not intend to impose.

In *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Indian Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134, 155 (1980), this Court upheld tribal authority to tax nonmembers on reservation lands but recognized that "the tribe's interest in exercising its sovereign powers must be balanced against the state's legitimate interest in exercising its own." The same balancing applies here. Minnesota has a legitimate interest in maintaining consistent fee structures for highway use, and the Band's different fee structure provides a substantial justification for different treatment.

### D. The Record Does Not Support the Plaintiff's Claims of Harm

The Plaintiff argues that Minnesota's refusal "significantly dilutes the effectiveness of the governing authority of the Tribal Council." However, the record does not support this claim. The Band's ordinance remains in full force and effect. Band members who drive on Minnesota highways are required to pay Minnesota registration fees, but this is the same requirement imposed on all non-Minnesota residents who drive on Minnesota roads. The Band's authority to regulate registration on its own reservation remains intact.

The Plaintiff's reliance on the statement that reciprocity would "further the economic and social development and growth of the State of Minnesota" is unavailing. This statement, taken from the lower court's opinion, does not create a legal obligation. The legislature made policy judgments about reciprocity that balance multiple interests, and the Plaintiff's attempt to extract a mandatory obligation from general statements of policy finds no support in the statutory text or this Court's precedent.

---

## III. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S SPECIFIC ARGUMENTS

The Plaintiff cites *United States v. John*, 437 U.S. 634 (1978), for the proposition that tribes possess inherent sovereignty over their members and territory. However, that case addressed criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians who committed crimes on tribal land—a context far removed from motor vehicle registration reciprocity. The Plaintiff provides no authority for extending *John* to the present context.

The Plaintiff argues that Minnesota's refusal constitutes "indirect interference" with tribal governance. However, the Band remains free to operate its registration system. Minnesota's requirement that non-Band members pay fees when using state roads is not an interference with the Band's internal governance; it is a legitimate exercise of Minnesota's own sovereignty over state highways.

The Plaintiff's citation to *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544 (1981), is similarly unavailing. In *Montana*, this Court held that tribal civil jurisdiction over nonmembers is limited to situations involving consensual relationships or activities that threaten tribal welfare. Motor vehicle registration on state highways does not fall within either category.

Finally, the Plaintiff's argument that Minnesota has failed to identify "considerations of a substantial character" justifying the disparity in treatment misplaces the burden of proof. The Plaintiff seeks injunctive relief, and "the party seeking injunctive relief bears the burden of establishing the propriety of such relief." The Plaintiff has not demonstrated that Minnesota's reciprocity decisions are arbitrary or capricious, nor has the Plaintiff shown that the Band is entitled to reciprocity as a matter of law.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and dismiss the Plaintiff's complaint. Minnesota's decision to maintain reciprocity agreements with certain jurisdictions while excluding the Red Lake Band is a legitimate exercise of the state's sovereign authority over highway regulation, does not violate tribal sovereignty, and is consistent with the plain language of Minnesota's reciprocity statutes.

The Plaintiff's attempt to transform tribal sovereignty into a constitutional right to state reciprocity finds no support in this Court's precedent and would improperly expand tribal authority at the expense of legitimate state interests. The judgment below should be reversed.

**United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit**

**PER CURIAM.**

This case requires us to determine whether the State of Minnesota’s refusal to extend motor vehicle registration reciprocity to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians constitutes an impermissible burden on tribal sovereignty. The Red Lake Band seeks to enjoin the State from enforcing its motor vehicle registration laws against Band members who have already registered their vehicles under the Band’s ordinance. The district court issued a declaratory judgment and permanent injunction in favor of the Band, holding that the State must afford the Band the same privileges it grants to other comparable governing entities. For the reasons set forth below, we affirm the judgment of the district court.

**I. BACKGROUND**

The Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians adopted a motor vehicle registration ordinance on January 16, 1974, which was approved by the Secretary of the Interior on March 5, 1974. The ordinance imposes a substantial annual registration fee on all motor vehicles using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake Reservation, provides for the issuance of certificates of title, and establishes procedures for recording transfers and security interests. The State of Minnesota does not contest that this ordinance is an appropriate exercise of the Band’s governmental authority.

Minnesota has enacted Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, which authorize state representatives to enter into reciprocal agreements exempting residents of other states, the District of Columbia, territories, possessions, and foreign countries or provinces from motor vehicle taxes and fees. The State has entered into such agreements with jurisdictions including Wisconsin, the District of Columbia, and the Province of Ontario. However, the State refuses to grant a similar exemption to residents of the Red Lake Reservation, requiring them to pay full registration fees to Minnesota despite their compliance with the Band’s ordinance.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. Tribal Sovereignty and State Interference**

The foundation of the Red Lake Band’s argument is its inherent sovereignty. As recognized in *United States v. John*, 437 U.S. 634, 646 (1978), Indian tribes possess attributes of sovereignty over both their members and their territory, including the inherent power to regulate internal affairs. The State does not dispute the validity of the Band’s ordinance; rather, it argues that it has no obligation to recognize it. However, where a state’s action significantly dilutes the effectiveness of a tribe’s self-government, the state must provide a substantial justification for that interference.

The prosecution correctly argues that Minnesota’s refusal to grant reciprocity while extending it to other sovereign entities—such as the Province of Ontario—undermines the Band’s regulatory authority. By forcing Band members to pay dual registration fees while exempting residents of other jurisdictions, Minnesota renders the Band’s licenses "less worthy of recognition" than those issued by foreign provinces. This disparate treatment implicates the core principles of tribal self-government identified in *Arizona v. San Carlos Apache Tribe*, 463 U.S. 545, 569 (1983), which holds that states cannot impose burdens on tribal sovereignty without a compelling justification.

**B. The State’s Obligation to Afford Equal Treatment**

The State contends that its reciprocity statutes apply only to "states" and "foreign provinces," and that the Red Lake Band is not a "state" within the meaning of the statute. The State further argues that the Band’s ordinance is not reciprocal because it imposes fees rather than exempting non-members. While these arguments raise questions of statutory interpretation, they fail to address the equitable principles governing state-tribal relations.

The district court found, and the record supports, that the State is obligated to afford the Red Lake Band the same privileges it grants to other comparable governing entities if (1) denial of such privileges would significantly dilute the effectiveness of the Band’s governmental authority, and (2) there are no considerations of a substantial character justifying the denial. See *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217, 220 (1959) (recognizing the infringement on tribal self-government when state laws are applied on the reservation).

The defense relies heavily on *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946), for the proposition that tribal powers are not absolute and are subject to congressional authority. While true, this principle does not absolve the State of its obligation to respect tribal sovereignty where the State has voluntarily chosen to extend reciprocity to other jurisdictions. The State’s decision to exempt residents of foreign provinces like Ontario demonstrates that it has the capacity to recognize non-state regulatory schemes. To exclude the Band—a distinct political community exercising governmental authority over its territory—without a substantial justification, is arbitrary and discriminatory.

**C. Evaluation of the Defense’s Justifications**

The defense argues that the Band is not similarly situated to states or foreign provinces because its fee structure differs and it does not exempt Minnesota residents. However, the defense has failed to cite any precedent establishing that a difference in fee structure constitutes a "substantial character" justification for undermining tribal sovereignty. The defense cites *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Indian Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134, 155 (1980), to argue that the State’s interest in managing its highway system justifies the disparity. Yet, *Colville* involved a balance of interests regarding taxation, and here, the State’s own policy is to promote the "fullest possible use of its highway system" through reciprocity. Excluding the Band does not advance this policy; rather, it hinders the economic and social development of the region.

Furthermore, the defense’s reliance on *New Mexico v. Mescalero Apache Tribe*, 462 U.S. 324, 334 (1983), regarding the balancing of wildlife management interests, is inapposite. The regulation of motor vehicles on public highways is a matter of public safety and commerce, where uniform recognition of licenses serves the public interest. The State offers no evidence that recognizing the Band’s registration would compromise highway safety or revenue collection in a way that recognizing Ontario’s registration does not.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The State of Minnesota has broad authority to regulate its highways, but that authority is not unlimited when it conflicts with the inherent sovereignty of Indian tribes. The State has chosen to extend reciprocity to a wide range of jurisdictions, including foreign provinces. Having done so, it cannot deny that same privilege to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians absent a substantial justification. The record is devoid of such justification. To the contrary, the evidence suggests that granting reciprocity would further the economic and social development of the State.

Accordingly, the judgment of the district court is AFFIRMED.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

## United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit

### Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians v. State of Minnesota

## INTRODUCTION

The State of Minnesota respectfully submits this rebuttal to the plaintiff's attempt to affirm the district court's judgment. Contrary to the appellate ruling, the State contends that the court erred in its application of tribal sovereignty principles and in its interpretation of Minnesota's reciprocity statutes. The State requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and dismiss the Band's complaint.

---

## I. THE APPELLATE COURT ERRED IN FINDING THAT THE BAND IS COMPARABLE TO STATES AND FOREIGN PROVINCES

The central error in the lower court's analysis was its flawed comparison between the Red Lake Band and the sovereign entities enumerated in Minnesota's reciprocity statutes. The appellate court incorrectly concluded that the Band is "similarly situated" to states, the District of Columbia, and foreign provinces such as Ontario.

### A. The Statutory Text Does Not Include Tribes

Minnesota Statutes Section 168.181 authorizes agreements with "other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces." The plain language of this statute makes clear that the Minnesota Legislature intended to extend reciprocity only to governmental entities that are either states of the Union, foreign nation-states, or their subdivisions. Indian tribes are not mentioned because they were not intended to be included.

In *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 687 (1993), the Supreme Court recognized that "tribes are not states" and that "the unique sovereign status of tribes has been acknowledged by this Court since the founding of the Republic." The appellate court's conflation of tribal governance with state and foreign provincial governance contradicts this fundamental principle.

### B. The Band's Ordinance Lacks True Reciprocity

Even if this Court were to find that tribes could potentially qualify for reciprocity under the statutes, the Band's ordinance fails the basic requirement of reciprocity. The statute authorizes exemptions to promote "reciprocal recognition of vehicle registration" and to encourage "the fullest possible use of its highway system" through mutual accommodation.

The Band's ordinance imposes "a substantial annual registration fee on all motor vehicles" and provides no exemption whatsoever for Minnesota residents who drive on the reservation. This is fundamentally different from the reciprocal agreements with Wisconsin, Ontario, and other jurisdictions, where the mutual recognition serves the interests of both parties in facilitating commerce and travel.

As the Supreme Court held in *California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians*, 480 U.S. 202, 216 (1987), "state jurisdiction is pre-empted by federal law" only when tribal activities "threaten to undermine the state's ability to maintain fiscal integrity." Here, the Band's non-reciprocal fee structure demonstrates that it seeks not mutual governance but rather an exemption from state law without corresponding obligations.

---

## II. THE APPELLATE COURT IMPROPERLY SHIFTED THE BURDEN OF PROOF TO THE STATE

The lower court's framework erroneously placed upon the State the burden of demonstrating "considerations of a substantial character" to justify treating the Band differently from other jurisdictions. This represents a fundamental misapplication of law.

### A. The Band Bears the Burden of Establishing a Right to Exemption

Under basic principles of administrative law and tribal sovereignty, the party seeking to enjoin state action bears the burden of establishing that such action violates federal law. See *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 561 (1992). The Band has not demonstrated that Minnesota's refusal to extend reciprocity violates any federal statute or treaty right.

The Band's reliance on *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217 (1959), is misplaced. That case involved the application of state court jurisdiction over a civil dispute arising on the reservation—a clear intrusion on tribal sovereignty. Here, by contrast, Minnesota is merely regulating the use of its own highways by drivers who seek to use public roads maintained with state resources. The State is not asserting jurisdiction over the Band's internal affairs; it is exercising its inherent police powers over state highways.

### B. The State's Highway Regulation Is a Compelling Interest

The State has a fundamental interest in maintaining its highway system and ensuring the safety of all users of public roads. This interest is sufficient to justify requiring all drivers using Minnesota highways to comply with state registration requirements.

In *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. at 695, the Court recognized that "the Tribes' sovereign immunity from suit" does not preclude states from exercising regulatory jurisdiction over matters of legitimate state concern. The regulation of motor vehicle registration on state highways is precisely such a matter.

The State's interest is analogous to the wildlife management interests at issue in *New Mexico v. Mescalero Apache Tribe*, 462 U.S. 324, 334 (1983), where the Court held that "the State has a legitimate interest in protecting its wildlife resources" and that "tribal sovereignty is not absolute." The same principle applies here: the State's interest in highway safety and revenue collection for road maintenance is a legitimate governmental interest that outweighs the Band's claimed right to have its licenses recognized without meeting state requirements.

---

## III. THE APPELLATE COURT IGNORED THE DOCTRINE OF PREEMPTION AND CONGRESSIONAL PLENARY POWER

The lower court failed to properly consider the doctrine of federal preemption and Congress's plenary authority over Indian affairs.

### A. Federal Law Does Not Require State Recognition of Tribal Licenses

The Band has not identified any federal statute or regulation that requires Minnesota to recognize tribal motor vehicle registrations. The approval of the Band's ordinance by the Secretary of the Interior does not create such a requirement. As the Court held in *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946), "tribal powers are not absolute" and are "subject to congressional authority."

Congress has not mandated that states recognize tribal vehicle registrations, and the Secretary's approval of the Band's ordinance does not preempt state law. The Band's attempt to use the Secretary's approval as a sword against state regulation exceeds the scope of that approval.

### B. The State Has Not Waived Its Sovereign Immunity

The appellate court's reasoning implicitly treats the State's voluntary extension of reciprocity to some jurisdictions as a waiver of its right to refuse reciprocity to others. This is incorrect.

In *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Indian Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134, 155 (1980), the Court recognized that "the State has not waived its sovereign immunity" through the Indian Trader Statutes or otherwise. The same principle applies here: the State's decision to extend reciprocity to certain jurisdictions does not constitute a waiver of its right to refuse reciprocity to the Band.

The State's reciprocity agreements are bilateral arrangements based on mutual benefits. The Band's ordinance, which imposes fees on all vehicles without reciprocating, does not offer the mutual benefits that justify the State's extension of reciprocity to other jurisdictions.

## IV. THE APPELLATE COURT'S REMEDY IS IMPROPER AND UNWORKABLE

Even if the Band were entitled to some form of relief, the remedy ordered by the district court and affirmed by the appellate court is unworkable and beyond the court's authority.

### A. The Court Cannot Compel the State to Enter into Reciprocity Agreements

The district court ordered the State to "afford the Red Lake Band the same privileges it grants to other comparable governing entities." This is effectively a mandatory injunction requiring the State to enter into a reciprocity agreement with the Band. However, the decision of whether to enter into such agreements is a political question committed to the executive branch, not the judiciary.

In *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 217 (1962), the Court held that political questions are nonjusticiable because they involve "the discretion of the executive." The negotiation of reciprocity agreements is precisely such a discretionary executive function.

### B. The Ordered Remedy Creates Practical Problems

If the State were compelled to recognize the Band's registration, it would create significant practical problems. The Band's registration system does not meet Minnesota's requirements for titling, security interests, and record-keeping. Forcing the State to recognize Band registrations would undermine the integrity of Minnesota's vehicle registration system and potentially enable fraud.

These verification issues are precisely why the State requires foreign jurisdictions to enter into formal reciprocity agreements with verification mechanisms.

**PER CURIAM.**

Upon review of the rebuttals submitted by both parties, we reconsider the district court’s judgment granting injunctive and declaratory relief to the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians. Consequently, we reverse the judgment of the district court.

**I. STATUTORY INTERPRETATION AND THE SCOPE OF RECIPROCITY**

The central issue is whether Minnesota’s reciprocity statutes, Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, obligate the State to exempt Red Lake Band members from motor vehicle registration fees. The district court’s analysis focused heavily on equitable considerations and the "dilution" of tribal authority. However, the State’s rebuttal correctly identifies that the starting point of any such analysis must be the plain language of the statute.

Minn. Stat. § 168.181 authorizes the Commissioner of Public Safety to enter into agreements with "other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces." The statute does not mention Indian tribes. In *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 687 (1993), the Supreme Court recognized that "tribes are not states" and possess a unique sovereign status distinct from that of states and their subdivisions. The Minnesota Legislature’s specific enumeration of eligible entities—omitting tribes—indicates a deliberate choice to limit reciprocity to those listed jurisdictions. The Band’s argument that it is a "comparable governing entity" cannot override the clear statutory text. See *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946) (noting that tribal powers are subject to and defined by federal authority and statutory limitations).

**II. THE REQUIREMENT OF RECIPROCITY**

Even if the statute were ambiguous regarding the inclusion of tribes, the State’s rebuttal establishes that the Band fails to satisfy the fundamental requirement of reciprocity. The State’s policy, as declared by the legislature, is to promote the "fullest possible use of its highway system" through "reciprocal recognition of vehicle registration." Reciprocity inherently involves mutual concession; the State exempts residents of other jurisdictions because those jurisdictions exempt Minnesota residents.

The record shows that the Red Lake Band’s ordinance "imposes a substantial annual registration fee on all motor vehicles using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake reservation." There is no indication that the Band exempts Minnesota residents from this fee. Therefore, the relationship is not reciprocal. The Band seeks the benefit of exemption without assuming the burden of exempting non-members. As noted in *California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians*, 480 U.S. 202, 216 (1987), state jurisdiction is generally preempted only when tribal activities threaten state interests in a way that justifies federal intervention. Here, the State’s interest in maintaining a reciprocal system for the mutual benefit of its citizens is a legitimate regulatory objective. The Band’s non-reciprocal fee structure justifies the State’s refusal to grant an exemption.

**III. STATE INTERESTS AND THE BURDEN OF PROOF**

The district court placed the burden on the State to demonstrate "considerations of a substantial character" justifying the disparity. The State correctly argues that this misplaces the burden. Under *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 561 (1992), the plaintiff seeking injunctive relief bears the burden of establishing a violation of federal law. The Band has not identified a federal statute or treaty right that compels Minnesota to recognize tribal vehicle registrations absent a reciprocal agreement.

Furthermore, the State has a compelling interest in regulating motor vehicles on its highways to ensure safety and generate revenue for road maintenance. This interest is analogous to the state’s interest in protecting wildlife resources upheld in *New Mexico v. Mescalero Apache Tribe*, 462 U.S. 324, 334 (1983). The State’s police power over its highways is a fundamental attribute of sovereignty that is not nullified by the existence of a tribal ordinance. See *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Indian Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134, 155 (1980) (recognizing the state’s interest in collecting taxes from non-members).

**IV. THE REMEDY**

Finally, the district court’s remedy—effectively ordering the State to afford the Band the same privileges as other jurisdictions—is unworkable. The decision to enter into a reciprocity agreement is a discretionary executive function. As held in *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 217 (1962), the negotiation of such agreements presents a political question committed to the executive branch, not the judiciary. The court cannot compel the State to enter into an agreement where the statutory prerequisites—specifically, reciprocity—are not met.

**V. CONCLUSION**

The State of Minnesota has established that its reciprocity statutes do not encompass Indian tribes and that the Red Lake Band’s ordinance fails to meet the statutory requirement of reciprocity. The State’s refusal to exempt Band members from registration fees is a valid exercise of its police power and does not violate federal law. The equitable concerns raised by the Band cannot override the clear statutory mandate and the absence of a reciprocal obligation.

Accordingly, the judgment of the district court is **REVERSED**.

# DEFENSE CLOSING STATEMENT

## United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit

### Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians v. State of Minnesota

---

## INTRODUCTION

The defense respectfully submits that the plaintiff-appellant's closing statement, while eloquently framed, fundamentally misapprehends the applicable legal framework and the limited scope of this Court's review. The State of Minnesota's refusal to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band is firmly grounded in statutory text, the absence of reciprocal obligations, and legitimate state interests. This Court should affirm the reversal of the district court's judgment and uphold the State's exercise of its regulatory authority.

---

## I. THE PLAINTIFF'S STATUTORY INTERPRETATION IGNORES CLEAR LEGISLATIVE INTENT

The plaintiff contends that Minnesota's reciprocity statutes, Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, should be interpreted to include Indian tribes despite their explicit omission from the statutory text. This argument cannot prevail.

### A. The Plain Language of the Statute Controls

Minnesota's legislature deliberately enumerated the entities eligible for reciprocity agreements: "other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces." The absence of Indian tribes from this list reflects a conscious legislative choice, not an inadvertent omission. As this Court recognized in its updated ruling, "the Minnesota Legislature's specific enumeration of eligible entities—omitting tribes—indicates a deliberate choice to limit reciprocity to those listed jurisdictions."

In *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 687 (1993), the Supreme Court explicitly recognized that "tribes are not states" and possess a unique sovereign status distinct from that of states and their subdivisions. The plaintiff cannot overcome clear statutory text by invoking general principles of tribal sovereignty.

### B. The Statute's Purpose Does Not Override Its Text

The plaintiff argues that the statute's purpose—"to promote and encourage the fullest possible use of its highway system"—requires including tribes. However, the legislature expressly limited this policy to "reciprocal recognition of vehicle registration" with specifically designated entities. The policy declaration does not create a mandate to extend reciprocity to every sovereign entity that might request it. The plaintiff asks this Court to rewrite the statute to include tribes, but "courts are not authorized to insert language that the legislature deliberately omitted." *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946).

---

## II. THE PLAINTIFF'S RECIPROCITY ARGUMENT FAILS ON ITS OWN TERMS

The plaintiff contends that reciprocity is satisfied because the Band regulates vehicles on the reservation while Minnesota regulates vehicles on state highways—that these are "distinct regulatory spheres." This argument contradicts the very nature of reciprocity as defined by Minnesota law.

### A. Reciprocity Requires Mutual Concession

Minnesota's reciprocity policy exists to secure "mutual concession" between jurisdictions. The State exempts residents of other jurisdictions because those jurisdictions exempt Minnesota residents. This mutual benefit is the foundation of the reciprocity system.

The record establishes that the Red Lake Band's ordinance "imposes a substantial annual registration fee on all motor vehicles using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake reservation." Critically, there is no evidence that the Band exempts Minnesota residents from this fee. The Band seeks the benefit of exemption from Minnesota's fees without providing any corresponding benefit to Minnesota residents. This is not reciprocity; it is a one-sided demand for special treatment.

### B. The Band's Regulatory Distinction Is Irrelevant

The plaintiff's attempt to distinguish between "regulatory spheres" misunderstands the practical operation of reciprocity. When a Minnesota resident drives on the Red Lake reservation, they are subject to the Band's registration fee. When a Band member drives on Minnesota highways, they are subject to Minnesota's registration fee. The Band's ordinance creates a reciprocal burden, not a reciprocal exemption.

The plaintiff argues that "reciprocity here is simply about recognizing the Band's registration for vehicles on Minnesota highways." This is precisely the problem—the Band seeks recognition without offering recognition in return. Such a one-sided arrangement is antithetical to the reciprocity framework that Minnesota has established.

---

## III. THE STATE HAS LEGITIMATE INTERESTS THAT SUPPORT ITS POSITION

The plaintiff dismisses the State's interests in safety and revenue collection as insufficient to justify burdening tribal self-government. However, the State's interests are substantial and legitimate.

### A. The State's Police Power Over Highways Is Fundamental

The State of Minnesota has a compelling interest in regulating motor vehicles on its highways to ensure safety and generate revenue for road maintenance. This interest is a fundamental attribute of state sovereignty. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Indian Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134, 155 (1980), "the State has a valid statutory scheme for taxation" and "the State has a strong interest in enforcing itslicensing scheme."

The State's interest here is analogous to the interests recognized in *New Mexico v. Mescalero Apache Tribe*, 462 U.S. 324, 334 (1983), where the Court upheld state jurisdiction over tribal members engaged in hunting and fishing on non-reservation lands. The State may legitimately regulate activities on its own highways regardless of the regulatory framework tribal members may have at home.

### B. The State Does Not Bear the Burden of Justification

The plaintiff incorrectly asserts that the State must demonstrate a "compelling interest" to justify its refusal to grant reciprocity. This misplaces the burden of proof. Under *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 561 (1992), the party seeking injunctive relief bears the burden of establishing a violation of federal law.

The Band has not identified any federal statute or treaty provision that compels Minnesota to recognize tribal vehicle registrations absent a reciprocal agreement. The plaintiff cites *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217, 223 (1959), for the proposition that "state law does not apply to Indians on reservations when it interferes with tribal self-governance." However, *Williams v. Lee* addressed the application of state court jurisdiction over civil suits involving Indians, not the state's authority to regulate on state highways. The plaintiff conflates tribal internal governance with the state's authority over state-owned infrastructure.

The State's refusal to grant reciprocity is not an "assault on the Band's inherent sovereignty"—it is an exercise of the State's own sovereignty over its highway system. The Band remains free to regulate vehicles on its reservation; Minnesota is not required to subsidize that regulation by exempting Band members from state fees.

## IV. THE REMEDY SOUGHT IS IMPROPER AND UNWORKABLE

The plaintiff dismisses the defense's characterization of the remedy as "unworkable" as a "red herring." To the contrary, the district court's remedy improperly intrudes on executive branch authority.

### A. Reciprocity Agreements Are Discretionary

The decision to enter into a reciprocity agreement is a discretionary executive function. As this Court held in its updated ruling, citing *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 217 (1962), "the negotiation of such agreements presents a political question committed to the executive branch, not the judiciary."

The district court's injunction effectively orders the State to treat the Band as it treats other jurisdictions "under its own statutes." However, the statutes do not include tribes. The court cannot compel the State to extend privileges that the legislature has not authorized.

### B. The Plaintiff Seeks a Political Solution to a Political Problem

The plaintiff asks this Court to order Minnesota to grant reciprocity to the Band. However, the proper avenue for obtaining such reciprocity is negotiation with the executive branch, not litigation in the courts. If the Band desires reciprocity, it should seek to negotiate a reciprocity agreement with Minnesota, just as other jurisdictions do.

For the foregoing reasons, the defense respectfully requests that this Court affirm the reversal of the district court's judgment. The State of Minnesota's reciprocity statutes do not include Indian tribes, the Band has not established reciprocity, and the State's refusal to grant an exemption is a valid exercise of its regulatory authority. The plaintiff's arguments, while framed in the language of tribal sovereignty, do not identify any federal law that compels Minnesota to extend reciprocity to the Red Lake Band. **PER CURIAM.**

Upon consideration of the closing arguments submitted by the Red Lake Band of Chippewa Indians (the "Band") and the State of Minnesota, we reaffirm our decision to reverse the judgment of the district court. While the Band raises significant concerns regarding the preservation of tribal self-government, the State’s statutory framework and the absence of a reciprocal agreement compel the conclusion that Minnesota is not obligated to extend vehicle registration exemptions to the Band under the current laws.

**I. STATORY TEXT AND LEGISLATIVE INTENT**

The Band argues that Minnesota’s reciprocity statutes, Minn. Stat. §§ 168.181 and 168.187, should be interpreted to include Indian tribes to fulfill the statutory purpose of promoting the "fullest possible use of its highway system." However, this argument cannot overcome the plain language of the statutes. The legislature explicitly authorized the Commissioner of Public Safety to enter into agreements with "other states, the District of Columbia, territories and possessions of the United States, and foreign countries or provinces." The omission of Indian tribes from this enumeration is deliberate.

As the Supreme Court noted in *South Dakota v. Bourland*, 508 U.S. 679, 687 (1993), "tribes are not states" and possess a unique status distinct from the entities listed in the statute. The principle of *expressio unius est exclusio alterius*—the expression of one thing is the exclusion of another—applies. By specifically listing the jurisdictions with which Minnesota may contract, the legislature indicated its intent to exclude all others. As held in *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946), courts are not authorized to insert language that the legislature has deliberately omitted. The Band’s request for this Court to rewrite the statute to include tribes is denied.

**II. THE ABSENCE OF RECIPROCITY**

The concept of reciprocity is central to this dispute. Minnesota’s policy is based on mutual concession: the State exempts residents of other jurisdictions because those jurisdictions exempt Minnesota residents. The Band contends that reciprocity exists because the two entities regulate in "distinct regulatory spheres"—the Band on the reservation and the State on state highways. This interpretation is flawed.

The record establishes that the Band’s ordinance "imposes a substantial annual registration fee on all motor vehicles using the public streets or highways of the Red Lake reservation." There is no evidence that the Band exempts Minnesota residents from this fee. Consequently, when a Minnesota resident drives on the reservation, they are subject to the Band’s fee, but when a Band member drives in Minnesota, the State demands a second fee. This is not a reciprocal relationship; it is a unilateral imposition of costs on Band members who use state highways without a corresponding benefit to Minnesota residents.

The Band seeks the privilege of exemption without assuming the burden of exempting non-members. This one-sided arrangement does not satisfy the statutory requirement of "reciprocal recognition." The State’s refusal to grant an exemption in the absence of such reciprocity is consistent with the logic of *Washington v. Confederated Tribes of Colville Indian Reservation*, 447 U.S. 134, 155 (1980), where the Court recognized the state’s interest in ensuring that those who benefit from state services contribute to the cost of those services.

**III. TRIBAL SOVEREIGNTY VERSUS STATE POLICE POWER**

The Band relies heavily on *Williams v. Lee*, 358 U.S. 217, 223 (1959), arguing that state law cannot apply on the reservation to interfere with tribal self-government. However, *Williams* and its progeny prevent states from infringing on the *internal* sovereignty of the tribe. They do not strip the State of its authority to regulate motor vehicle safety and revenue collection on *state* highways.

The State’s interest in maintaining a safe and funded highway system is a fundamental attribute of its sovereignty. As noted in *New Mexico v. Mescalero Apache Tribe*, 462 U.S. 324, 334 (1983), state interests may outweigh tribal interests when the state acts outside the reservation regarding matters of legitimate local concern. Here, the State is regulating vehicles traveling on its own public roads. The Band’s sovereignty allows it to register vehicles on the reservation, but that sovereignty does not automatically invalidate the State’s registration requirements for travel outside the reservation.

**IV. BURDEN OF PROOF AND JUDICIAL REMEDY**

The Band seeks an injunction compelling the State to afford it the same privileges as states and foreign provinces. Under *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 561 (1992), the plaintiff seeking such relief bears the burden of establishing a violation of federal law. The Band has not identified a federal statute or treaty that compels Minnesota to recognize tribal registrations absent a reciprocal agreement. General principles of tribal sovereignty are insufficient to override the specific statutory text enacted by the State legislature.

Furthermore, the remedy sought by the Band is inappropriate for the judiciary. The decision to enter into a reciprocity agreement is a discretionary executive function. As held in *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 217 (1962), the negotiation of such agreements presents a political question committed to the executive branch. The district court’s injunction effectively ordered the State to treat the Band as a "state" for reciprocity purposes, despite the legislature’s decision to the contrary. This exceeds the authority of the federal courts.

**V. CONCLUSION**

The State of Minnesota has clearly defined the entities eligible for vehicle registration reciprocity, and Indian tribes are not included. The Red Lake Band has not established a reciprocal relationship with the State, as its ordinance imposes fees on all vehicles using reservation roads, including those owned by Minnesota residents. The State’s refusal to exempt Band members from registration fees is a valid exercise of its police power and does not violate federal law.

Accordingly, the judgment of the district court is **REVERSED**.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
