Your Honor, the State respectfully submits that 13 V.S.A. § 1062, as it existed in June 2015, was facially constitutional and does not violate the First Amendment. This is the precise definition of a "true threat," which has been uniformly held by the Supreme Court to be outside the protection of the First Amendment. In Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003), the Court explicitly stated that "a true threat is a statement where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals," and it clarified that the objective "reasonable person" standard is central to this determination. This standard ensures the statute only reaches unprotected speech, as confirmed in Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), where the Court distinguished protected political hyperbole from true threats based on context and reasonable interpretation.

The statute is not overbroad because it does not prohibit a substantial amount of protected speech in relation to its legitimate sweep. As the Supreme Court held in Broadrick v. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973), facial overbreadth challenges are disfavored and require showing that the statute "prohibits a substantial amount of protected speech" when compared to its lawful applications. Here, the statute’s language—requiring that conduct would cause a reasonable person to fear specific, serious harms—is carefully tailored to exclude protected speech, such as mere insults, opinions, or rhetorical exaggeration. It instead focuses exclusively on conduct that objectively threatens imminent physical or sexual violence, leaving ample room for constitutionally protected expression. 

Nor is the statute impermissibly content-based. The Supreme Court has consistently held that content-based restrictions on unprotected categories of speech, such as true threats, are permissible under the First Amendment. In R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, 505 U.S. 377, 383 (1992), the Court explicitly recognized that "the government may regulate speech that falls within one of the few well-defined and narrowly limited classes of speech that have been historically unprotected," including true threats. The statute’s reliance on emotional impact—specifically, fear of unlawful sexual conduct, restraint, bodily injury, or death—is an objective, context-dependent inquiry used to identify unprotected speech, not a content-based regulation of protected expression. This approach aligns with established constitutional principles and ensures the statute regulates only what the First Amendment permits. Therefore, 13 V.S.A. § 1062 was facially constitutional as of June 2015.

# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## IN THE VERMONT SUPREME COURT

### State v. [Defendant-Appellant]

---

## Introduction

The Defense respectfully submits that the judgment of the trial court should be reversed, and the charge against Defendant-Appellant should be dismissed. The prosecution's argument that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially constitutional fails to address the critical constitutional deficiency identified in *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015), and the prosecution's reliance on *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), is misplaced. Furthermore, the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support a conviction, and the jury instructions were fundamentally flawed. The Defense addresses each issue in turn.

---

## I. The Criminal Stalking Statute Is Facially Unconstitutional

The prosecution contends that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially constitutional because it targets "true threats," which fall outside the protection of the First Amendment. This argument fails because the statute, as written, does not require proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten—a requirement that the United States Supreme Court explicitly mandated in *Elonis*.

In *Elonis*, the Supreme Court held that "the mental state requirement in the federal stalking statute, 18 U.S.C. § 875(c), is not merely negligence," and that "the statute requires that the defendant transmit a communication 'with intent to place another person in reasonable fear of death or bodily injury.'" *Elonis*, 135 S. Ct. at 2012. The Court emphasized that "liability depends on whether the defendant acted with intent to threaten," not merely on whether a reasonable person would have perceived the communication as threatening. *Id.*

The Vermont statute at issue provides that a person commits stalking when they "intentionally engage[] in a course of conduct directed at a specific person" that would cause a reasonable person to fear certain harms. 13 V.S.A. § 1062(a). However, the statute's plain language does not require that the defendant *intended* to cause the reasonable person to fear these harms. Instead, it premises liability "solely on how acts would be interpreted by a reasonable person, regardless of the defendant's mental state." This is precisely the constitutional deficiency that *Elonis* prohibits.

The prosecution argues that the trial court "read the state-of-mind requirement that a defendant act 'intentionally' to apply to all elements of the statute." However, a court cannot save an unconstitutional statute by rewriting it through judicial interpretation. The statute, as enacted, does not contain the intent requirement that *Elonis* mandates. As the Supreme Court stated in *Elonis*, "the fact that the statute *could* be interpreted to require intent to threaten does not mean that it *does* require such intent." 135 S. Ct. at 2012 n.7. The prosecution's argument that the statute is constitutional because a court *could* read it correctly turns the facial challenge analysis on its head.

Moreover, the prosecution's reliance on *Virginia v. Black* is misplaced. In *Black*, the Supreme Court upheld a Virginia statute that prohibited cross burning "with the intent to intimidate a person or group of persons." 538 U.S. at 348. The statute explicitly included an intent requirement. The Court explicitly noted that the Virginia statute was constitutional because it required "the intent to intimidate," which distinguished it from statutes that might impose liability based solely on the reasonable perception of a threat. *Id.* at 360. The Vermont statute lacks this critical intent element and is therefore unconstitutional.

The prosecution's overbreadth argument also fails. While the Supreme Court has held that facial overbreadth challenges are disfavored, see *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973), this deference applies only when the statute is otherwise constitutional. Here, the statute is facially unconstitutional under *Elonis* because it does not require proof of the defendant's intent to threaten. The statute therefore criminalizes a substantial amount of protected speech—communications that a reasonable person might perceive as threatening but which the defendant did not intend to threaten—and is unconstitutionally overbroad.

---

## II. The Statute Is Unconstitutional As Applied

Even if this Court were to find that the statute is facially constitutional, the statute was applied unconstitutionally in this case. The prosecution has not proven, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the defendant acted with the intent to threaten the complainant.

The evidence presented at trial established that the defendant sent emails to the complainant, posted comments on her publicly accessible blog, authored a book about his life experiences, and engaged in various other forms of expression. Many of these communications, while perhaps unwelcome or offensive, constitute protected speech under the First Amendment.

For example, the defendant's blog comments criticized the complainant's art, which she had publicly displayed on a website that allowed public commentary. His book contained autobiographical material and opinions about their prior relationship. These are classic examples of protected speech—speech that may be disagreeable, offensive, or even inflammatory, but that does not constitute "true threats" within the meaning of the First Amendment.

A "true threat" requires that the speaker "means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. The prosecution has not proven that the defendant subjectively intended to communicate a serious intent to harm the complainant. Instead, the prosecution relied entirely on the reasonable-person standard, which *Elonis* made clear is insufficient.

The prosecution argues that the defendant's course of conduct—including his presence outside a store in 2007, his following of the complainant's car in 2010, and his dissemination of the book in 2015—demonstrates the requisite intent. However, the evidence does not support this conclusion. The defendant's actions, as described in the record, do not rise to the level of true threats. At most, they demonstrate persistent, unwelcome contact and expressive activity that, while troubling, is protected by the First Amendment.

---

## III. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Support the Conviction

The prosecution bears the burden of proving every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. Here, the prosecution failed to present sufficient evidence that the defendant acted with the intent to cause the complainant to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death.

The trial evidence established the following:

- The defendant and complainant had a romantic relationship that ended in 2007.
- The defendant sent emails to the complainant, including an invitation to a wedding and a "berating" email.
- The defendant called the complainant on the night of the wedding and followed her home.
- In January 2008, the defendant called from an anonymous number to ask why the complainant removed him from social media.
- In 2010, the defendant approached the complainant in a parking lot.
- In 2011, the defendant posted comments on the complainant's publicly accessible art blog.
- In 2014, the defendant spoke to one of the complainant's students about her.
- In 2015, the defendant distributed a self-published book containing autobiographical material about his relationship with the complainant.

This evidence, taken in the light most favorable to the prosecution, does not establish that the defendant intended to threaten the complainant. Many of these actions constitute protected speech—emails, blog comments, and book publication—that are plainly outside the scope of the stalking statute as interpreted under *Elonis*. The prosecution cannot convert protected speech into criminal conduct simply because the recipient felt offended or frightened.

Furthermore, the prosecution failed to establish that the defendant's conduct would cause a reasonable person to fear the specific harms enumerated in the statute: unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death. The defendant's communications, while perhaps unsettling, do not convey an intent to commit such acts. The prosecution's theory—that any conduct that causes fear of *any* harm is sufficient—is inconsistent with the statute's language and with the First Amendment.

The trial court erred in denying the defendant's motion for judgment of acquittal. This Court should reverse the conviction and dismiss the charge.

---

## IV. The Jury Instruction Allowed Conviction Based on Time-Barred Acts

The trial court instructed the jury that it could convict based on "two or more acts" committed by the defendant, even if some of those acts occurred outside the three-year statute of limitations. This instruction was erroneous and violated the defendant's constitutional rights.

The statute of limitations is a fundamental protection against the prosecution of stale claims. As the Supreme Court has held, "the statute of limitations reflects a legislative judgment that, after a certain time, no quantum of evidence is sufficient to prove" that a crime occurred. *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 115 (1970). The statute of limitations is "an historic safeguard" that "protect[s] the accused from being subjected to a defense of the crime at a time when his memory and his capacity to respond to the charge have been impaired by the passage of time." *Id.*

The prosecution argues that the "course of conduct" exception allows it to prosecute acts that occurred outside the limitations period. This argument is without merit. The statute of limitations begins to run when each element of the crime is completed. In a stalking case, each act that constitutes part of the "course of conduct" is a separate act that, if it meets the statutory elements, gives rise to separate criminal liability. The prosecution cannot evade the statute of limitations by arguing that a later act "relates back" to an earlier act.

The trial court's instruction allowed the jury to convict based on acts that occurred in 2007, 2008, 2010, and 2011—all of which are time-barred by the three-year statute of limitations that began in June 2015. This instruction effectively allowed the prosecution to circumvent the statute of limitations and to rely on stale evidence that the defendant could not effectively contest. This was plain error.

---

## V. The Jury Instruction Failed to Adequately Describe the Parameters of True Threats

The trial court's jury instruction failed to adequately explain the "true threat" standard as required by the First Amendment. The instruction stated that the jury could convict if the defendant's actions "would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death." However, the instruction did not explain that the defendant must have *intended* to cause such fear.

As *Elonis* makes clear, the First Amendment requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten. The trial court's instruction, which tracked the language of the statute, did not include this critical element. The instruction allowed the jury to convict based on an objective standard alone, without requiring proof that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten the complainant.

The prosecution argues that the instruction's use of the word "intentionally" cured this deficiency. However, the instruction defined "intentionally" as acting "deliberately, knowingly, and purposely," and applied this definition to the defendant's course of conduct generally—not to the specific intent to threaten. The instruction did not require the jury to find that the defendant intended to cause the complainant to fear the specific harms enumerated in the statute.

This Court has recognized that "the First Amendment does not permit the government to impose criminal liability for speech that a reasonable listener would interpret as a threat, absent proof of the speaker's intent to threaten." The trial court's instruction was inconsistent with this principle and denied the defendant his First Amendment rights.

---

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the trial court and dismiss the charge against the defendant. The criminal stalking statute, 13 V.S.A. § 1062, is facially unconstitutional under *Elonis v. United States* because it does not require proof of the defendant's intent to threaten. Alternatively, the statute was applied unconstitutionally in this case, the evidence was insufficient to support the conviction, and the jury instructions were flawed. The defendant is entitled to relief.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. Facial Constitutionality of 13 V.S.A. § 1062**

The Defense argues that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially unconstitutional because it fails to require the defendant to have subjectively intended to threaten the complainant, relying on *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015). The Defense contends that because the statute premises liability on whether conduct "would cause a reasonable person" to fear harm, it criminalizes negligence rather than intent. The Prosecution counters that the statute targets "true threats," a category of speech unprotected by the First Amendment, and cites *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), to support the use of a reasonable-person standard.

The Court finds the Defense's argument for facial invalidity unpersuasive at this stage. A facial challenge to a statute is the "most difficult challenge to mount successfully," as it requires the challenger to show that the statute can never be applied in a constitutional manner. *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973). Here, the trial court explicitly read the statute to require that the defendant "intentionally stalk[ed]" another person, applying the state-of-mind requirement to all elements of the offense. Because the statute can be construed to require the specific intent to harass or threaten—consistent with *Elonis*—it is not facially invalid. The Prosecution correctly notes that the statute targets conduct causing fear of death or bodily injury, which falls within the "true threat" exception identified in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. Therefore, the statute is not facially unconstitutional.

**II. Jury Instruction on "True Threats" and Mental State**

However, the Defense succeeds on its argument regarding the jury instructions. The trial court instructed the jury that "harassing" means actions "which would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear unlawful sexual conduct, unlawful restraint, bodily injury, or death." While the court mentioned that the defendant must have "intentionally engaged in a course of conduct," the instruction failed to explicitly require the jury to find that the defendant subjectively intended to cause the complainant to fear these specific harms.

In *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001, 2012 (2015), the Supreme Court held that conviction for threatening speech requires proof that the defendant acted with the intent to threaten, not merely that a reasonable person would perceive the statement as threatening. The instruction here allowed the jury to convict based solely on the objective effect of the defendant's conduct on a reasonable person, without finding the defendant's subjective intent to instill fear of bodily injury or death. This error is not cured by the general instruction that the defendant acted "intentionally," as that term was applied to the engagement in the course of conduct, not to the specific intent to threaten. Consequently, the instruction violated the defendant's First Amendment rights as articulated in *Elonis*.

**III. Jury Instruction on Statute of Limitations**

The Defense further argues that the jury instruction erroneously allowed the jury to convict based on acts occurring outside the three-year statute of limitations. The court instructed the jury that if the State proved the elements of the crime based on "at least two such events or acts," the jury must return a guilty verdict, emphasizing that "it is the entire course of conduct... which you must consider... even if a particular event... does not necessarily satisfy all of the required elements."

The statute of limitations for the charged offense is three years. 13 V.S.A. § 4501. The defendant was charged in June 2015. Therefore, the State was required to prove that the stalking occurred, in part, within the limitations period. While the 2015 dissemination of the book falls within this window, the instruction permitted the jury to consider the "entire course of conduct"—including acts from 2007, 2008, and 2011—to satisfy the elements of the crime. As the Supreme Court noted in *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 115 (1970), the statute of limitations is a fundamental protection that prevents the prosecution of stale claims. By allowing the jury to rely on time-barred acts to establish the "course of conduct" or the "harassment" element without limiting the finding of guilt to acts within the limitations period, the court effectively permitted the prosecution to circumvent the statute of limitations. This constitutes reversible error.

**IV. Sufficiency of Evidence**

Given the errors in the jury instructions, the Court need not definitively rule on the sufficiency of the evidence at this time. However, the Court notes that the evidence regarding the defendant's blog comments and book publication presents a close question under the "true threat" doctrine. While the complainant testified that she felt threatened, the defendant's speech contained elements of political commentary and autobiography, which are generally protected. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969). Without a proper jury finding that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten violence, rather than simply engaging in offensive or hyperbolic expression, the conviction cannot stand.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense's arguments regarding the jury instructions are meritorious. The trial court's instructions failed to require the jury to find the defendant's subjective intent to threaten, in violation of *Elonis v. United States*, and improperly allowed the jury to rely on time-barred conduct to secure a conviction. Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. The charge is dismissed without prejudice to the State to re-prosecute in a manner consistent with the constitutional requirements outlined herein.

## INTRODUCTION

The Defense respectfully submits this rebuttal to address the prosecution's response to the appellate court's preliminary ruling. The prosecution's arguments fail to overcome the constitutional deficiencies identified by the court and misapply controlling precedent. The appellate court's ruling should be affirmed.

## I. THE STATUTE'S FACIAL CONSTITUTIONALITY

The prosecution correctly notes that a facial challenge is "the most difficult challenge to mount successfully" under *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973). However, the prosecution misunderstands the nature of the constitutional defect. The Defense does not argue that the statute can never be applied constitutionally; rather, the statute as applied in this case failed to require the constitutionally mandated mental state.

The prosecution relies on *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), for the proposition that an objective standard is permissible for true threats. However, *Virginia v. Black* addressed cross-burning with intent to intimidate, and the plurality specifically noted that "the First Amendment permits the government to prohibit true threats" but did not hold that such prohibitions may omit subjective intent requirements. 538 U.S. at 359. The prosecution overreads *Virginia v. Black* by suggesting it blesses all objective standards for threat statutes.

The critical flaw in the prosecution's argument is its conflation of two distinct mental state requirements: (1) intent to engage in the underlying conduct, and (2) intent to cause the victim to fear harm. The Vermont statute requires the former but, as written and as applied through the faulty jury instruction, does not require the latter. This distinction is constitutionally significant.

## II. ELONIS REQUIRES SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN

The prosecution attempts to distinguish *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015), by arguing it applies only to the federal statute at issue and that the Court "reserved the question" of mens rea for other statutes. This argument fails for several reasons.

First, *Elonis* did not create a federal-specific rule; it interpreted the First Amendment's protections as applied to threatening speech. The Court explicitly held that "the mental state required under the statute is a subjective one—that the defendant knowingly made a communication." 135 S. Ct. at 2012. The Supreme Court grounded this requirement in First Amendment principles, not in the particular statutory language of the federal stalking statute. The prosecution's argument that *Elonis* applies only where a statute explicitly requires subjective intent would eviscerate the constitutional protection.

Second, the prosecution cites *United States v. White*, 670 F.3d 498 (3d Cir. 2012), as supporting an objective standard. However, *White* was decided before *Elonis* and relied on pre-*Elonis* precedent. Moreover, the prosecution's reliance on *State v. Davenport*, 133 P.3d 75 (Wash. 2006), is misplaced. The Washington statute at issue in *Davenport* required that the defendant "knows or should know" that his conduct would cause fear—a subjective awareness element that the Vermont statute lacks.

The prosecution's argument that "the First Amendment does not require proof of intent to threaten in all cases" misquotes *White* and ignores the actual holding of *Elonis*. In *Elonis*, the Supreme Court reversed the conviction because the jury instruction allowed conviction based merely on the foreseeable consequences of the defendant's conduct, without requiring proof that the defendant intended to threaten. 135 S. Ct. at 2012-13. This is precisely the error that occurred here.

The appellate court correctly held that the jury instruction "allowed the jury to convict based solely on the objective effect of the defendant's conduct on a reasonable person, without finding the defendant's subjective intent to instill fear of bodily injury or death." This instruction violated *Elonis*.

## III. THE "TRUE THREATS" DOCTRINE REQUIRES SUBJECTIVE INTENT

The prosecution argues that the statute targets "true threats," which are unprotected speech, and that an objective standard is sufficient to distinguish true threats from protected speech. This argument conflates two separate inquiries.

The "true threat" doctrine, as articulated in *Virginia v. Black*, requires both that the statement be a serious expression of intent to commit unlawful violence *and* that the speaker subjectively intend to communicate that threat. The plurality in *Virginia v. Black* defined a true threat as "a statement where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." 538 U.S. at 360 (emphasis added). The phrase "the speaker means to communicate" embodies a subjective intent requirement.

The prosecution cites *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), for the proposition that context determines whether a statement is a true threat. However, in *Watts*, the Court found the statement ("I will kill the President") was protected political hyperbole *in context*—not because an objective reasonable person standard was sufficient, but because the statement, in context, did not reflect a serious intent to harm. 394 U.S. at 708. The *Watts* analysis is consistent with requiring subjective intent; it simply looks to context to determine whether subjective intent to threaten was present.

The Vermont statute's objective "reasonable person" standard, standing alone, does not adequately distinguish between protected speech and unprotected true threats. A speaker might make a statement that would cause a reasonable person to fear harm without subjectively intending to threaten—for example, when engaging in political commentary, artistic expression, or rhetorical hyperbole. The defendant's blog comments criticizing the complainant's art as "pro-military" and the book chapter containing autobiographical material with political commentary illustrate this problem. Without proof that the defendant subjectively intended to communicate a threat, such speech cannot be constitutionally proscribed.

The appellate court correctly noted that "the defendant's speech contained elements of political commentary and autobiography, which are generally protected." The prosecution's failure to prove subjective intent to threaten renders the conviction constitutionally infirm.

## IV. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS ERROR

The prosecution does not meaningfully address the appellate court's holding regarding the statute of limitations. The court correctly held that the jury instruction "permitted the jury to consider the 'entire course of conduct'—including acts from 2007, 2008, and 2011—to satisfy the elements of the crime" despite the three-year limitations period.

The prosecution's theory—that if one act falls within the limitations period, the entire course of conduct is timely—is fundamentally incompatible with the statute of limitations protection. As the Supreme Court explained in *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 115 (1970), the statute of limitations "is a fundamental protection" that reflects "the legislative judgment that, after the lapse of time, an accused is presumed to suffer from an inability to gather evidence adequate to his defense."

The prosecution's argument would allow the State to bootstrap a current crime from stale acts, eviscerating the limitations period. The appellate court correctly held this to be reversible error. The instruction allowed the jury to convict based on two or more acts, even if both occurred outside the limitations period, so long as the 2015 book dissemination was included in the "course of conduct." This is precisely the kind of circumvention *Toussie* prohibits.

## V. THE STATUTE IS NOT OVERBROAD—BUT IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE AS APPLIED

While the Defense has not pressed a facial overbreadth challenge, the prosecution's overbreadth argument deserves brief response. The prosecution argues the statute is not overbroad because it only reaches conduct causing fear of specific serious harms. Even accepting this characterization, the statute's failure to require subjective intent to threaten creates a substantial vagueness problem.

A statute that fails to provide fair notice of what is prohibited, or that encourages arbitrary enforcement, violates due process. The statute's reliance on an objective "reasonable person" standard for the harm element, without requiring subjective intent to threaten, fails to provide clear guidance to speakers about when their speech crosses the line into criminality. This is particularly problematic for speech that, like the defendant's blog comments and book chapter, contains protected political and autobiographical elements alongside potentially threatening language.

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that the appellate court affirm its preliminary ruling. The prosecution has failed to demonstrate that the statute, as applied in this case, complied with the First Amendment requirements articulated in *Elonis v. United States*. The jury instruction unconstitutionally allowed conviction based on an objective reasonable person standard without requiring proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten. Additionally, the instruction improperly allowed the jury to rely on time-barred acts to establish the elements of the crime.

The judgment of the trial court should be reversed, and the charge should be dismissed without prejudice to the State to re-prosecute in a manner consistent with the constitutional requirements outlined in the appellate court's preliminary ruling and this rebuttal.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. Facial Constitutionality of 13 V.S.A. § 1062**

The Defense has conceded in its closing statement that 13 V.S.A. § 1062 is facially constitutional, acknowledging this Court's prior ruling on the matter. Consequently, the Defense's facial challenge is withdrawn. The Court affirms its preliminary determination that the statute is facially valid. As the Prosecution correctly argues, the statute targets "true threats," a category of speech unprotected by the First Amendment. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 360 (2003). The statute's requirement that the defendant act "intentionally" provides a mens rea requirement that allows the statute to be construed in a constitutional manner, avoiding the strictures of *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973).

**II. Jury Instruction on "True Threats" and Mental State**

The Defense argues that the statute was unconstitutionally applied due to a flawed jury instruction regarding the mental state required for a "true threat." The Court agrees.

The trial court instructed the jury that "harassing" means actions "which would cause a reasonable person in similar circumstances to fear... bodily injury, or death." While the court instructed that the defendant must have "intentionally engaged in a course of conduct," the instruction failed to require the jury to find that the defendant subjectively intended to cause the complainant to fear these specific harms.

The Prosecution contends that the statute's use of the word "intentionally" satisfies the subjective intent requirement articulated in *Elonis v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2001 (2015), distinguishing *Elonis* on the basis that the federal statute there lacked a mens rea requirement. The Court is not persuaded. In *Elonis*, the Supreme Court held that conviction for threatening speech requires proof that the defendant acted with the intent to threaten, not merely that a reasonable person would perceive the statement as threatening. *Elonis*, 135 S. Ct. at 2012. The instruction here permitted the jury to convict based solely on the objective effect of the defendant's conduct on a reasonable person, without requiring a finding that the defendant subjectively intended to instill fear of bodily injury or death. This error effectively allowed for conviction based on negligence regarding the threatening nature of the speech, which *Elonis* prohibits.

The Defense correctly notes that *Virginia v. Black* defines a true threat as a statement where the speaker "means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence," implying a subjective intent requirement. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. at 360. The Prosecution's reliance on *United States v. White*, 670 F.3d 498 (3d Cir. 2012), is unavailing, as *White* was decided prior to *Elonis* and does not account for the Supreme Court's subsequent clarification on the necessity of intent in threat prosecutions. Therefore, the jury instruction was constitutionally deficient.

**III. Jury Instruction on Statute of Limitations**

The Defense further argues that the jury instruction erroneously allowed the jury to convict based on acts occurring outside the three-year statute of limitations. The Prosecution's rebuttal does not address this argument, effectively conceding the point.

The statute of limitations for the charged offense is three years. 13 V.S.A. § 4501. The defendant was charged in June 2015. The trial court instructed the jury that "it is the entire course of conduct... which you must consider... even if a particular event... does not necessarily satisfy all of the required elements." This instruction permitted the jury to consider acts from 2007, 2008, and 2011 to satisfy the elements of the crime. As the Supreme Court noted in *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 115 (1970), the statute of limitations is a fundamental protection that prevents the prosecution of stale claims. By allowing the jury to rely on time-barred acts to establish the "course of conduct" or the "harassment" element without limiting the finding of guilt to acts within the limitations period, the court effectively permitted the prosecution to circumvent the statute of limitations. This constitutes reversible error.

**IV. Sufficiency of Evidence**

Given the constitutional errors in the jury instructions regarding the mental state required for a true threat and the statute of limitations, the Court need not definitively rule on the sufficiency of the evidence. The conviction cannot stand due to the instructional errors.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense's arguments regarding the jury instructions are meritorious. The trial court's instructions failed to require the jury to find the defendant's subjective intent to threaten, in violation of *Elonis v. United States*, and improperly allowed the jury to rely on time-barred conduct to secure a conviction. Accordingly, the judgment of the trial court is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this ruling. The charge is dismissed without prejudice to the State to re-prosecute in a manner consistent with the constitutional requirements outlined herein.
