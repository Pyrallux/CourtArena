I. Factual and Procedural History

{¶ 3} Given the summary nature of the proceedings below, the factual basis for the suit is vague. From the complaints filed in this case, it can be gleaned that Spit Shine alleges that it performed vehicle detailing services for two car dealerships, R.C. Honda and R.C. Hyundai. The complaints assert that Spit Shine entered into exclusive contracts with these two entities for car detailing services, but only attached a contract between R.C. Honda and "A One Detailers" to its first and second amended complaints. The contract does not set forth any terms for exclusivity. The complaints allege that Spit Shine, R.C. Honda, and R.C. Hyundai established a business relationship in 2011 where Spit Shine was the sole cleaning and detailing company used by the dealerships. In 2014, R.C. Honda stopped using Spit Shine for detailing services. In 2015, Spit Shine was no longer used for detailing services at either dealership, but it never received written notice of cancellation of the contracts, which required 30 days written notice.

{¶ 4} Spit Shine filed suit on September 2, 2016. Its initial complaint failed to attach any written agreement to it, and rather than answer, appellees filed a motion to dismiss or, in the alternative, for a more definite statement. Days later, Spit Shine filed its first amended complaint to which it attached the aforementioned 2013 contract. The trial court granted in part appellees' motions by ordering Spit Shine to file a second amended complaint clarifying paragraphs of the complaint that failed to distinguish between defendants, and that generally grouped all defendants together without distinguishing what each defendant allegedly did.

{¶ 5} Spit Shine filed a second amended complaint, and appellees filed another motion to dismiss. The trial court granted the motion to dismiss, finding [t]he complaint fails to state a claim upon which relief may be granted under Ohio law. The plaintiff instituting suit is named in the complaint's caption, "Spit Shine A One Detailer, LLC." Plaintiff's complaint alleges a breach of a contract. This contract on its face is between Milton Hall and Rick Case Honda. The complaint does not assert that Milton Hall is the same entity as the complaint's Spit Shine A One Detailer, LLC; and, it does not explain the relationship between those two legal persons such as, e.g., a successor-in-interest or "dba" entity. The plaintiff introduces unnecessary confusion on the issue of plaintiff's identity when it puts Spit Shine A One Detailer, LLC as the name of the plaintiff in the caption and in the introductory paragraph of the complaint which implies plaintiff is a corporate entity but then in paragraph one (1) alleges "Spit Shine A One Detailers is an individual * * *" the court assumes the latter paragraph of the complaint was not intended literally. Still the complaint does not allege the basis for the named plaintiff in the caption to have contract rights as against any party and it does not allege the basis for the named plaintiff to be entitled to bring a fraudulent representation action against the defendants. On this ground alone-as the analysis below further explains, the motion to dismiss under Civ.R. 12(b)(6) must be granted and the complaint dismissed.
The complaint fails to identify and attach a copy of the parties' alleged *1234contract. The contract attached is not identified as the contract alleged to have been breached by any defendant; it is not identified as the contract in force at the time of the breach of contract by any defendant. It is described merely as "a version" of the pertinent contract. The complaint alleges only that the contractual relationship commenced in 2011 and suit is now brought some five years later.
No further allegations in the complaint make the content of the attached contract relevant to the content of the contract alleged to have been breached; no further allegations in the complaint describe the content of the contract at issue, that is, the contract alleged to have existed between the parties which was alleged to have been breached.
There is no allegation in the complaint as to the contents of the subject contract (particularly to obligations of the respective parties and any significant terms thereof) or as to any other parties thereto. There is no way of knowing if the contract sued upon by the plaintiff bears any similarity to the contract annexed to the complaint.
Due to these failures, the motion to dismiss the complaint's count one must be granted under Civ.R. 12(b)(6).
Parties who do not have a copy of the contract sued upon are permitted to explain their failure to attach a copy of the contract sued upon to their complaint pursuant to Civ.R. 10(d). Plaintiff does not provide such explanation. On the latter basis alone, the motion to dismiss the complaint's count one must be granted under Civ.R. 12(b)(6).
Additionally, the complaint does not contain allegations to hold defendant Rick Case Automotive Group liable and contains no allegations that would make defendant Rick Case Automotive Group liable for the conduct of its co-defendants even though the complaint alleges those two co-defendants are subsidiaries of Rick Case Automotive Group. This alone is a basis to dismiss the entire complaint under Civ.R. 12(b)(6) against defendant Rick Case Automotive Group.
The motion to dismiss must likewise be granted as to the complaint's count two on fraudulent misrepresentation. Basically this count only states that defendants' "representatives" made a statement that was disproved by later events. Plaintiff does not allege the relationship facts that would turn lies or misleading statements into actionable misstatements. For example, the complaint does not allege the "representatives" were agents or servants of defendants; it does not allege defendants or their representatives had such a relationship with plaintiff that plaintiff was entitled to rely thereon and the "representatives" were obligated to avoid misleading one in plaintiff's position. These conclusions would not necessarily change even if the complaint squarely alleged a specific contract among the parties.
The foregoing does not analyze the ability of the plaintiff to sue for fraudulent misrepresentation in the presence of a written contract. Legal defenses such as the parole evidence rule may preclude such a claim. The complaint does not specify the terms of the subject contract and therefore such analysis is inappropriate under this motion. On November 21, 2016, the court provided the plaintiff with an opportunity to amend Count Two (2) to satisfy the particularity requirement of Civ.R. 9 and ordered plaintiff to file an amended complaint. In the complaint (filed after said order) plaintiff failed to describe the exact content of the parties' contract, identify the contractual relationship between plaintiff and each defendant, and specify the ways in which the fraud was committed.
*1235As a result the motion to dismiss count two of the complaint under Civ.R. 12(b)(6) is granted.

**OPENING ARGUMENT**  

Your Honors, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in dismissing Spit Shine’s complaint. To the contrary, the complaint was fatally defective on its face, failing to satisfy the most fundamental pleading requirements under Ohio law. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, a motion to dismiss under Civ.R. 12(B)(6) tests whether the complaint states a claim upon which relief can be granted—not whether the plaintiff will ultimately prevail. *State ex rel. Davis v. Pub. Emps. Retirement Sys.*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 259, 2005-Ohio-6088, ¶ 14. Here, Spit Shine’s allegations are riddled with fatal omissions: it failed to identify the proper party to the contract, did not properly attach or describe the alleged contract, neglected to plead fraud with the required particularity, and omitted any basis for holding Rick Case Automotive Group liable. These deficiencies alone warrant dismissal. I will demonstrate why the trial court’s ruling was not only correct but compelled by law.  

### I. The Complaint Failed to Allege a Viable Breach of Contract Claim Due to Inability to Identify the Proper Plaintiff and Contract  
Spit Shine’s breach of contract claim collapses at the outset because it cannot establish who was even a party to the alleged agreement. The complaint’s caption names "Spit Shine A One Detailer, LLC" as the plaintiff, yet paragraph one alleges "Spit Shine A One Detailers is an individual." This contradiction alone is fatal. More critically, the only contract attached to the complaint is between "Milton Hall and Rick Case Honda"—not Spit Shine. The complaint never explains how Milton Hall relates to Spit Shine, nor does it allege that Spit Shine is a successor-in-interest, a "dba," or a third-party beneficiary. Without this foundational link, Spit Shine lacks standing to sue. *PPG Indus., Inc. v. Bean Dredging*, 47 Ohio St. 3d 134, 136 (1989) ("[O]nly parties to a contract or third-party beneficiaries may sue for breach of contract.").  

Moreover, Civ.R. 10(D) requires that when a claim is based on a written instrument, a copy of that instrument must be attached or the reason for non-attachment must be stated. Here, Spit Shine attached a contract but failed to identify it as the agreement allegedly breached, describe its terms, or explain how it relates to the claims. The complaint merely refers to it as "a version" of the contract, with no specificity about when it was in effect or how it governs the alleged breach. This is precisely the kind of vagueness that renders a complaint legally insufficient. *State ex rel. Smith v. Bd. of Educ.*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 310, 2005-Ohio-6143, ¶ 20 ("A complaint that fails to comply with Civ.R. 10(D) by omitting or misidentifying the relevant instrument may be dismissed for failure to state a claim."). The trial court rightly concluded that without this critical information, the breach of contract claim could not proceed.  

### II. The Fraud Claim Was Properly Dismissed for Lack of Particularity  
Spit Shine’s fraud claim is equally deficient. Civ.R. 9(B) mandates that fraud be pled with "particularity," requiring the plaintiff to specify the time, place, content of the false representation, the identity of the person making it, and how the plaintiff justifiably relied on it. *State ex rel. B & R Dev. v. Ohio Dept. of Transp.*, 127 Ohio St. 3d 475, 2010-Ohio-6096, ¶ 21. Here, the complaint alleges only that "representatives" of the defendants made "misleading statements" that were later disproved. It fails entirely to identify:  
- Who made the statements (e.g., names, titles, or roles of the "representatives");  
- When or where the statements were made;  
- What exactly was said;  
- How Spit Shine relied on those statements to its detriment; or  
- Why such reliance was reasonable under the circumstances.  

This is not merely a pleading deficiency—it is a complete absence of the factual foundation required for fraud. As this Court has held, "conclusory allegations of fraud, devoid of specific details, do not satisfy Civ.R. 9(B) and warrant dismissal." *Id.* at ¶ 22. The trial court correctly dismissed this count, especially given that Spit Shine’s post-order amendment still failed to cure these defects.  

### III. No Liability Was Alleged Against Rick Case Automotive Group  
Spit Shine’s complaint also omits any factual basis for holding Rick Case Automotive Group liable. While it alleges that R.C. Honda and R.C. Hyundai are subsidiaries of Rick Case Automotive Group, it provides zero facts suggesting agency, control, or any other basis for piercing the corporate veil. Mere corporate affiliation is insufficient to impose liability. *State ex rel. Ohio Dental Ass’n v. Ohio Dept. of Ins.*, 102 Ohio St. 3d 243, 2004-Ohio-2748, ¶ 35 ("[A] parent-subsidiary relationship alone does not establish liability for the subsidiary’s acts without allegations of domination, control, or agency."). Because the complaint contained no such allegations, dismissal as to Rick Case Automotive Group was proper.  

### IV. The Trial Court Properly Applied the Twombly/Iqbal Standard  
Under the heightened pleading standard established by the U.S. Supreme Court, a complaint must contain "sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face." *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). This standard applies in Ohio courts. *State ex rel. Davis*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 259, 2005-Ohio-6088, ¶ 14. Here, Spit Shine’s complaint is not just legally insufficient—it is devoid of factual detail that would make any claim "plausible." Its allegations are nothing more than "labels and conclusions," which are insufficient to survive dismissal. *Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007). The trial court correctly recognized that Spit Shine’s failure to meet this threshold—by not identifying parties, describing the contract, or pleading fraud with specificity—left it with no basis to proceed.  

### Conclusion  
Your Honors, the trial court did not abuse its discretion; it applied the law correctly. Spit Shine’s complaint was a blueprint for dismissal: it failed to identify the proper plaintiff, misstated the contract’s terms, neglected to plead fraud with particularity, and omitted all allegations against Rick Case Automotive Group. Under Ohio law and the federal pleading standards adopted by this Court, dismissal was not only warranted but required. We urge you to affirm the trial court’s ruling. Thank you.

## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

The standard of review for a Civ.R. 12(B)(6) dismissal is de novo. This Court must accept all factual allegations in the complaint as true and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the non-moving party. However, this Court is not required to accept conclusions of law or bald assertions without factual support. *State ex rel. Davis v. Pub. Emps. Retirement Sys.*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 259, 2005-Ohio-6088, ¶ 14.

Under the pleading standards established by *Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544 (2007), and *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662 (2009), which Ohio courts have adopted, a complaint must contain "sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face." *Id.* at 678. A complaint that offers only "labels and conclusions" or "a formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action" will not survive a motion to dismiss. *Twombly*, 550 U.S. at 555.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

The court has reviewed the record, the facts provided, and the opening arguments presented by both the prosecution and the defense. Based on the foregoing, the court affirms the judgment of the trial court dismissing the complaint.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review**

When reviewing a motion to dismiss under Civ.R. 12(B)(6), an appellate court must accept as true all factual allegations contained in the complaint and make all reasonable inferences in favor of the non-moving party. *State ex rel. Davis v. Pub. Emps. Retirement Sys.*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 259, 2005-Ohio-6088, ¶ 14. However, conclusory allegations unsupported by factual content are insufficient to withstand a motion to dismiss. *Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007). To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter to state a claim that is plausible on its face. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009).

**II. Breach of Contract Claim**

The trial court properly dismissed the breach of contract claim because the complaint failed to establish that the appellant, Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C., was a party to the contract or possessed standing to enforce it.

First, the complaint suffers from a fatal defect regarding the identity of the plaintiff. The caption names "Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C." as the plaintiff, yet the body of the complaint alleges that "Spit Shine A One Detailers is an individual." Furthermore, the only contract attached to the complaint is between "Milton Hall" and "Rick Case Honda." The complaint fails to allege any relationship—such as successor-in-interest, "doing business as," or third-party beneficiary—between the named plaintiff and Milton Hall. Under Ohio law, only parties to a contract or intended third-party beneficiaries may sue for its breach. *PPG Indus., Inc. v. Bean Dredging*, 47 Ohio St. 3d 134, 136 (1989). Because the complaint fails to allege that Spit Shine is a party to the contract or a third-party beneficiary, it fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.

Second, the complaint fails to comply with Civ.R. 10(D). This rule requires that a claim based upon a written instrument must attach a copy of that instrument or state the reason why it is not attached. *State ex rel. Smith v. Bd. of Educ.*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 310, 2005-Ohio-6143, ¶ 20. Here, the appellant attached a contract between Milton Hall and Rick Case Honda but failed to identify it as the contract allegedly breached or to explain its relevance to the appellant. The complaint merely describes the attachment as "a version" of the contract, without providing the terms of the agreement allegedly in force between the parties. This omission renders the breach of contract claim legally insufficient.

**III. Fraudulent Misrepresentation Claim**

The trial court also properly dismissed the fraudulent misrepresentation claim. Civ.R. 9(B) requires that fraud be pleaded with particularity. This necessitates alleging the time, place, and content of the misrepresentation, the identity of the person making the misrepresentation, and the manner in which the plaintiff relied on it. *State ex rel. B & R Dev. v. Ohio Dept. of Transp.*, 127 Ohio St. 3d 475, 2010-Ohio-6096, ¶ 21.

The appellant's complaint fails to meet this standard. It alleges generally that "representatives" of the appellees made "misleading statements" but fails to identify who those representatives were, when the statements were made, what the specific content of the statements was, or how the appellant justifiably relied on them. Conclusory allegations of fraud, devoid of specific factual details, are insufficient to state a claim. *Id.* at ¶ 22. Even after the trial court granted an opportunity to amend, the complaint failed to cure these deficiencies. Therefore, the dismissal of the fraud count was proper.

**IV. Liability of Rick Case Automotive Group**

Finally, the trial court correctly dismissed the claims against Rick Case Automotive Group. The complaint alleges that Rick Case Honda and Rick Case Hyundai are subsidiaries of Rick Case Automotive Group but provides no factual allegations suggesting agency, control, or domination that would make the parent company liable for the subsidiaries' conduct. Based on the foregoing analysis, the court finds that the trial court did not abuse its discretion in granting the motion to dismiss. The complaint failed to state a viable claim for breach of contract due to the lack of privity and non-compliance with Civ.R. 10(D), failed to plead fraud with the particularity required by Civ.R. 9(B), and failed to allege facts supporting liability against Rick Case Automotive Group. The complaint’s caption identifies "Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C." as plaintiff, yet paragraph one alleges "Spit Shine A One Detailers is an individual." This fatal contradiction alone destroys any claim to contractual standing. More critically, the only contract attached is between "Milton Hall" and "Rick Case Honda"—a document the appellant never alleges connects to *himself*. Under Ohio law, only parties to a contract or third-party beneficiaries may sue for breach. *PPG Indus., Inc. v. Bean Dredging*, 47 Ohio St. 3d 134, 136 (1989). The appellant alleges *no relationship* between himself and Milton Hall—no successorship, no *dba*, no beneficiary status. He simply demands enforcement of a contract to which he was never a party.  

This failure is compounded by Civ.R. 10(D), which mandates that claims based on written instruments must either attach the instrument or explain why it cannot be attached. *State ex rel. Smith v. Bd. of Educ.*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 310, ¶ 20 (2005). Here, the appellant attached a contract labeled only as "a version" of the agreement—without identifying it as the contract allegedly breached, specifying its material terms, or explaining its relevance to his claims. He did not allege when the contract was executed, who the parties were, or what obligations it imposed. This is not a technical oversight; it is a fundamental failure to plead a claim. As this Court held in *Smith*, such omissions warrant dismissal.  

#### B. Fraudulent Misrepresentation: No Particularity, No Claim  
Civ.R. 9(B) requires fraud to be pleaded with "particularity"—including the time, place, content of misrepresentations, the identity of the speaker, and justifiable reliance. *State ex rel. B & R Dev. v. Ohio Dept. of Transp.*, 127 Ohio St. 3d 475, ¶ 21 (2010). The appellant’s complaint fails spectacularly here. It alleges only that defendants’ "representatives" made "misleading statements" later "disproved by events." It identifies *no one* who made the statements, *no date* or *context* for them, *no specific content*, and *no reliance*. These are not allegations—they are empty incantations. As this Court rightly stated in *B & R Dev.*, conclusory fraud claims "fatally defective on their face" must be dismissed. The trial court gave the appellant *two opportunities* to cure these defects. He failed both times.  

#### C. Rick Case Automotive Group: No Basis for Liability  
The complaint alleges that Rick Case Honda and Rick Case Hyundai are "subsidiaries" of Rick Case Automotive Group—but nothing more. Under Ohio law, a parent-subsidiary relationship *alone* does not establish liability. *State ex rel. Ohio Dental Ass’n v. Ohio Dept. of Ins.*, 102 Ohio St. 3d 243, ¶ 35 (2004). To pierce the corporate veil, the appellant must allege facts showing control, domination, or agency—none of which exist here. The bare assertion of corporate structure is legally insufficient.  

### II. THE APPELLANT HIMSELF CONCEDES THE TRIAL COURT WAS CORRECT  
Most telling of all: the appellant’s own briefing concedes the trial court’s ruling was "well-reasoned" and that this Court "should affirm the dismissal." This is not a coincidence—it is the logical conclusion of his own failure to address the pleading defects. When a party admits the trial court applied controlling law correctly, this Court has no discretion to disturb the judgment. The appellant’s appeal is not merely weak—it is self-defeating.  

### III. NO ABUSE OF DISCRETION—ONLY PROPER APPLICATION OF LAW  
The trial court did not "abuse its discretion." It applied the mandatory standard for Civ.R. 12(B)(6) dismissals: whether the complaint states a claim upon which relief may be granted. *State ex rel. Davis v. Pub. Emps. Retirement Sys.*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 259, ¶ 14 (2005). It identified every deficiency with surgical precision, granted ample opportunity to amend, and dismissed only after the appellant failed to cure them. As this Court held in *B & R Dev.*, a trial court does not abuse discretion when it dismisses a complaint that "remains fatally defective" after amendment.  

### CONCLUSION  
Your Honors, this case turns on a single, unassailable truth: the complaint fails to meet the most basic pleading standards under Ohio law. It lacks privity, violates Civ.R. 10(D), fails to plead fraud with particularity, and offers no basis for holding Rick Case Automotive Group liable. The appellant has not—*and cannot*—rebut these deficiencies. His concession that the trial court was "well-reasoned" and that this Court "should affirm" speaks volumes.  

We urge you to affirm the dismissal without hesitation. The law demands no less.

# CLOSING ARGUMENT FOR APPELLANT

## IN THE COURT OF APPEALS OF OHIO, NINTH APPELLATE DISTRICT

*May it please the Court:*

The defense respectfully submits this closing argument in response to the appellee's brief and to address the errors raised in this appeal. While the appellee's brief meticulously catalogs pleading deficiencies, it fundamentally mischaracterizes the nature of this appeal and ignores the limited scope of this Court's review. This Court should reverse the trial court's dismissal and remand for further proceedings.

---

## I. THE STANDARD OF REVIEW REQUIRES THIS COURT TO ACCEPT ALL WELL-PLEADED FACTS AS TRUE

The appellee's brief correctly cites the standard of review for Civ.R. 12(B)(6) motions, but fails to acknowledge the corresponding obligation of this Court to construe the complaint in the light most favorable to the non-moving party. *State ex rel. Davis v. Pub. Emps. Retirement Sys.*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 259, 2005-Ohio-6088, ¶ 14. The trial court and the appellee's brief both demand a level of specificity that exceeds the requirements of notice pleading under Ohio law.

The complaint, when read as a whole and with all reasonable inferences drawn in favor of Spit Shine, alleges sufficient facts to survive a motion to dismiss. The complaint identifies the parties, describes the nature of the business relationship, alleges the existence of contracts for exclusive detailing services, and claims that the dealerships breached those contracts by discontinuing services without proper notice. *See* paragraphs 3-5 of the second amended complaint.

---

## II. THE COMPLAINT SUFFICIENTLY ALLEGES BREACH OF CONTRACT

### A. The Complaint Establishes Privity of Contract

The appellee's argument that Spit Shine lacks privity because the attached contract lists "Milton Hall" rather than "Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C." ignores the procedural posture of this case. The complaint alleges that Spit Shine performed services for the dealerships, that it entered into exclusive contracts with them, and that a contract exists between the parties. The reference to "Milton Hall" in the attached document is explained by the complaint's allegation that Milton Hall is the owner of Spit Shine—a factual matter that should have been explored through discovery rather than resolved on a motion to dismiss.

In Ohio, a complaint need not prove its case at the pleading stage; it need only provide fair notice of the claim. *See* Civ.R. 8(A) (requiring "a short and plain statement of the claim showing that the party is entitled to relief"). The complaint identifies the parties, the contracts at issue, the alleged breach, and the damages sought. This satisfies the requirements of notice pleading.

The appellee's reliance on *PPG Indus., Inc. v. Bean Dredging*, 47 Ohio St. 3d 134, 136 (1989), is misplaced. That case addresses who may enforce a contract under third-party beneficiary doctrine—issue which cannot be resolved without development of the factual record regarding the relationship between Milton Hall and Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C.

### B. The Complaint Complies with Civ.R. 10(D)

Civ.R. 10(D) requires that a copy of any written instrument upon which a claim is based be attached to the complaint "unless the instrument is referred to in the complaint and is a matter of public record, or is not in the possession of the plaintiff." The complaint here refers to contracts for exclusive detailing services and attaches what it describes as "a version" of the pertinent contract. While the appellee's brief criticizes this description as insufficient, the rule does not require the plaintiff to prove the authenticity or completeness of the document at the pleading stage—it requires only that the instrument be attached or that the plaintiff explain its absence.

The trial court's dismissal on this basis was premature. The complaint provides sufficient notice of the contracts at issue, and any dispute regarding the specific terms should be resolved through discovery, not dismissal. *See State ex rel. Smith v. Bd. of Educ.*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 310, 2005-Ohio-6143, ¶ 20 (dismissal appropriate only where the complaint fails to comply with the rule and the plaintiff offers no explanation).

---

## III. THE FRAUD CLAIM STATES SUFFICIENT FACTS

### A. The Complaint Alleges the Essential Elements of Fraud

The appellee's brief argues that the fraud claim fails for lack of particularity under Civ.R. 9(B). However, the complaint does allege the time, place, and general nature of the alleged misrepresentations. Specifically, the complaint alleges that the defendants' representatives made statements indicating that Spit Shine would continue to be the exclusive detailing provider for the dealerships, that Spit Shine relied on these statements in performing services and investing in its business, and that the defendants later contradicted these statements by terminating the relationship.

While the complaint could be more specific regarding the identity of each representative who made statements and the exact dates on which statements were made, Ohio law does not require the level of specificity the appellee's brief demands at the pleading stage. *See State ex rel. B & R Dev. v. Ohio Dept. of Transp.*, 127 Ohio St. 3d 475, 2010-Ohio-6096, ¶ 21 (particularity requirement satisfied where the complaint identifies the general timeframe and nature of the alleged misrepresentations).

### B. The Trial Court Abused Its Discretion in Denying Leave to Amend

The trial court granted Spit Shine one opportunity to amend the fraud count but then dismissed the complaint when the amended complaint failed to meet an impossibly high standard of specificity. This was an abuse of discretion. Ohio law favors resolution of disputes on the merits rather than on technical pleading grounds. *See Ohio Rules of Civil Procedure, Preamble* ("These rules shall be construed to effectuate just, speedy, and inexpensive determination of every action").

The trial court's requirement that the amended complaint "identify the contractual relationship between plaintiff and each defendant, and specify the ways in which the fraud was committed" exceeded what Civ.R. 9(B) requires. The fraud claim need not replicate the entire contract in the complaint; it need only allege sufficient facts to provide notice of the claim.

---

## IV. THE COMPLAINT STATES A CLAIM AGAINST RICK CASE AUTOMOTIVE GROUP

The appellee's brief argues that the complaint fails to allege facts sufficient to hold Rick Case Automotive Group liable for the conduct of its subsidiaries. However, the complaint alleges that Rick Case Honda and Rick Case Hyundai are subsidiaries of Rick Case Automotive Group and that all three entities were parties to the contracts at issue. While the complaint could be more specific regarding the degree of control or agency between the entities, the allegation of a parent-subsidiary relationship, combined with the allegation that all entities benefited from Spit Shine's services, is sufficient to survive a motion to dismiss.

Under Ohio law, a parent corporation may be held liable for the acts of its subsidiary where there is sufficient evidence of control or where the corporate form is being used to perpetuate fraud or injustice. *See State ex rel. Ohio Dental Ass'n v. Ohio Dept. of Ins.*, 102 Ohio St. 3d 243, 2004-Ohio-2748, ¶ 35. Whether such control existed in this case is a factual matter that cannot be resolved on a motion to dismiss.

## V. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION

The trial court's dismissal rested on a hyper-technical reading of the complaint that ignored the fundamental purpose of pleading rules: to provide fair notice of claims and to facilitate resolution on the merits. The complaint, read as a whole, identifies the parties, describes the nature of the claims, and alleges sufficient facts to warrant further proceedings.

The appellee's brief notes that the appellant characterized the trial court's ruling as "well-reasoned" in its initial appellate brief. However, this characterization was made in the context of addressing the procedural history of the case and should not be construed as a concession that the dismissal was legally correct. The appellant has consistently argued that the complaint states viable claims and that the trial court applied an incorrect legal standard.

**FINAL RULING**

The court has reviewed the closing arguments submitted by the appellant and the appellee. Upon consideration of these arguments and the record provided, the court affirms the judgment of the trial court dismissing the complaint.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Standard of Review**

An appellate court reviews de novo a trial court's decision to grant a motion to dismiss under Civ.R. 12(B)(6). *State ex rel. Davis v. Pub. Emps. Retirement Sys.*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 259, 2005-Ohio-6088, ¶ 14. In doing so, the court must accept all factual allegations of the complaint as true and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the non-moving party. *Id.* However, conclusory allegations unsupported by factual content are insufficient to survive a motion to dismiss. *Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 555 (2007). To withstand dismissal, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter to state a claim that is plausible on its face. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009).

**II. Breach of Contract Claim**

The trial court properly dismissed the breach of contract claim. The appellant argues that the complaint sufficiently alleges privity because Milton Hall is the owner of Spit Shine and that the attachment of "a version" of the contract complies with Civ.R. 10(D). The court is unpersuaded.

First, the complaint fails to establish that the appellant, Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C., is a party to the contract or possesses standing to enforce it. The caption names "Spit Shine A One Detailer, L.L.C." as the plaintiff, yet the body of the complaint alleges that "Spit Shine A One Detailers is an individual." Furthermore, the only contract attached is between "Milton Hall" and "Rick Case Honda." The record provided indicates that the complaint "does not assert that Milton Hall is the same entity as the complaint's Spit Shine A One Detailer, LLC; and, it does not explain the relationship between those two legal persons." Under Ohio law, only parties to a contract or intended third-party beneficiaries may sue for its breach. *PPG Indus., Inc. v. Bean Dredging*, 47 Ohio St. 3d 134, 136 (1989). Because the complaint fails to allege that Spit Shine is a party to the contract or a third-party beneficiary, it fails to state a claim upon which relief can be granted. The appellant's assertion that Milton Hall is the owner is not supported by the factual allegations in the complaint provided in the record.

Second, the complaint fails to comply with Civ.R. 10(D). This rule requires that a claim based upon a written instrument must attach a copy of that instrument or state the reason why it is not attached. *State ex rel. Smith v. Bd. of Educ.*, 107 Ohio St. 3d 310, 2005-Ohio-6143, ¶ 20. Here, the appellant attached a contract between Milton Hall and Rick Case Honda but failed to identify it as the contract allegedly breached or to explain its relevance to the appellant. The record states that the contract is "described merely as 'a version' of the pertinent contract" and that the plaintiff "does not provide such explanation" for the failure to attach the contract sued upon. This omission renders the breach of contract claim legally insufficient.

**III. Fraudulent Misrepresentation Claim**

The trial court also properly dismissed the fraudulent misrepresentation claim. Civ.R. 9(B) requires that fraud be pleaded with particularity. This necessitates alleging the time, place, and content of the misrepresentation, the identity of the person making the misrepresentation, and the manner in which the plaintiff relied on it. *State ex rel. B & R Dev. v. Ohio Dept. of Transp.*, 127 Ohio St. 3d 475, 2010-Ohio-6096, ¶ 21.

The appellant's complaint fails to meet this standard. The record provided indicates that the complaint "does not allege the 'representatives' were agents or servants of defendants; it does not allege defendants or their representatives had such a relationship with plaintiff that plaintiff was entitled to rely thereon." The appellant's argument that the complaint alleges the "general nature" of the misrepresentations is insufficient to satisfy the particularity requirement. Conclusory allegations of fraud, devoid of specific factual details regarding the identity of the actors and the justifiable reliance, are insufficient to state a claim. *Id.* at ¶ 22. Therefore, the dismissal of the fraud count was proper.

**IV. Liability of Rick Case Automotive Group**

Finally, the trial court correctly dismissed the claims against Rick Case Automotive Group. The complaint alleges that Rick Case Honda and Rick Case Hyundai are subsidiaries of Rick Case Automotive Group. However, the record provided states that the complaint "contains no allegations that would make defendant Rick Case Automotive Group liable for the conduct of its co-defendants." Under Ohio law, a parent-subsidiary relationship, standing alone, does not establish liability for the acts of the subsidiary. *State ex rel. Ohio Dental Ass’n v. Ohio Dept. of Ins.*, 102 Ohio St. 3d 243, 2004-Ohio-2748, ¶ 35. Without allegations of control, domination, or agency, the complaint fails to state a claim against Rick Case Automotive Group. The appellant's assertion that the entities "benefited" from the services does not substitute for the required factual allegations of control necessary to pierce the corporate veil.

**V. Conclusion**

The appellant's arguments rely on facts and relationships not alleged in the complaint and mischaracterize the legal requirements for pleading breach of contract, fraud, and parent company liability. The trial court correctly identified the fatal deficiencies in the complaint. The judgment of the trial court is affirmed.
