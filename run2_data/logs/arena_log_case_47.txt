**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

The issue before this Court is whether the trial court’s declaration of a mistrial during Mr. Dickinson’s first trial bars his retrial on double jeopardy grounds. Specifically, we must determine whether the mistrial was declared with the defendant’s consent or was necessitated by "manifest necessity." Based on the facts provided and the arguments presented, the Court finds that the trial court abused its discretion in declaring a mistrial. Neither the defendant’s consent nor manifest necessity justified the termination of the proceedings. Consequently, the double jeopardy clause bars retrial, and the charges must be dismissed with prejudice.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Defendant Did Not Consent to the Mistrial**

The Fifth Amendment protects a defendant from being tried twice for the same offense unless the defendant consents to the termination of the first trial. *United States v. Dinitz*, 424 U.S. 600, 607–08 (1976). Consent must be clear and unambiguous; it cannot be implied from a failure to object when the defendant has explicitly expressed a desire to proceed.

The record demonstrates that defense counsel explicitly and repeatedly stated a preference to continue the trial with the empaneled jury. Counsel noted that a mistrial was a possibility but "made clear that he preferred instead to proceed with the same jury, without any further reference to the scrap of paper." He further indicated he would not move to strike the surprise testimony to avoid emphasizing it. While counsel reserved the right to seek dismissal *if* the court deemed a mistrial necessary, this conditional reservation does not constitute consent to the mistrial itself. Rather, it reinforces the position that the mistrial was forced upon the defense by the court.

The argument that defense counsel’s failure to formally object to the mistrial constitutes implied consent is unpersuasive. As established in *United States v. Jorn*, 400 U.S. 470, 485 (1971), waiver of double jeopardy protections requires a voluntary act inducing the mistrial. Here, the defense actively argued *against* the mistrial. The silence cited by the State occurred after the court had already initiated the discussion of mistrial and after counsel had already placed his preference on the record. Under *State v. McCray*, 218 Ariz. 589, 190 P.3d 212, 216 (Ct. App. 2008), implied consent requires a totality of circumstances showing acceptance. Here, the totality shows a defense strategy to mitigate the surprise testimony by proceeding, not an acceptance of a mistrial. Therefore, Mr. Dickinson did not consent to the mistrial.

**B. Manifest Necessity Did Not Exist**

In the absence of consent, a mistrial is permissible only if there was a "manifest necessity" for the action. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505–06 (1978). Manifest necessity exists only when there is "no alternative to declaring a mistrial to prevent a miscarriage of justice." The trial judge must exercise "sound discretion" and make a "diligent effort" to consider less drastic alternatives before aborting the trial.

The trial court in this case failed to meet this standard. While the court engaged in a "lengthy discussion" regarding the surprise testimony, it did not conduct the requisite diligent inquiry into the specific alternatives available. The prosecutor explicitly stated that the State "can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence." Defense counsel also expressed a willingness to proceed without the note. Despite these offers, the court did not rule on the motion to exclude the evidence, nor did it attempt to give a curative instruction to the jury to disregard the surprise testimony.

This failure is fatal under Arizona precedent. In *State v. Smith*, 141 Ariz. 510, 687 P.2d 1263, 1266 (Ct. App. 1984), the court reversed a conviction where a mistrial was declared for surprise testimony, holding that the trial court abused its discretion by "failing to consider alternatives such as excluding the evidence or giving a curative instruction." Similarly, in *State v. Lopez*, 237 Ariz. 258, 349 P.3d 283, 287 (Ct. App. 2015), the court emphasized that a mistrial is not manifestly necessary when the trial court could have excluded the evidence or instructed the jury to disregard it.

The State argues that the prejudice was incurable because the testimony contradicted the defense’s opening statement theory regarding the serial numbers. While the trial judge is in the best position to assess the trial atmosphere, *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. at 507, this discretion is not unlimited. The availability of a viable alternative—exclusion of the evidence, offered by the State itself—precludes a finding of manifest necessity. The court’s unilateral decision to declare a mistrial without first attempting to exclude the evidence or instruct the jury was an abuse of discretion. See *State v. Williams*, 227 Ariz. 489, 259 P.3d 195, 199 (2011) (holding that a mistrial declared without manifest necessity requires dismissal with prejudice).

**III. CONCLUSION**

The trial court declared a mistrial without the defendant’s consent and without manifest necessity. The court failed to consider the readily available alternative of excluding the surprise evidence, as offered by the prosecution and desired by the defense. This failure constitutes a violation of Mr. Dickinson’s Fifth Amendment protection against double jeopardy.

## III. NO MANIFEST NECESSITY EXISTED FOR THE MISTRIAL

The absence of manifest necessity is even more glaring. The Supreme Court has consistently held that a mistrial may be declared without a defendant's consent only when "no alternative to declaring a mistrial to prevent a miscarriage of justice" remains. *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497, 505–06 (1978). The trial court bears an affirmative duty to exercise "sound discretion" and make a "diligent effort" to consider less drastic alternatives before aborting the trial.

**First**, the prosecution itself offered to exclude the surprise evidence. The prosecutor explicitly stated: "the State can proceed forward with the exclusion of this evidence." This is a direct admission by the party with the strongest interest in obtaining a conviction that the evidence was removable. When the prosecution confirms that exclusion is feasible, manifest necessity cannot exist.

**Second**, defense counsel expressed willingness to proceed without reference to the note. The defense did not seek to exploit the surprise testimony; rather, counsel wished to continue with the jury as empaneled.

**Third**, the trial court could have issued a curative instruction to the jury to disregard the surprise testimony regarding the serial number on the note. The court made no effort to explore this remedy.

The failure to consider these alternatives constitutes an abuse of discretion under Arizona precedent. In *State v. Smith*, 141 Ariz. 510, 687 P.2d 1263, 1266 (Ct. App. 1984), this Court reversed a conviction where a mistrial was declared for surprise testimony, holding that the trial court abused its discretion by "failing to consider alternatives such as excluding the evidence or giving a curative instruction." Similarly, in *State v. Lopez*, 237 Ariz. 258, 349 P.3d 283, 287 (Ct. App. 2015), the Court emphasized that "a mistrial is not manifestly necessary when the trial court could have excluded the evidence or instructed the jury to disregard it."

The prosecution's attempt to characterize the surprise testimony as "incurable" is directly contradicted by the prosecution's own statement that exclusion was possible. If the evidence could be excluded, any prejudice was by definition curable. The Constitution does not permit a retrial simply because the trial court failed to avail itself of readily available remedies.

## V. THE APPROPRIATE REMEDY IS DISMISSAL WITH PREJUDICE

When a mistrial is declared without the defendant's consent and without manifest necessity, the Double Jeopardy Clause mandates dismissal with prejudice. As this Court recognized in *State v. The policy rationale is clear: if retrial were permitted after an improperly declared mistrial, the government would have an incentive to provoke mistrials whenever its case appears weak. **B. Manifest Necessity Did Not Exist**
