A defense arising from a creditor's failure to comply with OCGA § 44-14-161 is not "based on suretyship"—it is based on a statutory mandate that exists independently of the suretyship relationship. As this Court recognized in PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. The plaintiff points to the waiver's reference to "anti-deficiency" laws as proof that the confirmation statute falls within its scope. However, this argument conflates different concepts. The confirmation statute is not an "anti-deficiency law" in the traditional sense. Traditional anti-deficiency laws, such as those found in some western states, limit or prohibit deficiency judgments entirely as a matter of public policy. Georgia's confirmation statute, by contrast, does not prohibit deficiency judgments—it merely requires that the foreclosure sale be confirmed as having brought fair market value before a deficiency may be pursued. This is a procedural safeguard, not an anti-deficiency prohibition. The statute's purpose is to ensure that the collateral fetched fair market value, not to shield guarantors from all liability. Ejusdem generis is a well-established principle of contractual interpretation that this Court has recognized applies to guaranty agreements. Co., 151 Ga. 46, 51 (106 SE 197) (1921) (applying ejusdem generis to interpret insurance contract provisions).

In the waiver provision, the specific examples given are "(A) the provisions of O.C.G.A. The specific examples—Section 10-7-24, one-action rules, and anti-deficiency laws—are all rooted in traditional suretyship principles. They concern the relationship between the guarantor and the creditor and the procedural mechanisms available to enforce the debt. The confirmation statute, by contrast, is a standalone statutory protection that applies regardless of the existence of a guaranty. It protects borrowers and third-party property owners, not merely sureties.

Under ejusdem generis, the phrase "any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency" must be interpreted in light of the specific examples that precede it. ### II. THE WAIVER DOES NOT UNAMBIGUOUSLY ENCOMPASS THE CONFIRMATION DEFENSE

#### A. The Prosecution Misreads the Waiver's Plain Language

The prosecution contends that the Waiver's reference to "any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency" unambiguously covers OCGA § 44-14-161. This reading ignores critical context and the well-established principle that Waiver provisions in guaranty agreements must be examined with care.

As this Court recognized in *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818, 824 (3) (b) (785 SE2d 505) (2016), the confirmation requirement is "a condition precedent to the lender's ability to pursue a guarantor for a deficiency after a foreclosure has been conducted." The prosecution conflates two distinct concepts: (1) laws that prohibit or prevent deficiency claims entirely, and (2) statutes that establish procedural prerequisites to pursuing such claims.

The Waiver specifically references "anti-deficiency" laws—statutes that categorically prohibit deficiency judgments, such as those protecting homeowners from deficiency liability following non-judicial foreclosure. See OCGA § 44-14-161 (b) (establishing confirmation as a prerequisite, not a prohibition). The confirmation statute does not "prevent" a deficiency claim in the same manner as an anti-deficiency law; rather, it establishes a procedural condition that must be satisfied before the claim may proceed. This distinction is fatal to the prosecution's interpretation.

The prosecution's argument that the Waiver need not specifically name OCGA § 44-14-161 to encompass it proves too much. If any statutory prerequisite to a deficiency action falls within "any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action," then the Waiver would also encompass notice requirements, statute of limitations provisions, and countless other procedural prerequisites—all of which "prevent" a deficiency claim until satisfied. Such an expansive reading contradicts the principle that waivers of statutory rights must be clear and unambiguous. *PNC Bank*, 298 Ga. at 824.

#### B. Ejusdem Generis Properly Limits the Waiver

The prosecution's argument that *ejusdem generis* does not apply because of the phrase "including, but not limited to" fundamentally misapprehends the canon's application. The phrase "including, but not limited to" merely indicates that the specific examples listed are illustrative. It does not eliminate the need to interpret the general words that follow.

The Waiver provides: "any rights or defenses arising by reason of (A) the provisions of O.C.G.A. Section 10-7-24 concerning Guarantor's right to require Lender to take action against Borrower or any 'one action' or 'anti-deficiency' law or any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency."

The specific examples—OCGA § 10-7-24, "one action" rules, and "anti-deficiency" laws—share a common characteristic: they are all rooted in traditional suretyship doctrine and protect the surety's interest in the creditor's enforcement of remedies against the principal debtor. The confirmation statute serves a fundamentally different purpose: ensuring that collateral brings fair market value. It does not protect the guarantor from the creditor's failure to pursue the debtor; it protects the debtor and the credit market from undervaluation of security.

As this Court correctly noted in its preliminary ruling, the confirmation statute "functions as a procedural safeguard to ensure fair market value, rather than a prohibition on deficiency judgments akin to traditional 'anti-deficiency' laws." The prosecution's attempt to conflate these distinct concepts contradicts the statute's text and purpose.

#### C. The "Impairment of Collateral" Argument Fails

The confirmation statute does not confer upon guarantors any "right to require Lender to take action against Borrower." It is a standalone procedural requirement.

Second, "impairment of collateral" in the suretyship context refers to the creditor's release or deterioration of security that benefits the surety. The confirmation statute has no relationship to this suretyship protection. The confirmation proceedings under OCGA § 44-14-161 (a) require the creditor to prove that the property sold for its fair market value. ## III. LEGAL ANALYSIS

### A. The Waiver Provision Does Not Unambiguously Encompass the Confirmation Defense Under OCGA § 44-14-161

The primary issue before this Court is whether the Guarantors waived their defense to the deficiency action based on RES-GA's failure to confirm the foreclosure sales as required by OCGA § 44-14-161. The Guarantors contend that the Court of Appeals erred in its determination that the Waiver unambiguously encompasses the confirmation defense.

#### 1. The Confirmation Statute Creates a Distinct Statutory Defense

OCGA § 44-14-161(a) provides that no deficiency judgment shall be recovered in any foreclosure sale of real estate "unless the court before whom the proceedings are pending shall, in addition to the judgment of foreclosure, render a judgment against the debtor for the amount of the deficiency" after finding that the property sold for its fair market value. This confirmation requirement is a condition precedent that must be satisfied before a lender may pursue a deficiency judgment.

As this Court recognized in *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818, 824 (3) (b) (785 SE2d 505) (2016), compliance with the confirmation statute "is a condition precedent to the lender's ability to pursue a guarantor for a deficiency after a foreclosure has been conducted." The confirmation requirement is not a traditional suretyship defense; rather, it is a statutory prerequisite that protects borrowers and guarantors from the impairment of collateral by ensuring that property is sold for fair market value before a deficiency may be pursued.

#### 2. The Waiver Language Must Be Strictly Construed Against RES-GA

Georgia law provides that guaranty agreements are strictly construed against the drafter. *Colonial Bank & Trust Co. v. First Nat'l Bank of Atlanta*, 242 Ga. 222, 225 (249 SE2d 758) (1978). This principle of strict construction requires that waiver provisions be interpreted narrowly and that any ambiguities be resolved in favor of the guarantor.

The Waiver provision states that the Guarantor waives:

> "any and all rights or defenses based on suretyship or impairment of collateral including, but not limited to, any rights or defenses arising by reason of (A) the provisions of O.C.G.A. Section 10-7-24 concerning Guarantor's right to require Lender to take action against Borrower or any 'one action' or 'anti-deficiency' law or any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency, against Guarantor..."

The Guarantors submit that this language, while broad, must be interpreted in context. The Waiver specifically references "suretyship" defenses and cites OCGA § 10-7-24, which addresses a guarantor's right to require the lender to proceed against the borrower. These are traditional suretyship protections that have long been recognized at law and in equity.

#### 3. The Principle of Ejus Generis Limits the Scope of "Any Other Law"

The Court should apply the principle of *ejus generis* to interpret the phrase "any other law" in the Waiver. Under this canon of construction, general words following specific words are construed to include only items of the same general nature as those specifically enumerated.

Here, the specific words are "one action" and "anti-deficiency" law—terms that describe traditional suretyship protections that prevent a lender from proceeding against a guarantor under certain circumstances. The "any other law" phrase should therefore be limited to laws of the same general character, namely, traditional suretyship defenses.

The confirmation statute under OCGA § 44-14-161 is fundamentally different. It does not prevent a lender from bringing an action against a guarantor based on suretyship principles; rather, it establishes a procedural prerequisite that the lender must satisfy before any deficiency action may proceed. The statute does not speak to the rights of guarantors vis-à-vis borrowers or the impairment of collateral in the traditional suretyship sense. Instead, it establishes a condition precedent that serves as a protection for all parties to a foreclosure, including guarantors.

#### 4. The "Imairment of Collateral" Language Does Not Incorporate the Confirmation Defense

The Court of Appeals concluded that the Waiver's explicit coverage of defenses based on "impairment of collateral" encompasses the confirmation defense because the confirmation statute serves to prevent impairment of collateral. This reasoning is flawed.

The "impairment of collateral" language in the Waiver refers to the common law and statutory protections that allow a guarantor to assert defenses when the lender impairs the collateral by releasing property, failing to perfect security interests, or similar actions. These are traditional suretyship defenses that relate to the lender's conduct regarding the collateral itself—not the procedural requirement that the lender obtain judicial confirmation of the sale price.

The confirmation statute does not address the lender's conduct in impairing the collateral; it addresses the fairness of the sale price after the foreclosure has occurred. A lender may have obtained the property for far less than fair market value without having engaged in any conduct that impairs the collateral in the traditional sense. The confirmation statute is thus distinct from the impairment of collateral defenses contemplated by the Waiver.

#### 5. The Waiver Cannot Override Clear Statutory Mandates

Even if the Waiver could be interpreted to encompass the confirmation defense, public policy considerations counsel against enforcing such a waiver. OCGA § 44-14-161 establishes a fundamental protection for borrowers and guarantors alike—a protection that ensures property is sold for fair market value before a deficiency may be pursued.

To permit a lender to circumvent this statutory protection through a contractual waiver would eviscerate the confirmation requirement and undermine the legislative intent behind the statute. The Confirmation statute represents a clear expression of Georgia public policy, and parties cannot contract away compliance with statutory mandates that serve to protect the integrity of the judicial process.

### B. The Damages Calculation Was Improper and Prejudicial

Even if this Court were to find that the Waiver encompasses the confirmation defense, the trial court's damages calculation was fundamentally flawed and requires reversal.

The trial court calculated RES-GA's damages as the difference between the amount due on the notes and "the higher values of each property as contended by the Guarantors' appraisers in testimony submitted during the confirmation proceedings."

This methodology was improper for several reasons:

1. **Burden of Proof Shift**: Under OCGA § 44-14-161, the burden of proving fair market value rests with the lender, not the guarantor. By using the Guarantors' own appraisal evidence—which was submitted to challenge the fairness of the sale price—to establish the deficiency, the trial court improperly shifted the burden of proof to the Guarantors.

2. **Inconsistent Positions**: The Guarantors' appraisers testified to higher property values in the confirmation proceedings specifically to demonstrate that the foreclosure sales did not bring fair market value. Using these same values to establish the deficiency creates an inherent inconsistency and penalizes the Guarantors for their attempt to protect their rights in the confirmation proceedings.

3. **Speculative Damages**: The use of appraisal values rather than actual market values at the time of foreclosure introduces speculation into the damages calculation. Appraisals are inherently estimates and may not reflect actual market conditions at the time of sale.

## IV. REBUTTAL OF PLAINTIFF'S CLAIMS

### A. Rebuttal: The Waiver Does Not Cover the Confirmation Defense

RES-GA argues that the Waiver's use of the phrase "including, but not limited to" demonstrates that the specific examples provided are illustrative rather than exhaustive. However, this argument ignores the context in which the Waiver was drafted and the principle of strict construction that applies to guaranty agreements.

The Waiver was clearly designed to address traditional suretyship defenses—the very types of defenses that have historically been available to guarantors. The inclusion of "any other law" must be read in light of this context. To interpret the Waiver as encompassing every possible defense that might prevent a lender from pursuing a deficiency would render the specific enumerations meaningless and create an absurd result.

Furthermore, RES-GA's argument that OCGA § 44-14-161 "prevents" the lender from bringing a deficiency action mischaracterizes the statute. The confirmation requirement is a procedural prerequisite, not a substantive bar. A lender who fails to obtain confirmation is not prevented from bringing a deficiency action—it is simply barred from recovering until the procedural requirement is satisfied. This distinction is critical and demonstrates that the confirmation statute is not of the same character as the "one action" and "anti-deficiency" laws specifically enumerated in the Waiver.

### B. Rebuttal: The PNC Bank Decision Does Not Control

RES-GA relies heavily on this Court's decision in *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818 (785 SE2d 505) (2016), for the proposition that the confirmation requirement can be waived. However, *PNC Bank* is distinguishable.

In *PNC Bank*, this Court held that "compliance with OCGA § 44-14-161 is a condition precedent to the lender's ability to pursue a guarantor for a deficiency after a foreclosure has been conducted, but a guarantor retains the contractual ability to waive the condition precedent requirement." *Id.* at 824. The Court in *PNC Bank* did not hold that any waiver provision automatically encompasses the confirmation defense; rather, it recognized that a guarantor may waive the requirement through specific contractual language.

The question before this Court is whether the Waiver provision in this case constitutes such a specific waiver. The Guarantors submit that it does not. The Waiver language is general and ambiguous, and under the principle of strict construction, must be interpreted in favor of the Guarantors.

### C. Rebuttal: The HWA Properties Decision Is Not Binding Authority

RES-GA also relies on the Court of Appeals decision in *HWA Properties, Inc. v. Community & Southern Bank*, 322 Ga. App. 877 (746 SE2d 609) (2013), as supporting its ability to pursue a deficiency despite failing to obtain confirmation. However, *HWA Properties* is not binding authority on this Court, and its reasoning is flawed.

The *HWA Properties* Court held that a creditor's failure to obtain valid confirmation does not impair its authority to obtain a deficiency judgment against a personal guarantor. This holding conflicts with the plain language of OCGA § 44-14-161, which establishes the confirmation requirement as a condition precedent to any deficiency judgment, regardless of whether the borrower or guarantor is being pursued.

## V. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Guarantors respectfully request that this Court:

1. **Reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals** and hold that the Waiver provision does not unambiguously encompass the confirmation defense under OCGA § 44-14-161;

2. **Hold that RES-GA's failure to confirm the foreclosure sales bars its deficiency action** against the Guarantors;

3. **In the alternative, reverse and remand for a new damages calculation** that properly allocates the burden of proof and does not rely on the Guarantors' appraisal evidence submitted in the confirmation proceedings.

The Waiver provision, properly construed, does not waive the Guarantors' defense based on RES-GA's failure to confirm the foreclosure sales. The confirmation requirement of OCGA § 44-14-161 is a statutory protection that cannot be waived through general language in a guaranty agreement. RES-GA's failure to obtain confirmation of the sales bars its deficiency action, and the trial court's judgment against the Guarantors must be reversed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final ruling following the submission of closing arguments by both the Defense (Jim L. York and John A. Drillot) and the Prosecution (RES-GA LJY, LLC). The Court has reviewed the arguments presented, the applicable case law, and the statutory framework governing guaranty agreements and foreclosure confirmations in Georgia. The issue presented is whether the Guarantors waived their defense to a deficiency action based on the creditor’s failure to confirm the foreclosure sales under OCGA § 44-14-161. After careful consideration of the parties’ rebuttals, the Court affirms its previous determination that the Waiver provision unambiguously encompasses the confirmation defense.

**II. ANALYSIS**

**A. The Waiver Provision Unambiguously Covers the Confirmation Defense**

The Defense argues that the Waiver provision is ambiguous and must be strictly construed against the drafter, RES-GA, citing *Colonial Bank & Trust Co. v. First Nat'l Bank of Atlanta*, 242 Ga. 222, 225 (249 SE2d 758) (1978). They contend that the principle of *ejus generis* limits the phrase "any other law" to traditional suretyship defenses, such as "one action" or "anti-deficiency" laws, and does not extend to the procedural confirmation requirements of OCGA § 44-14-161.

The Court is not persuaded by this interpretation. While strict construction is the rule for guaranty agreements, it applies only where the language is ambiguous. The Waiver provision explicitly states that the Guarantor waives rights or defenses arising by reason of "any 'one action' or 'anti-deficiency' law or any other law which may prevent Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency." The use of the phrase "including, but not limited to" signals that the enumerated examples are not exhaustive. See *PNC Bank, Nat'l Ass'n v. Smith*, 298 Ga. 818, 824 (3) (b) (785 SE2d 505) (2016).

OCGA § 44-14-161(a) mandates that no deficiency judgment shall be recovered unless the court confirms the sale price reflects the fair market value. Until this condition is met, the lender is statutorily barred from recovering a deficiency. Therefore, the statute functions as a law that "prevent[s] Lender from bringing any action, including a claim for deficiency." The plain language of the Waiver captures this statutory defense. The Defense’s attempt to characterize the confirmation statute merely as a "procedural prerequisite" distinct from a law preventing an action ignores the practical effect of the statute: without confirmation, the deficiency action is legally barred.

Furthermore, the Waiver covers defenses based on the "impairment of collateral." The confirmation statute is designed to prevent the impairment of collateral by ensuring the property is sold for its fair market value. Thus, the defense is intrinsically related to the impairment of collateral, a category of defenses the Guarantors expressly waived. The broad language of the Waiver, coupled with the "impairment of collateral" clause, leaves no ambiguity regarding the parties' intent to waive the confirmation defense.

**B. The Damages Calculation Was Harmless**

The Defense further argues that the trial court erred in calculating damages by using the higher values contended by the Guarantors’ appraisers, thereby shifting the burden of proof and creating prejudice.

The record reflects that the trial court calculated the deficiency using the "higher values of each property as contended by the Guarantors’ appraisers." By utilizing the values asserted by the Guarantors themselves—values which were higher than the foreclosure sale prices—the trial court effectively calculated the deficiency in the manner most favorable to the Guarantors. Using the Guarantors’ own evidence of value reduced the judgment amount against them. Consequently, even if the methodology was irregular, the Guarantors cannot demonstrate prejudice. An error that does not affect the substantial rights of the parties is harmless and does not warrant reversal.

**III. CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court finds that the Guarantors waived their right to assert the failure to confirm the foreclosure sales as a defense to the deficiency action. The Waiver provision’s plain language, specifically the "any other law" clause and the "impairment of collateral" language, unambiguously encompasses the confirmation requirement of OCGA § 44-14-161. Additionally, the trial court’s damages calculation, based on the Guarantors’ own appraisals, constituted harmless error. Therefore, the judgment of the Court of Appeals is affirmed.
