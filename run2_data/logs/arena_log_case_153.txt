They are simply part of the pool of eligible applicants, no different from others. ## STATEMENT OF FACTS

PACT Charter School is a public charter school located in Ramsey, Minnesota, serving students from kindergarten through twelfth grade. Respondent admits students through a random lottery system when applications exceed available capacity.

Appellants are residents of Ramsey, Minnesota. Appellant Matthew J. Look filed this action seeking a declaratory judgment interpreting Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, to require that charter schools provide preferential admission treatment to residents of the municipality where the school is located.

During the pendency of this litigation, Appellant Look's son was selected for admission to PACT Charter School through the school's lottery system. Subsequently, Appellant John P. Dehen, an attorney, was added as a plaintiff on behalf of his minor daughter, who was not selected for admission through the lottery.

The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent, holding that Minn.Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 9, does not extend a preference for admission to residents where a charter school is located. This appeal follows.

---

## ARGUMENT

### I. STANDARD OF REVIEW

On summary judgment, this Court reviews de novo whether there are any genuine issues of material fact and whether the district court correctly applied the law. *State v. Nguyen*, 741 N.W.2d 128, 133 (Minn. 2007). Summary judgment is appropriate when the movant demonstrates there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *Id.* at 133-34.

The interpretation of a statute is a question of law warranting de novo review. *Malmstadt v. Flood*, 678 N.W.2d 44, 49 (Minn. 2004). This Court must "interpret a statute's words according to their plain and ordinary meaning." *State v. Thiel*, 600 N.W.2d 272, 276 (Minn. 1999). When the statutory language is unambiguous, this Court must apply it as written, without adding or subtracting terms. *Id.*

### II. THE PLAIN LANGUAGE OF MINNESOTA STATUTE § 124D.10, SUBDIVISION 9, DOES NOT CREATE A MANDATORY RESIDENCY PREFERENCE

#### A. The Statutory Text Is Clear and Unambiguous

Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, provides, in relevant part:

> "A charter school shall admit students who reside in the district in which the charter school is located, or in a neighboring district, or in a district that has a cooperative agreement with the charter school authorizer, if the school has space available after admitting students who reside in the district in which the charter school is located."

This language is unambiguous. The statute uses the permissive verb "shall admit students who reside in the district" followed by a conditional clause: "if the school has space available after admitting students who reside in the district." The conditional "if" clause establishes when non-local students may be considered for admission—it does not create a preference hierarchy favoring local residents.

The statute does not use mandatory language requiring preference, priority, or preferential treatment for local residents. It does not state that local residents "shall be given priority" or "must be preferred" over other applicants. Rather, it establishes eligibility criteria for admission from various districts.

#### B. The Statute's Language Is Permissive, Not Mandatory

The Minnesota Court of Appeals has directly addressed this precise issue in *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 788 N.W.2d 692 (Minn. App. 2010). In that case, the court held that § 124D.10, subd. 9, "does not require charter schools to give preference to residents of the school district in which the school is located." *Id.* at 697. The court emphasized that the statute "allows charter schools to admit students from the district, neighboring districts, or districts with cooperative agreements, but does not mandate a preference for local residents over others." *Id.*

The court in *Charter School of St. Paul* further explained that the phrase "if the school has space available after admitting students who reside in the district" does not establish a hierarchy of admission priority. *Id.* at 698. Rather, it merely sets a condition for when students from neighboring districts may be admitted. *Id.* The statute's language is permissive—it allows admission from certain areas under certain conditions—but does not mandate preferential treatment for local residents.

This Court should follow the holding in *Charter School of St. Paul*, which is directly on point and binding authority.

### III. APPELLANTS' INTERPRETATION WOULD CONTRADICT THE STATUTE'S STRUCTURE AND PURPOSE

#### A. Charter Schools Are Designed to Promote School Choice

Minnesota's charter school statutory framework is designed to "improve student learning and increase learning opportunities for all students." Minn. Stat. § 124D.01. Charter schools are "public schools" that operate independently of traditional school district structures. Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 1.

The purpose of charter schools is to provide educational options and choice to families across the state. Reading a mandatory residency preference into § 124D.10, subd. 9, would contradict this purpose by artificially limiting charter school enrollment to local residents, thereby restricting the ability of families throughout Minnesota to access charter school educational opportunities.

#### B. The Statute's Overall Structure Confirms a Permissive Interpretation

Chapter 124D provides charter schools with significant autonomy in their operations, including admissions. Minn. Stat. § 124D.10, subd. 4 (granting charter schools authority to "admit students pursuant to this section"). This autonomy would be undermined if the statute were interpreted to mandate local residency preferences, which would limit schools' ability to serve diverse student populations.

### IV. RESPONDENT'S ADMISSIONS PROCESS COMPLIES WITH APPLICABLE LAW

PACT Charter School admits students through a random lottery system when applications exceed available capacity. This lottery system treats all applicants equally, without regard to residency. This approach is fully consistent with § 124D.10, subd. 9, which does not require—and indeed does not authorize—a residency preference in admissions.

Appellants' son was selected for admission through this lottery. His selection demonstrates that the lottery system operates fairly and does not discriminate against Ramsey residents. Appellant Dehen's daughter was not selected, but this does not give rise to a legal claim because no residency preference exists under the statute.

### V. APPELLANTS' CLAIMS ARE WITHOUT MERIT

#### A. No Declaratory Judgment Is Warranted

Appellants seek a declaratory judgment that § 124D.10, subd. 9, creates a residency preference. However, because the statute's language is clear and unambiguous, there is no basis for a declaratory judgment in Appellants' favor. The statute means what it says—it permits admission from certain districts under certain conditions but does not mandate preferential treatment for local residents.

#### B. The Claims Are Partially Moot

As to Appellant Look, his claim is moot because his son was admitted to PACT Charter School through the lottery. He received the relief he sought (admission for his son), and there is no remaining controversy requiring resolution.

As to Appellant Dehen's daughter, she has no statutory right to admission based on residency. The statute does not guarantee admission to Ramsey residents—it merely permits admission under certain conditions. Because no preference exists, Appellant Dehen cannot demonstrate the injury required to maintain this action.

> "A charter school shall admit students who reside in the district in which the charter school is located, or in a neighboring district, or in a district that has a cooperative agreement with the charter school authorizer, if the school has space available after admitting students who reside in the district in which the charter school is located."

The use of the mandatory term "shall" creates an obligation for charter schools to admit district residents. The conditional phrase "if the school has space available after admitting students who reside in the district" establishes the hierarchy: local residents must be admitted first, and only when capacity remains may schools consider students from outside the district.

The Respondent argues that this language is merely "permissive." However, this interpretation flips the statutory structure on its head. If the legislature intended a purely permissive approach, it would have used "may" rather than "shall." The word "shall" in statutory construction denotes a mandatory duty. See **State ex rel. Jenson v. Penney**, 216 N.W.2d 152, 158 (Minn. 1974) (holding that "shall" creates a mandatory obligation unless the context indicates otherwise).

The condition "if the school has space available after admitting students who reside in the district" does not undermine the preference—it confirms it. The statute explicitly requires that local residents be admitted first, with non-local students considered only "after" this requirement is satisfied. This is the very definition of a preference.

## II. THE RESPONDENT'S CITED PRECEDENT DOES NOT CONTROL

The Respondent relies heavily on **In re Charter School of St. Paul**, 788 N.W.2d 692 (Minn. App. 2010), but that case is distinguishable. In *Charter School of St. Paul*, the court addressed whether the statute required charter schools to give preference to residents of the school district over students residing in neighboring districts with cooperative agreements. The court held that it did not, because the statute explicitly permitted admission from neighboring districts.

Critically, however, the *Charter School of St. Paul* court did not hold that no preference exists at all. The question before that court was whether residents of the immediate district must be preferred over residents of neighboring districts with agreements. The court answered "no" because the statute treats these categories similarly once local residents are accommodated.

The present case presents a different question: whether district residents must be preferred over non-resident applicants from districts without cooperative agreements. The statute answers this clearly: students "who reside in the district" must be admitted first, and only then may students from other districts be considered "if the school has space available."

The *Charter School of St. Paul* court explicitly acknowledged that the statute "allows charter schools to admit students from the district, neighboring districts, or districts with cooperative agreements." This sequential listing reinforces the hierarchical structure: district residents first, then others.

## III. THE STATUTORY PURPOSE SUPPORTS A RESIDENCY PREFERENCE

The Respondent argues that imposing a residency preference would contradict the charter school law's purpose of promoting school choice. This argument fails.

Minnesota Statute § 124D.01 states that charter schools were created to "improve student learning and increase learning opportunities for all students." A residency preference does not contradict this purpose—it advances it. Local families deserve priority access to schools located in their communities. This is particularly important for families who may lack transportation resources to access schools in distant districts.

Furthermore, the legislature granted charter schools autonomy under § 124D.10, subdivision 4, but this autonomy operates within the framework established by the statute. The admission provisions in subdivision 9 represent the legislature's balance between autonomy and local access. The Respondent cannot invoke "autonomy" to escape the statute's clear mandate.

## IV. APPLICATION TO THE FACTS

PACT Charter School is located in Ramsey. Under § 124D.10, subdivision 9, the school must admit Ramsey residents before considering non-resident applicants. The school's lottery system, which treats all applicants equally regardless of residency, violates this statutory requirement.

Appellant Look's son was selected through the lottery, but this does not render the case moot. The question presented is whether the school's admission policy violates the statute—a question that remains live and capable of repetition. See **Firefighters' Relief Ass'n v. City of Minneapolis**, 229 N.W.2d 855, 860 (Minn. 1975) (holding that a case is not moot if the controversy is capable of repetition).

Regarding Appellant Dehen, his daughter applied for admission but was not selected through the lottery. Because the school failed to implement the required residency preference, Dehen's daughter was denied the priority to which she was entitled under Minnesota law.

## V. CONCLUSION

The statute's plain language creates a mandatory preference for district residents. The conditional phrase "if the school has space available after admitting students who reside in the district" establishes a clear admission hierarchy. The Respondent's interpretation would read the word "priority" into the statute, but it is the Respondent who seeks to add flexibility where the legislature mandated specificity.

We urge this Court to reverse the district court's judgment and hold that Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, extends a proximity preference to residents of the municipality where a charter school is located.

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The district court granted summary judgment to Respondent PACT Charter School. On appeal, the interpretation of a statute is a question of law reviewed *de novo*. *Malmstadt v. Flood*, 678 N.W.2d 44, 49 (Minn. 2004). This Court must interpret the statute's words according to their plain and ordinary meaning. *State v. Thiel*, 600 N.W.2d 272, 276 (Minn. 1999). When statutory language is clear and unambiguous, the court must apply it as written without adding or subtracting terms. *Id.*

**II. ANALYSIS**

The sole issue presented is whether Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, creates a mandatory "proximity preference" requiring charter schools to prioritize admission for residents of the municipality where the school is located. Having considered the closing arguments of both parties, the Court concludes that it does not.

**A. Plain Language of the Statute**

The Appellants argue that the mandatory term "shall" in the phrase "shall admit students who reside in the district" creates a binding obligation to prioritize local residents. They rely on *State ex rel. Jenson v. Penney*, 216 N.W.2d 152, 158 (Minn. 1974), for the proposition that "shall" denotes a mandatory duty. While "shall" generally imposes a duty, the Appellants' interpretation ignores the critical conditional language that follows.

The statute provides that a charter school may admit students from neighboring districts or districts with cooperative agreements "if the school has space available after admitting students who reside in the district in which the charter school is located." The Respondent correctly argues that this conditional structure defines the *eligibility* of non-resident students rather than establishing a hierarchy of admission priority for resident students. The phrase "if the school has space available after admitting students who reside in the district" sets a condition for when non-local students may be considered; it does not mandate that local residents must be admitted *before* non-residents in a queue or that non-residents cannot be admitted simultaneously if space exists.

The Appellants ask this Court to read a "priority" or "preference" into the text where none exists. As noted in *State v. Thiel*, 600 N.W.2d 272, 276 (Minn. 1999), courts "must apply [statutory language] as written without adding or subtracting terms." Because the legislature did not include terms such as "priority," "preference," or "first," the Court cannot insert them to create a residency mandate.

**B. Controlling Precedent**

The interpretation of § 124D.10, subd. 9 is governed by the binding precedent of *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 788 N.W.2d 692 (Minn. App. 2010). The Respondent accurately cites this case, in which the court explicitly held that the statute "does not require charter schools to give preference to residents of the school district in which the school is located." *Id.* at 697.

The Appellants attempt to distinguish *Charter School of St. Paul* by arguing that it addressed only the preference between district residents and residents of neighboring districts with cooperative agreements, rather than the general hierarchy between residents and non-residents. This distinction is unpersuasive. The *Charter School of St. Paul* court analyzed the specific statutory language at issue here—"if the school has space available after admitting students who reside in the district"—and concluded that it "does not establish a hierarchy of admission priority." *Id.* at 698. This broad holding applies with equal force to the Appellants' claim. The Appellants have offered no credible reason to disregard this binding precedent, nor have they identified any material difference in the statutory text. This Court is bound to follow *Charter School of St. Paul*.

**C. Statutory Purpose and Structure**

The Respondent argues that reading a mandatory residency preference into the statute would contradict the legislative purpose of promoting school choice and autonomy. Minnesota's charter school law is intended to "improve student learning and increase learning opportunities for all students." Minn. Stat. § 124D.01. Furthermore, § 124D.10, subd. 4 grants charter schools broad autonomy over admissions.

The Appellants contend that a residency preference advances the goal of serving local families. However, the statute's structure suggests a balance between local access and the broader goal of statewide school choice. Imposing a rigid residency mandate would eviscerate the autonomy granted to charter schools and transform them into de facto neighborhood schools, restricting school choice for families across the state. The Respondent's interpretation aligns with the statutory structure and purpose, whereas the Appellants' proposed reading would artificially limit enrollment.

**D. Application to Facts and Standing**

PACT Charter School utilizes a random lottery system for admissions when applications exceed capacity. This system treats all applicants equally without regard to residency. Because § 124D.10, subd. 9 does not authorize or require a residency preference, the school's lottery system is fully compliant with the statute.

The Respondent further argues that Appellant Look's claim is moot because his son was admitted to the school through the lottery system. The Appellants assert that the issue is "capable of repetition," citing *Firefighters' Relief Ass'n v. City of Minneapolis*, 229 N.W.2d 855, 860 (Minn. 1975). Even if the legal issue were capable of repetition, Look has received the relief he sought personally, and his individual claim is moot. Under *State v. Nguyen*, 741 N.W.2d 128, 133 (Minn. 2007), summary judgment is appropriate when no genuine issue of material fact exists and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

Regarding Appellant Dehen, the statute grants no right to admission based solely on residency. Consequently, Dehen cannot demonstrate a cognizable injury sufficient to maintain this action, as the failure to be selected in a neutral lottery does not constitute a statutory violation.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the plain language of Minnesota Statute § 124D.10, subdivision 9, and the controlling precedent of *In re Charter School of St. Paul*, 788 N.W.2d 692 (Minn. App. 2010), the statute does not create a mandatory proximity preference for residents of the municipality where a charter school is located. The Appellants' attempts to distinguish controlling precedent and to insert a preference into the statute are unavailing. The district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of Respondent PACT Charter School.

**IT IS HEREBY ORDERED:** That the district court's grant of summary judgment to Respondent is affirmed.
