Plaintiff, Joseph Michael Angelichio, filed a motion pursuant to Pa. ## I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN IMPOSING DEFAULT JUDGMENT WHERE LESSER SANCTIONS WERE AVAILABLE

The Pennsylvania Rules of Civil Procedure provide that sanctions for discovery violations should be proportionate to the violation. Pennsylvania law establishes that default judgment is an extreme sanction that should be reserved for the most egregious cases where lesser sanctions have proven ineffective or where the prejudice cannot be cured. The trial court's decision to enter judgment against Defendant based solely on his failure to appear for depositions, without adequate consideration of lesser sanctions, constitutes an abuse of discretion.

In *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa. Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa. Super. 1994), this Court held that before imposing the severe sanction of default judgment, the trial court must balance multiple factors including: the nature and severity of the violation, the willfulness or bad faith of the offending party, the prejudice to the opposing party, the possibility of curing that prejudice, and the importance of the precluded evidence. The trial court failed to properly conduct this balancing analysis.

Furthermore, in *Smith v. Bostic*, 872 A.2d 1238 (Pa. Super. 2005), this Court emphasized that default judgment is appropriate only when the party's conduct has been so egregious that lesser sanctions would be futile or when the prejudice is truly irreparable. The record here does not support such a finding.

The defendant appeared for no depositions between 2007 and his death in 2012—a period of approximately five years. During this time, the plaintiff had multiple opportunities to seek court intervention earlier. The plaintiff first requested deposition dates in June 2007, yet waited until May 2009 to file his first motion for contempt and sanctions. The plaintiff was aware that the defendant was the only eyewitness, yet waited over eighteen months after the initial request before taking any formal action. The plaintiff could have sought court-ordered sanctions much earlier in the process. The plaintiff hired a private investigator and located the defendant, demonstrating that the plaintiff had the means to compel attendance earlier in the litigation. The plaintiff served a subpoena for April 27, 2009, yet when the defendant refused to appear, the plaintiff waited until May 18, 2009, to file a motion for contempt—only a three-week delay after the scheduled deposition.

The plaintiff filed a second motion for contempt on May 10, 2010, but still waited nearly another year before the defendant's death in January 2012. This timeline demonstrates that the plaintiff did not pursue the deposition with the urgency that the alleged importance of the testimony would suggest.

## III. THE ALLEGED PREJUDICE IS UNFOUNDED AND NOT SUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT DEFAULT JUDGMENT

These findings establish that the fire originated from cooking equipment and was exacerbated by an improperly stored kerosene container. The defendant was not the owner or occupant in control of the premises; he was a tenant who performed maintenance in exchange for reduced rent. The plaintiff has presented no evidence linking the defendant's conduct—whether by act or omission—to the ignition of the fire.

The defendant gave an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire, in which he denied that he was the cause of the fire. **PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for a trial court’s imposition of sanctions for discovery violations is whether the court abused its discretion. An abuse of discretion exists if the law is overridden or misapplied, or if the judgment is manifestly unreasonable, arbitrary, or capricious. The decision to impose the severe sanction of default judgment is committed to the sound discretion of the trial court and will be reversed only upon a showing of abuse of that discretion. See *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994); *Smith v. Bostic*, 872 A.2d 1238 (Pa.Super. 2005).

**II. ANALYSIS**

The central issue is whether the trial court abused its discretion in entering a default judgment against the Defendant as a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders. Pennsylvania law mandates that a trial court must balance several factors before imposing the extreme sanction of default judgment. These factors include: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the willfulness or bad faith of the offending party; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing that prejudice; and (5) the importance of the precluded evidence. *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994).

**A. Nature and Severity of the Violation and Willfulness**

The record demonstrates a history of non-compliance spanning nearly five years. The Defendant failed to appear for scheduled depositions in 2007, 2008, and 2010. While the Defense argues that the Defendant’s counsel was unable to locate him for a period, the facts show that once the Plaintiff located the Defendant via a private investigator in 2009, the Defendant actively refused to appear for a videotaped deposition. Subsequently, the Defendant cancelled agreed-upon dates in November and December 2010. This pattern of evasion and refusal, culminating in the Defendant's death before testimony could be secured, indicates a high degree of severity and willfulness in frustrating the judicial process. The Defense’s argument that the refusal was merely a dispute over the "videotaped format" is unpersuasive given the timeline of cancellations and the ultimate failure to provide any testimony whatsoever.

**B. Prejudice to the Opposing Party and Possibility of Cure**

The Defendant was the sole eyewitness to the fire that caused the death of Tina Marie Plotts. The physical evidence establishes the cause of the fire (an unattended pot) and its rapid progression (kerosene leakage), but it does not establish the critical element of liability regarding the Defendant's conduct prior to the fire. Specifically, the physical evidence cannot identify who left the pot unattended or who was responsible for the defective kerosene container. As the sole eyewitness, the Defendant’s testimony was indispensable to determining whether his actions or omissions were a substantial factor in causing the fire.

Due to the Defendant’s death, this evidence is now permanently lost. The Defense argues that the prejudice is unfounded because the physical evidence is sufficient. However, in a negligence case, the Plaintiff must prove the defendant's breach of duty. Without the eyewitness testimony to explain the events leading up to the fire, the Plaintiff is irreparably prejudiced and cannot establish the necessary link between the Defendant's conduct and the fire's origin. As noted in *Smith v. Bostic*, 872 A.2d 1238 (Pa.Super. 2005), default judgment is appropriate where the prejudice is truly irreparable. Here, the death of the sole witness makes the prejudice incurable.

**C. Importance of the Precluded Evidence**

The importance of the precluded evidence cannot be overstated. The Defense contends that the Defendant’s unsworn statement to a state trooper denying responsibility renders his potential deposition testimony merely cumulative or self-serving. This argument ignores the fact that a sworn deposition would have subjected the Defendant to cross-examination regarding his actions that evening, his knowledge of the kerosene container, and the reasons for the unattended pot. The loss of this opportunity for cross-examination deprives the Plaintiff of the primary means to prove liability against the only available defendant present in the residence.

**D. Consideration of Lesser Sanctions**

The trial court is required to consider whether lesser sanctions would be appropriate. *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994). The Defense suggests that monetary sanctions, adverse inferences, or preclusion of certain defenses would have sufficed. However, where the central evidence needed to prove the case is permanently lost due to the Defendant's prolonged non-compliance, monetary sanctions cannot compensate the Plaintiff for the inability to prove his case. An adverse inference, while helpful, may not be sufficient to establish the specific factual causation required for negligence. Given the egregious nature of the delay and the permanent loss of testimony, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in determining that lesser sanctions would be futile.

**E. Plaintiff’s Diligence**

The Defense argues that the Plaintiff failed to diligently pursue the deposition, pointing to delays between 2007 and 2009. The facts show that the Plaintiff requested dates in 2007, attempted to schedule in 2008, and upon learning the Defendant was missing, hired a private investigator in 2009 to locate him. The Plaintiff filed motions for contempt in May 2009 and May 2010. This timeline reflects a reasonable effort to secure testimony despite the Defendant's evasion. It does not constitute a lack of diligence sufficient to shift the blame for the lost evidence to the Plaintiff.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court properly balanced the equities and did not abuse its discretion in entering default judgment against the Defendant. The Defendant’s willful non-compliance over several years directly resulted in the permanent loss of critical evidence, causing irreparable prejudice to the Plaintiff. Under the standards set forth in *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994) and *Smith v. Bostic*, 872 A.2d 1238 (Pa.Super. 2005), the sanction of default judgment was appropriate.

**IT IS ORDERED that the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, are affirmed.**

**UPDATED RULING**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review for a trial court’s imposition of sanctions for discovery violations is whether the court abused its discretion. An abuse of discretion exists if the law is overridden or misapplied, or if the judgment is manifestly unreasonable, arbitrary, or capricious. The decision to impose the severe sanction of default judgment is committed to the sound discretion of the trial court and will be reversed only upon a showing of abuse of that discretion. See *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994); *Smith v. Bostic*, 872 A.2d 1238 (Pa.Super. 2005).

**II. ANALYSIS**

The central issue is whether the trial court abused its discretion in entering a default judgment against the Defendant as a sanction for failure to comply with discovery orders. Pennsylvania law mandates that a trial court must balance several factors before imposing the extreme sanction of default judgment. These factors include: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the willfulness or bad faith of the offending party; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing that prejudice; and (5) the importance of the precluded evidence. *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994).

**A. Nature and Severity of the Violation and Willfulness**

The Defense argues that the violations were not severe or willful, characterizing the period from 2007 to early 2009 as an inability to locate the Defendant rather than evasion, and characterizing the subsequent refusal to appear as a legitimate procedural dispute over the videotaped format pursuant to Pa. R.C.P. 4017.1. While the Defense correctly notes that counsel represented an inability to locate the Defendant for a period, the record demonstrates a broader pattern of non-compliance. Even after the Plaintiff successfully located the Defendant in 2009, the Defendant failed to appear for scheduled depositions in November and December 2010. The Defense's contention that the refusal was solely based on the videotape format does not explain the cancellations of agreed-upon dates in late 2010. The trial court characterized this as a "dismal record of non-compliance," a finding supported by the timeline of missed appearances and cancellations spanning several years. While the "videotape dispute" may explain some of the friction, it does not excuse the ultimate failure to provide any testimony whatsoever prior to the Defendant's death.

**B. Prejudice to the Opposing Party and Possibility of Cure**

The Defense argues that the prejudice is not "truly irreparable" because the physical evidence establishes the cause of the fire (an unattended pot and kerosene leakage) and that alternative remedies, such as adverse inference instructions, would suffice. This argument misinterprets the nature of the prejudice. The physical evidence establishes the *mechanism* of the fire but does not establish the *human element* of liability. Specifically, the physical evidence cannot identify who left the pot unattended or who was responsible for the defective kerosene container. The Defense's assertion that the physical evidence "demonstrates that the fire was accidental and not caused by any affirmative act of negligence by the defendant" is a legal conclusion not supported by the factual record. The Defendant was the sole eyewitness to the events preceding the fire.

As held in *Smith v. Bostic*, 872 A.2d 1238 (Pa.Super. 2005), default judgment is appropriate where the prejudice is "truly irreparable." However, an adverse inference is a poor substitute for actual testimony regarding the specific details of the Defendant's actions and knowledge on the night of the fire. Without the ability to cross-examine the Defendant, the Plaintiff cannot effectively prove that the Defendant's conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in finding the prejudice incurable.

The loss of this opportunity for cross-examination deprives the Plaintiff of the primary means to prove liability against the only available defendant present in the residence. The importance of this evidence to the Plaintiff's case cannot be overstated.

**D. Consideration of Lesser Sanctions**

The trial court is required to consider whether lesser sanctions would be appropriate. *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994). ## INTRODUCTION

Your Honor, Members of the Court, the Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in opposition to the Plaintiff's request to affirm the trial court's orders entering default judgment against the Defendant, Tyrone Diefenderfer. The Defense acknowledges that the Defendant failed to appear for scheduled depositions on multiple occasions; however, the trial court's imposition of the extreme sanction of default judgment constitutes an abuse of discretion that must be reversed. ## I. THE TRIAL COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY FAILING TO PROPERLY WEIGH THE BERMAN FACTORS

The Defense acknowledges that the standard of review requires this Court to determine whether the trial court abused its discretion in imposing sanctions. *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994). However, an abuse of discretion exists where the trial court's judgment is "manifestly unreasonable, arbitrary, or capricious." Here, the trial court's conclusion that default judgment was the "most appropriate sanction" fails to account for critical facts that weigh against such a severe remedy.

### A. The Nature and Severity of the Violations Do Not Warrant Default Judgment

The Defense maintains that the trial court overestimated the severity of the discovery violations. The record demonstrates that from 2007 to early 2009, the Defendant's counsel genuinely could not locate the Defendant. This is not a case of willful evasion during that initial period—it was an inability to locate a client who was, apparently, avoiding contact. Once the Plaintiff hired a private investigator and located the Defendant in 2009, the core dispute became the format of the deposition—specifically, whether the Defendant could be compelled to submit to a videotaped deposition under Pa. R.C.P. 4017.1.

The Defense asserts that the Defendant's refusal to appear was based on a legitimate procedural concern regarding the videotaped format, not pure obstinacy. While the Defense acknowledges that the Defendant should have sought a court ruling on this issue rather than simply refusing to appear, this does not rise to the level of severity that warrants the death penalty of default judgment. The Defendant never testified under oath regarding the fire, but he did provide an unsworn statement to a state trooper on the night of the fire denying responsibility.

The Plaintiff's characterization of the Defendant's conduct as "willful obstruction" conflates a procedural dispute with criminal obstruction of justice. The *Berman* court explicitly stated that courts must consider whether the violation was "willful" before imposing default judgment. The Defendant's objection to the videotaped format, while ultimately unsuccessful, was not frivolous—Rule 4017.1 does establish specific requirements for videotaped depositions, and the Defendant had a right to seek enforcement of his understanding of the rule. The trial court should have resolved this dispute rather than allowing it to fester into a basis for default judgment.

### B. The Defendant Did Not Act in Bad Faith

The Defense strongly contests the characterization that the Defendant acted in "bad faith." The term "bad faith" in the context of discovery sanctions requires more than mere negligence or even recalcitrance—it requires a showing that the party "deliberately" obstructed the discovery process. *Smith v. Bostic*, 872 A.2d 1238 (Pa.Super. 2005).

Here, the record shows that the Defendant's counsel repeatedly represented that he could not locate the Defendant. While this may have been negligent, it does not constitute the type of deliberate, malicious conduct that *Berman* and *Smith* contemplate. The Defense notes that the Plaintiff has pointed to no evidence in the record establishing that the Defendant was intentionally hiding from discovery during the 2007-2009 period. The Plaintiff's counsel received the Defendant's last known address and Social Security number on January 7, 2009, yet waited until April 2009 to hire a private investigator. The Defense submits that if the Plaintiff had exercised greater diligence in locating the Defendant, the deposition could have been scheduled earlier—before the Defendant's death in January 2012.

The Plaintiff's argument that he "reasonably relied" on the Defendant counsel's representations does not absolve him of the duty to exercise due diligence. The *Berman* court held that the plaintiff's diligence is a factor to be considered. Here, the Plaintiff's two-year delay in pursuing alternative means of locating the Defendant undermines his claim of prejudice. If the Plaintiff had acted more promptly, the Defendant might have been deposed before his death.

## III. THE PREJUDICE TO THE PLAINTIFF WAS NOT IRREPARABLE

The Plaintiff's claim of "irreparable prejudice" relies on the assertion that the Defendant was the "sole eyewitness" to the fire. The Defense does not dispute that the Defendant was present in the residence on the night of the fire. However, the Defense submits that the prejudice has been overstated and that the trial court erred in finding it irreparable.

### A. Physical Evidence Establishes the Cause of the Fire

The record contains substantial physical evidence establishing the cause and origin of the fire. The cause was determined to be an unattended pot on the right rear burner of the electric stove. The rapid progression of the fire was caused by kerosene leaking from a defective kerosene container stored near the stove.

This physical evidence establishes that the fire was accidental—not the result of any affirmative act of negligence by the Defendant. The unattended pot and the defective kerosene container are inanimate objects that ignited without human intervention. The Defense submits that this physical evidence is more reliable than any testimony the Defendant could have provided, as it is based on scientific analysis rather than human memory or self-interest.

### B. Alternative Theories of Liability Remain Available

The Plaintiff sued the Defendant as the sole eyewitness present at the scene. However, the Plaintiff also named the landlords, Betsy Jo Myers and Joanne E. Myers, as defendants. The landlords owned the premises and, according to the facts, the Defendant performed maintenance for them in exchange for reduced rent.

The Defense submits that the Plaintiff can pursue alternative theories of liability against the landlords without the Defendant's testimony. The landlords had a duty to maintain the premises in a safe condition, including ensuring that defective kerosene containers were not stored near the stove. The Plaintiff can pursue discovery from the landlords to establish their negligence. The Defendant's testimony is not essential to proving this theory.

### C. Adverse Inference Instructions Would Have Cured Any Prejudice

The Defense maintains that the trial court could have entered an adverse inference instruction rather than default judgment. An adverse inference instruction would have allowed the Plaintiff to argue to the jury that the Defendant's refusal to appear for a deposition warranted an inference that his testimony would have been unfavorable to him.

The *Smith* court acknowledged that adverse inference instructions are a less severe sanction that may cure prejudice in some cases. Here, where the Defendant provided an unsworn statement to a state trooper denying responsibility, an adverse inference instruction would have allowed the Plaintiff to argue that the Defendant's refusal to submit to a deposition further supports an inference of negligence.

The Defense submits that the trial court erred by failing to consider this lesser sanction. The prejudice to the Plaintiff, while significant, was not so great that it could not be cured through an adverse inference instruction.

## IV. LESSER SANCTIONS WERE AVAILABLE AND APPROPRIATE

The Defense emphasizes that the trial court was required to consider lesser sanctions before imposing default judgment. *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994). The trial court's failure to adequately consider these alternatives constitutes an abuse of discretion.

### A. Monetary Sanctions

The trial court could have imposed monetary sanctions on the Defendant or his estate. Monetary sanctions would have compensated the Plaintiff for any costs incurred in connection with the failed depositions without depriving the Defendant of his day in court.

### B. Adverse Inference Instructions

As discussed above, an adverse inference instruction would have allowed the Plaintiff to present his case to a jury while still holding the Defendant accountable for his failure to participate in discovery.

### C. Preclusion of Defenses

The trial court could have precluded the Defendant from presenting certain defenses at trial. For example, the trial court could have precluded the Defendant from arguing that he was not present in the kitchen or that he had no knowledge of the kerosene container.

The Defense submits that any of these lesser sanctions would have been appropriate in this case. The trial court's failure to consider them was error.

## V. THE DEFAULT JUDGMENT IS DISPROPORTIONATE AND UNJUST

Finally, the Defense submits that the default judgment is disproportionate to the violations and unjust to the Defendant. The Defendant has never been afforded an opportunity to defend himself on the merits. The Plaintiff has never proven that the Defendant was negligent or that his conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire.

The default judgment effectively imposes liability without a trial, based solely on the Defendant's failure to participate in discovery. This is fundamentally unfair. The Defendant died in January 2012, and his estate should not be held liable for damages that have never been proven.

The Defense notes that the Plaintiff has not produced any evidence establishing that the Defendant left the pot unattended or was responsible for the defective kerosene container. The physical evidence points to an accidental fire, not to any affirmative act of negligence by the Defendant. The default judgment is based on speculation and conjecture, not on proof.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Defense respectfully requests that this Court reverse the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, and remand this matter for further proceedings.

The Defense acknowledges that the Defendant failed to appear for scheduled depositions. However, the trial court's imposition of default judgment as a sanction for these failures was an abuse of discretion. The trial court failed to properly weigh the *Berman* factors, failed to consider lesser sanctions, and imposed a sanction that is disproportionate to the violations and unjust to the Defendant.

The Plaintiff has not demonstrated irreparable prejudice. The physical evidence establishes the cause of the fire, alternative theories of liability remain available, and an adverse inference instruction would have cured any prejudice. The default judgment should be reversed.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The standard of review applicable to the trial court’s imposition of sanctions for discovery violations is whether the court abused its discretion. An abuse of discretion occurs if the law is overridden or misapplied, or if the judgment is manifestly unreasonable, arbitrary, or capricious. The decision to impose the severe sanction of default judgment is committed to the sound discretion of the trial court and will be reversed only upon a showing of abuse of that discretion. See *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994); *Smith v. Bostic*, 872 A.2d 1238 (Pa.Super. 2005).

**II. ANALYSIS**

The Appellant argues that the trial court abused its discretion by entering a default judgment against the estate of Tyrone Diefenderfer. We must determine whether the trial court’s decision was manifestly unreasonable given the totality of the circumstances. Pennsylvania law requires a trial court to balance several factors before imposing the extreme sanction of default judgment: (1) the nature and severity of the violation; (2) the willfulness or bad faith of the offending party; (3) the prejudice to the opposing party; (4) the possibility of curing that prejudice; and (5) the importance of the precluded evidence. *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994).

**A. Nature and Severity of the Violation and Willfulness**

The Defense contends that the violations were not severe or willful, characterizing the period from 2007 to early 2009 as an inability to locate the Defendant and the subsequent refusals as a legitimate procedural dispute regarding the videotaped format of the deposition under Pa. R.C.P. 4017.1. While the record supports that the Defendant’s counsel initially represented an inability to locate the Defendant, the timeline of non-compliance extends well beyond that period.

After the Plaintiff successfully located the Defendant in 2009, the Defendant failed to appear for scheduled depositions in November and December 2010. Specifically, on November 9, 2010, Defendant’s counsel notified the Plaintiff that the Defendant would not appear for a deposition scheduled for the next day. Subsequently, on November 30, 2010, counsel cancelled a December 2, 2010 deposition, claiming the Defendant was out of town. The Defense’s argument that these cancellations were solely based on a good-faith dispute over videotaping is unpersuasive. A procedural objection to the format of a deposition does not justify a categorical refusal to appear or the cancellation of agreed-upon dates. The trial court characterized this history as a "dismal record of non-compliance." Given the repeated failures to appear and the cancellation of dates after the Defendant was located, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in finding the violations severe and willful.

**B. Prejudice to the Opposing Party and Possibility of Cure**

The Defense argues that the prejudice was not "truly irreparable" because the physical evidence established the cause of the fire and alternative remedies, such as adverse inference instructions, would suffice. We disagree. The physical evidence establishes the *mechanism* of the fire—an unattended pot and a leaking kerosene container—but it does not establish the *human element* of liability. The physical evidence cannot identify who left the pot unattended or who was responsible for storing the defective kerosene container near the stove.

As held in *Smith v. Bostic*, 872 A.2d 1238 (Pa.Super. 2005), default judgment is appropriate where the prejudice is "truly irreparable." Here, the Defendant was the sole eyewitness to the events preceding the fire. His death in January 2012 means that his testimony is permanently lost. The Defense suggests that an adverse inference instruction could cure this prejudice. However, an adverse inference is a poor substitute for actual testimony regarding the specific details of the Defendant's actions and knowledge on the night of the fire. Without the ability to cross-examine the Defendant, the Plaintiff cannot effectively prove that the Defendant's conduct was a substantial factor in causing the fire. Therefore, the trial court did not abuse its discretion in finding the prejudice incurable.

**C. Importance of the Precluded Evidence**

The Defense contends that the Defendant's unsworn statement to a state trooper denying responsibility renders his potential deposition testimony merely cumulative or self-serving. This argument ignores the critical distinction between an unsworn statement and a deposition. A sworn deposition would have subjected the Defendant to cross-examination regarding his actions that evening, his knowledge of the kerosene container, and the reasons for the unattended pot. The loss of this opportunity for cross-examination deprives the Plaintiff of the primary means to prove liability against the only available defendant present in the residence. The importance of this evidence to the Plaintiff's case is paramount.

**D. Consideration of Lesser Sanctions**

The trial court is required to consider whether lesser sanctions would be appropriate. *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994). The Defense suggests that monetary sanctions, adverse inferences, or preclusion of certain defenses would have sufficed. However, where the central evidence needed to prove the case is permanently lost due to the Defendant's prolonged non-compliance, monetary sanctions cannot compensate the Plaintiff for the inability to prove his case. As noted in *Smith v. Bostic*, 872 A.2d 1238 (Pa.Super. 2005), when the central evidence needed to prove a case is permanently lost due to a party's conduct, lesser sanctions are futile. The trial court’s determination that lesser sanctions would be inadequate given the permanent loss of testimony was within its discretion.

**E. Plaintiff’s Diligence**

The Defense argues that the Plaintiff failed to exercise due diligence, pointing to a two-year delay between 2007 and 2009 before hiring a private investigator. While the Plaintiff's delay in locating the Defendant is noted, it does not absolve the Defendant of his obligation to comply with discovery once he was located. The record shows that once the Plaintiff located the Defendant in 2009, the Defendant actively refused to appear for a videotaped deposition and subsequently cancelled agreed-upon dates in late 2010. The Plaintiff filed motions for contempt in May 2009 and May 2010. The ultimate loss of testimony was caused by the Defendant's failure to appear *after* being located, not by the initial delay in finding him. The trial court did not abuse its discretion in determining that the Plaintiff's actions were sufficient to warrant the relief sought.

**III. CONCLUSION**

Based on the foregoing analysis, the trial court properly balanced the equities and did not abuse its discretion in entering default judgment against the Defendant. The Defense’s arguments regarding the "videotape dispute" and the Plaintiff's diligence do not overcome the fact that the Defendant's non-compliance directly resulted in the permanent loss of critical evidence, causing irreparable prejudice to the Plaintiff. Under the standards set forth in *Berman v. Kass*, 435 Pa.Super. 194, 645 A.2d 222 (Pa.Super. 1994) and *Smith v. Bostic*, 872 A.2d 1238 (Pa.Super. 2005), the sanction of default judgment was appropriate.

**IT IS ORDERED that the orders of the trial court dated February 25, 2014, January 30, 2014, and October 9, 2013, are affirmed.**
