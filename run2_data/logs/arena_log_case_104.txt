## In the Matter of Lisa Margaret Howard, Petitioner

Your Honor, the Defense respectfully submits this closing statement in support of the petition for a three-month actual suspension and respectfully urges the Court to adopt the referee's recommendation rather than the prosecution's request for a six-month suspension. While we appreciate the prosecution's acknowledgment of the mitigating factors in this case, we submit that the Court has the discretion—consistent with binding precedent—to impose the minimum three-month sanction, which adequately serves the purposes of attorney discipline: protection of the public, the courts, and the legal profession.

---

## I. THE COURT'S DISCRETION UNDER *IN RE SUTTER*

The starting point for this Court's analysis must be *In re Sutter*, 21 Cal. 3d 243 (Cal. 1978), which establishes that the Standards for Attorney Sanctions are "guidelines" that provide "flexibility" rather than "rigid mandates." Id. at 249. The Defense does not dispute that the Standards create presumptive penalties; rather, we submit that the Court retains inherent authority to depart from these presumptions where the record demonstrates that a lesser sanction adequately protects the public and the profession.

The prosecution attempts to cabin this discretion by arguing that *In re Rappaport* and *In re Kroll* establish an absolute six-month floor for misappropriation. This reading is incorrect. Neither case holds that six months is the *minimum* possible sanction for misappropriation; rather, both cases represent fact-specific applications of mitigation principles to reduce the presumptive one-year suspension. The *Sutter* Court expressly rejected a rigid application of the Standards, holding that "the unique characteristics of each case" must inform the discipline imposed. *Sutter*, 21 Cal. 3d at 249.

---

## II. *IN RE RAPPAPORT* AND *IN RE KROLL* DO NOT ESTABLISH A MANDATORY FLOOR

The prosecution relies heavily on *In re Rappaport*, 27 Cal. 3d 813 (Cal. 1980), but misreads its holding. In *Rappaport*, the Court reduced a one-year suspension to six months based on the attorney's "remarkable" rehabilitation. Id. at 823. The Court emphasized that the purpose of discipline is "protection of the public," not punishment. Id. at 822. However, the Court in *Rappaport* did not hold that six months is the *floor* below which no mitigation can go; rather, it exercised its discretion to reduce the penalty based on the specific facts before it.

The prosecution argues that the *Rappaport* Court "explicitly declined to impose a lesser sanction." This overstates the holding. The *Rappaport* Court was presented with a particular set of facts and determined that six months was appropriate for *that* attorney. The Defense does not ask this Court to impose a sanction below six months in the abstract; we ask that the Court exercise its discretion based on the *specific, extraordinary mitigating circumstances* present here—circumstances arguably more compelling than those in *Rappaport*.

Similarly, in *In re Kroll*, 43 Cal. 3d 1253 (Cal. 1987), the Court upheld a reduction from the presumptive minimum based on "compelling evidence of rehabilitation." Id. at 1264. The prosecution is correct that *Kroll* did not impose a three-month suspension. But *Kroll* also did not hold that three months is *impossible*; it simply applied the facts before it. The Defense submits that the facts here—where the petitioner has maintained sobriety for over two and a half years, made full restitution, has no prior discipline, and has actively assisted other attorneys in recovery—present mitigation at least as compelling as that in *Kroll*.

---

## III. THE UNIQUE MITIGATING CIRCUMSTANCES OF THIS CASE

The record establishes extraordinary mitigating factors that distinguish this case from typical misappropriation matters:

**A. Single Act, Small Sum, Full Restitution**

The misappropriation involved a single act concerning a relatively small settlement of $3,500. The petitioner made complete restitution to the client. This is not a case of ongoing or repeated misconduct; it was an isolated lapse in judgment during a period of active addiction.

**B. Compelling Rehabilitation**

She has participated actively in Alcoholics Anonymous, Cocaine Anonymous, and The Other Bar, assisting others who suffer from chemical dependency. Expert testimony placed her in the "highest percentile" of those whose recovery programs are likely to be successful.

The referee found this evidence "compelling" and concluded that "petitioner's chemical dependency was well under control and predicted that, under appropriate supervision, it would continue to be so." This Court should give great weight to these findings.

**C. No Prior Discipline**

The petitioner has no prior disciplinary record in over six years of practice. This weighs significantly in mitigation.

**D. Delay in Prosecution**

Charges were not brought until nearly three years after the mispublication and restitution. This delay, while not dispositive, supports the conclusion that the petitioner does not pose an ongoing risk to the public.

## IV. THREE-MONTH SUSPENSION ADEQUATELY PROTECTS THE PUBLIC

A three-month actual suspension is not a trivial sanction. It removes the attorney from practice for a significant period, requires reinstatement proceedings, and carries lasting professional consequences. The public is adequately protected because:

The question is not what *typically* warrants a three-month suspension, but what *adequately protects the public* in this specific case. Given the extraordinary mitigation present here, three months is sufficient.

**FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court for a final decision following the submission of closing arguments by both the Prosecution and the Defense. The Petitioner, Lisa Margaret Howard, stipulated to wilfully misappropriating client funds in the amount of $3,500. The Court previously issued a preliminary ruling imposing a six-month actual suspension, finding this sanction to be the minimum permissible discipline under the circumstances. The Defense now urges the Court to adopt the referee’s recommendation of a three-month actual suspension, arguing that the Standards for Attorney Sanctions are flexible guidelines and that Petitioner’s extraordinary mitigation justifies a departure below the six-month threshold. The Prosecution argues that six months is the mandatory floor for misappropriation, regardless of mitigation, as established by binding precedent. Having considered the arguments presented, the Court affirms its preliminary ruling that a six-month actual suspension is the appropriate discipline.

**II. DISCUSSION**

**A. The Scope of Judicial Discretion Under *In re Sutter***

The Defense correctly identifies the principle established in *In re Sutter*, 21 Cal. 3d 243 (1978), that the Standards for Attorney Sanctions are guidelines rather than rigid mandates. The Defense argues that this discretion allows the Court to impose a three-month suspension based on the unique characteristics of Petitioner’s case. While the Court agrees that it possesses the discretion to depart from the presumptive penalties, this discretion is not without limits. The Court must balance the need for flexibility with the necessity of maintaining consistency and public confidence in the disciplinary system. The discretion afforded by *In re Sutter* does not grant the Court authority to disregard the specific precedential constraints that apply to the offense of misappropriation.

**B. The Precedential Floor for Misappropriation**

The central legal question is whether the mitigating factors in this case permit a reduction below six months. The Defense contends that *In re Rappaport*, 27 Cal. 3d 813 (1980), and *In re Kroll*, 43 Cal. 3d 1253 (1987), do not establish a mandatory floor but rather represent fact-specific outcomes. The Court finds this argument unpersuasive in light of the specific holdings in those cases.

In *In re Rappaport*, 27 Cal. 3d 813 (1980), the Court addressed an attorney who had misappropriated funds but demonstrated remarkable rehabilitation and made full restitution. Despite these mitigating factors, which the Defense argues are comparable to those present here, the Court explicitly declined to impose a sanction of less than six months. The *Rappaport* Court emphasized that the purpose of discipline is the protection of the public and that a lesser sanction would fail to maintain public confidence in the profession. By stopping at six months, the *Rappaport* decision implicitly established a baseline for intentional misappropriation. The Defense’s assertion that *Rappaport* was merely a fact-specific determination ignores the Court’s clear rationale that the gravity of the offense requires a minimum level of sanction to deter similar conduct and uphold the integrity of the profession.

Similarly, in *In re Kroll*, 43 Cal. 3d 1253 (1987), the Court recognized that "compelling evidence of rehabilitation" may justify a reduction from the standard presumptive discipline. However, *Kroll* did not sanction a three-month suspension for misappropriation. The Prosecution correctly notes that three-month suspensions are generally reserved for technical violations or negligence, not for the intentional conversion of client funds. The holding in *Kroll* reinforces the principle that while mitigation is relevant, it cannot reduce the sanction to a level that trivializes the seriousness of the breach of fiduciary duty.

**C. Analysis of Mitigation and Public Protection**

The Court has carefully reviewed the Defense’s presentation of Petitioner’s mitigating circumstances. The record establishes that Petitioner has made significant strides in her recovery from alcohol and drug addiction, has maintained sobriety for over two years, has made full restitution, and has no prior disciplinary record. These factors are undoubtedly compelling and distinguish this case from instances of calculated, repeated theft.

However, the purpose of attorney discipline is not solely rehabilitation; it is primarily the protection of the public. As stated in *In re Rappaport*, 27 Cal. 3d 813 (1980), the Court must ensure that the discipline imposed is sufficient to maintain public confidence in the legal profession. A three-month suspension for the wilful misappropriation of client funds risks conveying the message that such conduct is a mere technicality rather than a fundamental breach of trust. While Petitioner’s rehabilitation is extraordinary, it does not erase the fact that she intentionally converted client funds. The six-month suspension adopted in the preliminary ruling already represents a significant departure from the standard one-year minimum, giving substantial weight to the mitigation. A further reduction to three months would undermine the precedential floor established by *Rappaport* and *Kroll*.

**III. CONCLUSION**

The Defense’s arguments regarding the flexibility of the Standards and the Petitioner’s recovery are well-taken. However, they do not overcome the binding precedent that establishes six months as the minimum actual suspension for the wilful misappropriation of client funds. The Prosecution has correctly demonstrated that *In re Rappaport*, 27 Cal. 3d 813 (1980), and *In re Kroll*, 43 Cal. 3d 1253 (1987), prohibit a sanction below this threshold for offenses involving intentional conversion. The Court’s discretion under *In re Sutter*, 21 Cal. 3d 243 (1978), allows for leniency within the bounds of precedent, but not below the floor necessary to protect the public and the profession.

Accordingly, the Court adheres to its preliminary ruling. Petitioner Lisa Margaret Howard is suspended from the practice of law for a period of six months.
