### I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy for Substantive Due Process Claims.  

The Supreme Court has consistently held that Bivens claims are disfavored and should not be extended to new contexts absent clear congressional direction. *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), established a narrow exception for Fourth Amendment violations by federal agents, but the Court has repeatedly refused to extend Bivens beyond its original scope. As the Court emphasized in *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017), "the Judiciary should not create new Bivens remedies in the absence of clear congressional direction." The Court further explained that "special factors counseling hesitation" must be considered whenever a plaintiff seeks to extend Bivens to a new context.  

This case presents precisely such a new context. Doe alleges he was detained by federal officials, subjected to interrogation without counsel, held in solitary confinement, and exposed to harsh conditions. These allegations seek to impose personal liability on a high-ranking executive official for actions taken during detention—a context entirely unaddressed by *Bivens* or any subsequent precedent. *Ziglar* makes clear that courts must exercise restraint in such circumstances, as extending Bivens here would intrude on the separation of powers and encroach on executive discretion in matters of government operations. *See id.* at 1858 ("[T]he Judiciary should not create new Bivens remedies where there are special factors counseling hesitation."). The district court ignored this directive by recognizing a Bivens remedy without any congressional authorization or clear precedent supporting such an extension.  

Moreover, *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988), reinforces that courts must not imply Bivens remedies where Congress has not provided a statutory alternative. While the district court dismissed Doe’s claims under the Detainee Treatment Act, that dismissal does not justify judicial creation of a new remedy. As *Schweiker* holds, "the existence of a congressional remedial scheme" is a critical factor, but even in its absence, courts must "hesitate" to imply Bivens where "special factors" counsel restraint. Here, the nature of the claims—against a Secretary of Defense for detention-related conduct—raises profound institutional concerns that require deference to Congress, not judicial innovation. The district court’s failure to recognize these special factors constitutes reversible error.  

### II. The District Court Erred in Denying Qualified Immunity.  

Even if a Bivens remedy were somehow available—which it is not—Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. To overcome qualified immunity, Doe must demonstrate that the right he asserts was "clearly established" at the time of the alleged conduct. *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223, 231 (2009). This requires "controlling precedent" that would have put a reasonable official on notice that his conduct violated the Constitution. *Id.* at 236.  

The district court held that Doe’s substantive due process claims were supported by a "shock the conscience" standard that was clearly established. But this is incorrect. The "shock the conscience" standard is inherently fact-specific and context-dependent. No precedent exists that clearly established this standard in the circumstances alleged here—specifically, detention and interrogation practices involving solitary confinement and denial of counsel. *See Iqbal*, 556 U.S. at 679 ("[A] plaintiff must plead factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.").  

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that qualified immunity shields officials when "the contours of the right [are] not sufficiently clear." *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002). Here, there is no "controlling precedent" addressing whether the specific practices alleged—such as solitary confinement during interrogation—violated substantive due process in the context of federal detention. Indeed, the district court’s conclusion that the right was "clearly established" is unsupported by any case law. As this Court recognized in *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 705 (D.C. Cir. 2008), qualified immunity applies where "the right asserted was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct." The district court’s denial of qualified immunity was therefore error.

For these reasons, the district court erred in two critical respects: (1) by recognizing a Bivens remedy in a new context where special factors counsel hesitation; and (2) by denying qualified immunity based on an unsupported conclusion that the right asserted was clearly established. ## INTRODUCTION

Appellee John Doe brings this action against former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld, alleging substantive due process claims under Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), arising from his detention by military authorities in Iraq. The district court denied Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss, holding that Doe could maintain a Bivens action and that Rumsfeld was not entitled to qualified immunity. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse the district court's order and dismiss Doe's claims in their entirety.

---

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN RECOGNIZING A BIVENS REMEDY FOR SUBSTANTIVE DUE PROCESS CLAIMS

### A. Bivens Remedial Expansion Is Disfavored and Limited to Original Context

The Supreme Court has consistently held that Bivens provides a narrow, disfavored remedy that should not be extended to new contexts without clear congressional authorization. In Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), the Court recognized an implied cause of action for Fourth Amendment violations by federal agents. However, the Court has repeatedly emphasized that this remedy is an anomaly and should be extended sparingly. See Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14, 20 (1980) (describing Bivens as "an anomaly in the law" and noting the Court's "disinclination to create new Bivens remedies").

The most recent and controlling guidance on this issue is Ziglar v. Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017), where the Supreme Court explicitly reaffirmed that "the Judiciary should not create new Bivens remedies in the absence of clear congressional direction" and that "special factors counseling hesitation" must be considered before extending Bivens to new contexts. Id. at 1857. The Abbasi Court further explained that when a plaintiff seeks to impose personal liability on a high-ranking government official for actions taken in an official capacity, this presents precisely the type of new context requiring judicial restraint. Id. at 1858.

### B. This Case Presents a New Context Where Special Factors Counsel Hesitation

Doe's allegations seek to hold Secretary Rumsfeld personally liable for detention and interrogation practices authorized and implemented during military operations in Iraq. This represents a quintessential new context for Bivens purposes. The district court erred in failing to recognize this.

First, Doe seeks to hold a Cabinet-level official personally liable for policies implemented in his official capacity as Secretary of Defense. As the Supreme Court explained in FDIC v. Meyer, 510 U.S. 471, 484 (1994), "the purpose of Bivens is to deter the individual officer from violating the constitutional rights of the plaintiff," but this rationale is significantly attenuated when the conduct is directed by high-level executive officials acting within their discretionary authority.

Second, Doe's claims arise in the context of military operations and national security. The political question doctrine and separation of powers principles counsel extreme deference to the executive branch in matters of military detention during wartime. See Department of the Navy v. Egan, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988) ("The President, Congress, and the Courts have recognized that the President and his subordinates have unique constitutional roles in matters of national security and foreign affairs."). The district court failed to consider these special factors.

Third, the Detainee Treatment Act, 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., and the Military Commissions Act establish comprehensive statutory schemes governing detention, interrogation, and tribunal procedures. Where Congress has provided—or declined to provide—remedial mechanisms, courts must hesitate before implying additional Bivens remedies. See Schweiker v. Chilicky, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988) (holding that the existence of a congressional remedial scheme is a critical factor counseling against Bivens extension).

### C. The District Court Misapplied Bivens Precedent

The district court relied on the "shock the conscience" standard to recognize a Bivens remedy, but this analysis is fundamentally flawed. The "shock the conscience" standard derives from substantive due process jurisprudence and addresses the merits of a constitutional claim—it does not address whether a Bivens remedy should be recognized in the first instance.

The district court cited no precedent extending Bivens to claims against a Secretary of Defense for detention-related conduct. This is not surprising. The Supreme Court has declined to extend Bivens to contexts involving prison conditions, see Minneci v. Pollard, 565 U.S. 118 (2012), and has emphasized that such extensions require "clear congressional direction," not judicial innovation. Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1857.

The district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy where Doe has not demonstrated that Congress intended such a remedy and where special factors counsel hesitation.

---

## II. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

### A. The Qualified Immunity Standard

Qualified immunity shields government officials from civil liability unless their conduct violates "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). This standard protects "all but the plainly incompetent or those who knowingly violate the law." Malley v. Briggs, 475 U.S. 335, 341 (1986).

To overcome qualified immunity, a plaintiff must demonstrate that: (1) the facts alleged make out a violation of a constitutional right; and (2) that right was "clearly established" at the time of the alleged conduct. Pearson v. Callahan, 555 U.S. 223, 232 (2009). Courts may address these prongs in whatever order is most appropriate. Id. at 236.

### B. The Right Asserted Was Not Clearly Established

The district court held that Doe possessed a clearly established substantive due process right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience." This holding was erroneous.

The "shock the conscience" standard is inherently fact-specific and context-dependent. It does not provide the "controlling precedent" required to put a reasonable official on notice that specific conduct violates the Constitution. As the Supreme Court explained in Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002), "officials can still be on notice that their conduct violates established law even in novel factual circumstances." However, this requires some analogized precedent—the very absence of such precedent here underscores the error in the district court's ruling.

No controlling precedent clearly established that the specific practices alleged—solitary confinement during interrogation, denial of counsel, detention without formal charges—violated substantive due process in the context of military detention during active hostilities. The district court cited no case law establishing such a right in circumstances remotely similar to those presented here.

To the contrary, the Supreme Court has recognized that military authorities possess significant discretion in detention matters, particularly during wartime. See Ex parte Quirin, 317 U.S. 1 (1942); Hamdi v. Rumsfeld, 542 U.S. 507 (2004) (plurality opinion) (recognizing executive authority to detain enemy combatants, though requiring some due process protections). The contours of any substantive due process right in this context were decidedly unclear in 2005-2006, when Doe's alleged detention occurred.

### C. Secretary Rumsfeld Did Not Violate Clearly Established Law

Moreover, even accepting Doe's factual allegations as true, they do not establish a violation of clearly established law. The alleged conduct—detention following intelligence gathering, interrogation, solitary confinement, and transfer to other facilities—while unpleasant, does not rise to the level of a constitutional violation in the military detention context absent controlling precedent establishing such a right.

The district court's reliance on the "shock the conscience" standard without identifying any case law applying that standard to similar circumstances was error. As this Court recognized in Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 705 (D.C. Cir. 2008), qualified immunity applies where "the right asserted was not clearly established at the time of the alleged conduct." The district court failed to identify any such controlling precedent here.

---

## III. THE DISTRICT COURT'S HOLDING IMPROPERLY SUBJECTS EXECUTIVE OFFICIALS TO PERSONAL LIABILITY

The implications of the district court's ruling are significant. If affirmed, any citizen detained during military operations could bring Bivens claims against the Secretary of Defense personally, alleging that detention conditions shock the conscience. This would transform Bivens from a narrow remedy into a general federal tort statute, precisely what the Supreme Court has warned against.

The separation of powers requires that courts refrain from creating new Bivens remedies that would chill executive action in areas committed to executive discretion. See Abbasi, 137 S. Ct. at 1858 ("[C]reating a Bivens remedy would require courts to interfere with the operations of the executive branch."). The district court failed to consider these institutional concerns.

---

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's order denying Secretary Rumsfeld's motion to dismiss. The district court erred in recognizing a Bivens remedy in a new context where special factors counsel hesitation, and in denying qualified immunity where no clearly established constitutional right was violated. Doe's claims against Secretary Rumsfeld should be dismissed in their entirety.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**Before:** The Court
**Case:** John Doe v. Donald Rumsfeld, No. 1:08-cv-01610 (RJL)
**Nature of Case:** Interlocutory Appeal from the United States District Court for the District of Columbia

**I. Jurisdiction and Standard of Review**

We have jurisdiction over this interlocutory appeal pursuant to the collateral order doctrine and the principles set forth in *Hartman v. Moore*, 547 U.S. 250, 257 n. 5 (2006), which permits review of a denial of qualified immunity. We review the district court's legal conclusions de novo. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008). In reviewing a motion to dismiss, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009).

**II. Analysis**

**A. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The district court held that Appellee could maintain a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), for substantive due process violations arising from his detention in Iraq. We find this holding to be in error.

The Supreme Court has consistently cautioned that *Bivens* remedies are "an anomaly in the law" and should not be expanded to new contexts absent clear congressional direction. *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 20 (1980); *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). In *Ziglar*, the Court articulated a two-step analysis: first, we ask whether the case presents a new *Bivens* context; and second, if so, whether special factors counsel hesitation. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857.

This case presents a new context distinct from the facts of *Bivens*. *Bivens* involved federal law enforcement agents executing a search warrant. *Bivens*, 403 U.S. at 389. Here, Appellee alleges he was detained by military personnel during active hostilities in a foreign combat zone, and he seeks to hold the Secretary of Defense personally liable for policies and conduct related to national security and military detention. This context involves "military detention dur[ing] wartime," which implicates unique separation-of-powers concerns. *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988).

Furthermore, special factors counsel hesitation against implying a remedy here. The arguments presented correctly highlight that extending *Bivens* to this context would require courts to intrude upon the discretion of the Executive Branch in matters of military operations and national security. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. Additionally, while the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) does not provide a private cause of action, the existence of this comprehensive statutory scheme governing the treatment of detainees is a factor that weighs against the implication of a judicial remedy. *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988). The district court failed to give adequate weight to these special factors and erred in implying a *Bivens* action in this context.

**B. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Government officials are entitled to qualified immunity unless they violate "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). To overcome this defense, Appellee must point to controlling precedent that places the constitutionality of the specific conduct beyond debate. *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223, 231 (2009).

The district court found that Appellee had a clearly established right to be free from detention and interrogation practices that "shock the conscience." However, the "shock the conscience" standard is inherently fact-specific and does not, by itself, clearly establish the illegality of the specific conduct alleged here—namely, the prolonged detention, solitary confinement, and interrogation of a U.S. citizen contractor in a war zone. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 739 (2002).

The arguments presented demonstrate that there is no controlling precedent establishing that the specific conditions of confinement and interrogation methods alleged in this case violate substantive due process in the context of military detention abroad. While *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), recognized the due process rights of enemy combatants, it did not clearly establish a right to specific conditions of confinement or personal liability for the Secretary of Defense in the circumstances alleged. Without such clearly established law, a reasonable official in Secretary Rumsfeld's position would not have been on notice that his conduct was unlawful. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d at 705.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy in this new context and that Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The arguments presented by the Appellant are persuasive and unopposed by contrary authority in the record provided. Accordingly, we reverse the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss and remand with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.

**I. Jurisdiction and Standard of Review**

We have jurisdiction over this interlocutory appeal pursuant to the collateral order doctrine and the principles set forth in *Hartman v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009).

**II. Analysis**

**A. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The district court held that Appellee could maintain a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), for substantive due process violations arising from his detention in Iraq. We reaffirm our conclusion that this holding was in error.

The Supreme Court has articulated a two-step analysis for *Bivens* actions. First, we ask whether the case presents a new *Bivens* context—meaning it is "different in a meaningful way" from previous *Bivens* cases decided by the Court. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). Second, if so, we ask whether special factors counsel hesitation in implying a remedy. *Id.*

Appellee argues that this case does not present a new context because *Bivens* remedies extend to "constitutional violations arising from federal detention," citing *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 20 (1980). However, Appellee's reliance on *Carlson* is misplaced. *Carlson* involved the failure to provide medical care to a federal prisoner in the United States, a scenario far removed from the military detention of a United States citizen contractor in a foreign combat zone during active hostilities. As the Appellant correctly notes, *Ziglar* specifically distinguished *Carlson* and emphasized that extending *Bivens* to national security and military detention contexts involves "meaningful" differences implicating separation-of-powers concerns. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

The facts alleged here—detention by the United States Marine Corps and NCIS in Iraq, housing with suspected enemy combatants, and interrogation regarding national security matters—fall squarely within the "special context" of military operations. *Department of the Navy v. This context is distinct from the domestic law enforcement context of *Bivens* and the federal prison context of *Carlson*.

Furthermore, special factors counsel hesitation against implying a remedy here. The existence of the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., demonstrates that Congress has "expressly ... created a remedial scheme" or at least has occupied the field of detainee treatment, which is a factor weighing against the implication of a judicial remedy. *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988). While the DTA may not provide a private right of action, its existence indicates that Congress, rather than the Judiciary, is the appropriate body to address these grievances. Additionally, the separation-of-powers concerns implicated by suing the Secretary of Defense for policies regarding military detention in a war zone are profound. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. Therefore, the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* action in this new context.

**B. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Government officials are entitled to qualified immunity unless they violate "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). To overcome this defense, Appellee must point to controlling precedent that places the constitutionality of the specific conduct beyond debate. *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223, 231 (2009).

Appellee relies on *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730 (2002), and *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), to argue that the "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established and that the unlawfulness of the alleged conduct was "apparent." However, these cases involved domestic law enforcement or prison officials, not the Secretary of Defense overseeing military detention in a foreign war zone. *Hope* involved a prison guard's use of a hitching post, and *Lewis* involved a high-speed police chase. Neither case addressed the unique considerations of military detention or the liability of high-ranking executive officials for conditions of confinement in a combat theater.

Appellee also cites *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), and *Doe v. The absence of controlling precedent placing the Secretary of Defense's liability for these specific military detention policies "beyond debate" means that a reasonable official in Secretary Rumsfeld's position would not have been on notice that his conduct was unlawful. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d at 705.

The arguments presented by the Appellant are persuasive and supported by controlling precedent. **FINAL RULING**

**Before:** The Court
**Case:** John Doe v. Donald Rumsfeld, No. 1:08-cv-01610 (RJL)
**Nature of Case:** Interlocutory Appeal from the United States District Court for the District of Columbia

**I. Jurisdiction and Standard of Review**

We have jurisdiction over this interlocutory appeal pursuant to the collateral order doctrine and the principles set forth in *Hartman v. Moore*, 547 U.S. 250, 257 n. 5 (2006). We review the district court's legal conclusions de novo. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C. Cir. 2008). In reviewing a motion to dismiss, we accept the well-pleaded factual allegations as true and construe reasonable inferences in the plaintiff's favor. *Ashcroft v. Iqbal*, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79 (2009).

**II. Analysis**

**A. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The district court held that Appellee could maintain a cause of action under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), for substantive due process violations arising from his detention in Iraq. Having reviewed the arguments presented by the Appellant, we reaffirm our conclusion that this holding was in error.

The Supreme Court has articulated a two-step analysis for *Bivens* actions. First, we ask whether the case presents a new *Bivens* context—meaning it is "different in a meaningful way" from previous *Bivens* cases decided by the Court. *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). Second, if so, we ask whether special factors counsel hesitation in implying a remedy. *Id.*

Appellee argues that this case does not present a new context because *Bivens* remedies extend to "constitutional violations arising from federal detention," citing *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14, 20 (1980). However, as the Appellant correctly notes, *Carlson* involved the failure to provide medical care to a federal prisoner in the United States, a scenario far removed from the military detention of a United States citizen contractor in a foreign combat zone during active hostilities. *Ziglar* specifically distinguished *Carlson* and emphasized that extending *Bivens* to national security and military detention contexts involves "meaningful" differences implicating separation-of-powers concerns. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858.

The facts alleged here—detention by the United States Marine Corps and NCIS in Iraq, housing with suspected enemy combatants, and interrogation regarding national security matters—fall squarely within the "special context" of military operations. *Department of the Navy v. Egan*, 484 U.S. 518, 530 (1988) (recognizing the President's constitutional authority over military affairs). This context is distinct from the domestic law enforcement context of *Bivens* and the federal prison context of *Carlson*.

Furthermore, special factors counsel hesitation against implying a remedy here. The existence of the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., demonstrates that Congress has "expressly ... created a remedial scheme" or at least has occupied the field of detainee treatment, which is a factor weighing against the implication of a judicial remedy. *Schweiker v. Chilicky*, 487 U.S. 412, 423 (1988). While the DTA may not provide a private right of action, its existence indicates that Congress, rather than the Judiciary, is the appropriate body to address these grievances. Additionally, the separation-of-powers concerns implicated by suing the Secretary of Defense for policies regarding military detention in a war zone are profound. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1858. Therefore, the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* action in this new context.

**B. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a *Bivens* remedy were available, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Government officials are entitled to qualified immunity unless they violate "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). To overcome this defense, Appellee must point to controlling precedent that places the constitutionality of the specific conduct beyond debate. *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223, 231 (2009).

Appellee relies on *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730 (2002), and *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998), to argue that the "shock the conscience" standard was clearly established and that the unlawfulness of the alleged conduct was "apparent." However, as the Appellant argues, these cases involved domestic law enforcement or prison officials, not the Secretary of Defense overseeing military detention in a foreign war zone. *Hope* involved a prison guard's use of a hitching post, and *Lewis* involved a high-speed police chase. Neither case addressed the unique considerations of military detention or the liability of high-ranking executive officials for conditions of confinement in a combat theater.

Appellee also cites *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), and *Doe v. United States*, 789 F.2d 1383 (9th Cir. 1986). *Rochin* is a criminal case involving state police, and *Doe* involved a federal agent in the United States. Neither case clearly establishes that the Secretary of Defense is personally liable for the substantive due process violations of a contractor detained in Iraq under the circumstances alleged here. As the Appellant notes, *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), addressed the procedural due process rights of enemy combatants but did not establish clearly established law regarding the substantive conditions of confinement or personal liability for the Secretary of Defense in this context.

While the alleged conduct—physical abuse and harsh interrogation—is reprehensible, the question is whether the specific right violated was clearly established in 2005. The absence of controlling precedent placing the Secretary of Defense's liability for these specific military detention policies "beyond debate" means that a reasonable official in Secretary Rumsfeld's position would not have been on notice that his conduct was unlawful. *Wilson v. Libby*, 535 F.3d at 705.

**III. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we conclude that the district court erred in implying a *Bivens* remedy in this new context and that Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. The arguments presented by the Appellant are persuasive and supported by controlling precedent. Accordingly, we reverse the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss and remand with instructions to dismiss the claims against Secretary Rumsfeld.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
