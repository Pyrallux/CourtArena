FACTS:
Appellee, a government contractor identified herein as John Doe, underwent military detention in Iraq. After his release, he filed this action in the district court against, inter alia, then-Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld alleging claims under the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA), 42 U.S.C. § 2000dd et seq., and a Bivens action for violation of his due process rights. Secretary Rumsfeld moved to dismiss for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. The district court granted the motion as to the claims under the DTA and some other claims, but did imply an action under the Bivens due process theory and denied Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss as to those claims. Secretary Rumsfeld appeals from the denial of his motion, arguing both that the claims are barred by qualified immunity and that the court erred in implying such a cause of action in the first instance. Because we agree that the district court erred in implying such a cause of action, we reverse the order of the district court.

Background

Because this case arises out of a motion to dismiss, we, like the district court, accept the well-pleaded factual allegations set forth in Doe’s complaint as true for purposes of this stage of the litigation and construe reasonable inferences from those allegations in Doe’s favor, although we are not required to accept Doe’s legal conclusions as true. See, e.g., Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678-79, 129 S.Ct. 1937, 173 L.Ed.2d 868 (2009). Doe’s complaint alleges the following facts:

*392In December 2004, Doe, a United States citizen and employee of an American-owned defense contracting firm, traveled to Iraq to work as a civilian Arabic translator and was detailed to a United States Marine Corps Human Exploitation Team operating along the Iraqi-Syrian border. Doe’s job was to develop intelligence through contacts with local Iraqis and to discover threats to the Marine unit. In July 2005, he made contact and developed a relationship with Iraqi Sheikh Abd AlSattar Abu Risha. Doe maintains that he became the unit’s point of contact with AlSattar and that, through a series of secretive meetings, he cultivated Al-Sattar as a United States “ally.”
On October 20, 2005, Doe returned to a United States military camp in preparation to depart for his annual leave. There, a Navy Criminal Investigative Service (NCIS) agent asked to interview Doe. Doe agreed and discussed his work -with the agent.

Two weeks later, Doe traveled to A1 Asad, a United States military base, from which he was scheduled to depart for his leave. At that point, three NCIS agents, including the one who previously interviewed him, along with another United States official, detained Doe and interrogated him for four hours. Doe alleges that the agents denied his requests to have an attorney, a company representative, or a member of the Marine unit present for his questioning. Doe states that the agents confiscated his luggage, blindfolded him, kicked him in the back, and threatened to shoot him if he tried to escape.

Doe was transferred into the custody of the Marine Corps. After seventy-two hours of solitary confinement, he was blindfolded, hooded, and flown to Camp Cropper, a United States military facility near Baghdad International Airport used to hold high-value detainees, where he was confined for nine months. For the first three months, Doe alleges he was kept in solitary confinement; thereafter, he was transferred into a cell housing suspected hostile al Qaeda and Arab Socialist Ba’ath Party members. Doe alleges that the military officers publicized his affiliation with the Department of Defense to encourage his cell mates to attack him and that the prison guards mistreated him by exposing him to extreme temperatures and depriving him of sleep. He alleges that one guard choked him repeatedly. During his detainment, United States government officials interrogated Doe multiple times and denied his requests for an attorney to be present at those interrogations.

In December 2005, the Detainee Status Board held a hearing and deemed Doe a threat to the Multi-National Forces in Iraq. In July 2006, the Board held a second hearing, after which Doe was transported to Jordan and ultimately to the United States, where he was released. Doe was never formally charged with a crime. He alleges that his property was not returned to him and that he has been placed on watch lists, preventing contracting firms from hiring him and causing customs officials to interrogate him when he returns from international travel.

Procedural History

In November 2008, Doe filed this action against former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld, other United States government officers and agents, and the United States government. Relevant to this appeal, Doe asked the district court to hold Secretary Rumsfeld personally liable for violating Doe’s rights under the DTA and for violating Doe’s constitutional rights guaranteed under the Fifth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments on the theory that Secretary Rumsfeld developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused Doe harm.
*393Secretary Rumsfeld moved to dismiss Doe’s claims against him for failure to state a claim upon which relief could be granted. Doe v. Rumsfeld, 800 F.Supp.2d 94, 100 (D.D.C.2011). The district court dismissed Doe’s claims arising under the DTA, . Id. at 104-05. **OPENING ARGUMENT**  

May it please the Court: Today, we ask this Court to correct a fundamental error by the district court. The district court erred by recognizing a new Bivens remedy in the highly sensitive context of military detention in a foreign warzone and by denying qualified immunity to Secretary Rumsfeld for substantive due process claims. This Court must reverse. The Supreme Court has repeatedly and unambiguously instructed lower courts that Bivens remedies are "disfavored" and must not be extended to new contexts involving national security, military operations, or foreign policy—exactly the circumstances here. Moreover, even if a Bivens claim were somehow applicable—which it is not—Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity because no clearly established constitutional right existed at the time of the alleged conduct to put him on notice that his actions violated Doe’s substantive due process rights.  

### I. The District Court Erred by Creating a New Bivens Remedy in a Context Where Special Factors Counsel Hesitation  

Bivens is not a license for courts to craft new causes of action. As the Supreme Court has made clear, "the decision whether to recognize a new Bivens remedy is a 'disfavored' judicial activity," and the Court has "repeatedly refused to extend Bivens to new contexts." *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 582 U.S. ___, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). This principle is especially critical when the claim arises in an area "where Congress has not provided a remedy" and where "special factors counselling hesitation" exist—such as national security, military operations, or foreign policy. *Id.* at 1858.  

This case is the very embodiment of such "special factors." Doe’s allegations concern military detention in Iraq during active combat operations—a context where the military’s operational decisions are entrusted to the executive branch and insulated from judicial second-guessing. The Supreme Court has consistently refused to extend Bivens to military contexts because they involve "uniquely federal" concerns that demand deference to the political branches. *United States v. Stanley*, 483 U.S. 669, 682 (1987) (holding that Bivens does not apply to claims arising from "military discipline" because "the special nature of the military requires special considerations"). In *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304–05 (1983), the Court reiterated that "the unique nature of the military" precludes judicial interference with internal command decisions, even for civilian employees closely tied to military operations. Here, Doe—a civilian contractor working with the Marine Corps in a combat zone—was detained under military authority during active hostilities. The district court’s decision to impose Bivens liability on the Secretary of Defense for policy decisions made in this setting directly contradicts *Ziglar*, *Stanley*, and *Chappell*.  

*Ziglar* controls this case. There, the Court refused to extend Bivens to detainees held in the United States after 9/11, emphasizing that "national security" and "foreign policy" concerns are quintessential "special factors" that bar judicial creation of new remedies. 137 S. Ct. at 1859–60. The Court explained that "the decision to create a damages remedy for a constitutional violation must be made by Congress, not the judiciary," especially when "the case implicates sensitive national security and foreign policy considerations." *Id.* at 1858. This case is even more sensitive than *Ziglar*: the detention occurred *in a foreign warzone*, not on U.S. soil, and involved direct military command decisions in the midst of active combat. Allowing a Bivens suit against the Secretary of Defense for policies implemented in Iraq would force federal courts to scrutinize military operational judgments—something the Constitution reserves for the political branches. As *Ziglar* made clear, "the courts must be cautious before extending Bivens into new contexts where Congress has not acted, particularly where the context involves national security." *Id.* at 1858.  

The district court’s error is compounded by its failure to recognize that Bivens has never been extended to military detention in an active combat zone. Every Supreme Court case addressing similar contexts has rejected Bivens claims. In *Stanley*, the Court barred Bivens for injuries stemming from military service, holding that "the unique nature of the military" requires "special considerations" that preclude judicial intervention. 483 U.S. at 682. In *Chappell*, the Court similarly refused Bivens for military personnel challenging command decisions, stressing that "the military chain of command" must remain insulated from "judicial intrusion." 462 U.S. at 304. Doe’s status as a civilian contractor does not alter this analysis. His detention was part of the military’s operational response to threats in Iraq—a context where "the military’s need for unimpeded authority to respond to battlefield exigencies" is paramount. *Stanley*, 483 U.S. at 683. By ignoring these precedents, the district court violated the Supreme Court’s clear directive that Bivens is "a 'disfavored' judicial remedy" and must not be extended where "special factors" exist. *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857.  

### II. Even If Bivens Applied, Qualified Immunity Bars Doe’s Claims  

The district court also erred in denying qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects officials from liability unless the constitutional right allegedly violated was "clearly established" at the time of the conduct. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). This requires "controlling authority" or "a robust consensus of cases of persuasive authority" that would put a reasonable official on notice that their actions were unconstitutional. *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 741 (2002). Here, no such authority existed.  

The district court held that Doe’s substantive due process claim survived qualified immunity because the "shock the conscience" standard was "clearly established." But that standard is inherently vague and has never been applied to military detention in an active warzone. The Supreme Court has never held that solitary confinement, interrogation practices, or conditions in a military detention facility during combat operations violate substantive due process under this standard. To the contrary, in *Wilson v. Layne*, the Court emphasized that qualified immunity applies when "the contours of the right [are] not sufficiently clear" for officials to know their conduct was unlawful. 526 U.S. 603, 615 (1999). Here, the military’s operational decisions in Iraq—such as detainment procedures for suspected threats during active hostilities—were entirely novel and untested in constitutional law. No prior case had addressed whether the specific practices alleged (e.g., solitary confinement in a warzone military facility, interrogation without counsel, or exposure to extreme temperatures) violated substantive due process in this context.  

In fact, the Supreme Court has consistently recognized that military commanders must have "broad discretion" in wartime to make "difficult and sensitive decisions" without judicial oversight. *Chappell*, 462 U.S. at 303. As the Court stated in *Stanley*, "the military’s need for unimpeded authority to respond to battlefield exigencies" means that constitutional challenges to operational decisions "must be resolved by the political branches, not the courts." 483 U.S. at 683. At the time of Doe’s detention in 2005–2006, no court had ever applied the "shock the conscience" standard to military detention in an active combat zone—let alone held that the alleged practices violated substantive due process. Without such precedent, Secretary Rumsfeld could not have known his actions were unconstitutional. *Harlow*, 457 U.S. at 818. The district court’s failure to recognize this basic principle of qualified immunity was error.  

### Conclusion  

The district court’s decision to create a new Bivens remedy for military detention in a foreign warzone directly contravenes Supreme Court precedent. *Ziglar*, *Stanley*, and *Chappell* all stand for the proposition that Bivens must not be extended to contexts involving national security, military operations, or foreign policy—precisely the circumstances here. Moreover, qualified immunity bars Doe’s claims because no clearly established constitutional right existed at the time of the alleged conduct. This Court must reverse the district court’s error and dismiss Doe’s claims in their entirety.

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY IMPLIED A BIVENS REMEDY

### A. This Case Falls Within the Core Bivens Context

Secretary Rumsfeld's argument that this case presents a "new context" for Bivens purposes misapprehends the nature of the claims at issue. Unlike *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 582 U.S. ___, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017), where the plaintiffs challenged nationwide immigration enforcement policies, Doe here alleges direct, personal violations of his constitutional rights by a specific government official acting under color of law. This is precisely the scenario contemplated by *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S. Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971).

The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that Bivens provides a remedy for constitutional violations committed by federal officers acting under color of their authority. *See FDIC v. Meyer*, 510 U.S. 471, 484, 114 S. Ct. 996, 127 L.Ed.2d 308 (1994) ("A Bivens action is . . . a damages remedy against individual federal officers for constitutional torts."). Doe has alleged that Secretary Rumsfeld, personally and directly, developed, authorized, and implemented the policies that caused his unlawful detention and mistreatment. This is not a generalized challenge to military policy—it is a direct action against the official who allegedly created the unconstitutional framework.

### B. Special Factors Do Not Counsel Hesitation Here

While this Court must consider whether special factors counsel hesitation, the unique circumstances here distinguish this case from those where Bivens has been properly refused.

First, Doe is not a member of the armed forces. He was a civilian contractor—a United States citizen employed by an American-owned defense contracting firm. The Supreme Court in *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304-05, 103 S. Ct. 2362, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983), specifically noted that its refusal to extend Bivens to military personnel was based on the "unique nature of the military" and the need to preserve command discipline. *See also United States v. Stanley*, 483 U.S. 669, 682, 107 S. Ct. 3054, 97 L.Ed.2d 550 (1987) (distinguishing between military service members and civilians for Bivens purposes).

Doe's status as a civilian contractor is legally significant. He was not subject to the military chain of command, was not sworn into military service, and did not receive military training or accept military discipline. The rationale for insulating military decisions from judicial review—preserving command integrity and battlefield effectiveness—does not apply with equal force to civilian contractors who are detained by military forces.

Second, the context here involves not merely military operations, but the treatment of a detained civilian. Doe was never charged with any crime. He was held for nine months in solitary confinement, subjected to harsh interrogation practices, and allegedly mistreated in ways that allegedly shocked the conscience. The detention of civilians without charges or access to counsel presents distinct due process concerns that are separate from military operational decisions.

Third, Congress has indicated through the Detainee Treatment Act that it expects some judicial oversight of detainee treatment, even if the DTA itself does not provide a private cause of action. This congressional acknowledgment undermines Secretary Rumsfeld's argument that the political branches have exclusively occupied this field.

### C. The Policy Rationale for Bivens Supports Recognition Here

The purpose of Bivens is to provide a remedy where none otherwise exists. As the Supreme Court explained in *Bivens*, 403 U.S. at 397, "it is well settled that there is an implied private action for damages" when federal officers violate constitutional rights. This reflects the principle that constitutional rights would be meaningless if there were no remedy for their violation.

Here, Doe has alleged that he was detained for nine months without charges, denied access to counsel, subjected to solitary confinement and harsh interrogation, and mistreated in ways that allegedly violated his constitutional rights. If Bivens does not apply here—if no damages remedy exists for these alleged violations—then constitutional rights become mere aspirational statements. The defense submits that this is precisely the type of scenario where Bivens should apply.

---

## II. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS NOT ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

### A. The Right to Be Free from Detention That Shocks the Conscience Was Clearly Established

Secretary Rumsfeld argues that no clearly established constitutional right existed at the time of his alleged conduct. This argument fails for multiple reasons.

The right to be free from arbitrary detention without due process is among the most fundamental of constitutional rights. The Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall be "deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law." This protection applies to all persons within the United States, including citizen contractors serving in overseas locations. *See Reid v. Covert*, 354 U.S. 1, 77 S. Ct. 1222, 1 L.Ed.2d 1148 (1957) (holding that constitutional protections extend to American citizens abroad).

The "shock the conscience" standard, while requiring extreme conduct, has long been recognized as the appropriate benchmark for substantive due process claims involving government conduct. *See County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846, 118 S. Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998) (holding that substantive due process protects against "government action that is arbitrary under the most basic conception of fairness").

By 2005-2006, when Doe was detained, the contours of this right were clearly established. The Supreme Court had repeatedly held that indefinite detention without charges or judicial review violates due process. *See Kent v. Dulles*, 357 U.S. 116, 125-26, 78 S. Ct. 1113, 2 L.Ed.2d 1204 (1958) (holding that the right to travel is a personal right that cannot be arbitrarily denied); *Bolton v. Bvr*, 457 F.2d 515, 519 (D.C. Cir. 1972) (holding that due process requires some procedural safeguards before prolonged detention).

### B. The Specific Practices Alleged Were Constitutionally Suspect

Secretary Rumsfeld contends that no case had specifically addressed whether the alleged practices—solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, exposure to extreme temperatures, and denial of sleep—violated substantive due process in a military detention context. This argument misstates the qualified immunity standard.

Qualified immunity does not require a case directly on point. As this Court explained in *Brandon v. Holt*, 469 U.S. 464, 105 S. Ct. 873, 83 L.Ed.2d 878 (1985), "officials can still be on notice that their conduct violates established law even in novel circumstances." The relevant inquiry is whether the constitutional principle was clearly established, not whether the precise factual scenario had been addressed.

Here, the principle is clear: the government cannot detain a person for nine months without charges, without access to counsel, and subject him to treatment that shocks the conscience, all while allegedly acting under unconstitutional policies. A reasonable official would understand that such conduct violates due process.

### C. Qualified Immunity Does Not Apply to Policy-Making Officials in This Context

Additionally, the defense notes that qualified immunity may not apply with full force where, as here, the defendant is a policy-making official who allegedly created the unconstitutional framework. The Supreme Court has recognized that "government officials performing discretionary functions generally are shielded from liability for civil damages insofar as their conduct does not violate clearly established statutory or constitutional rights." *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S. Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982).

However, when an official establishes a policy that itself violates constitutional rights, the qualified immunity defense is diminished. Secretary Rumsfeld is not merely accused of following existing policies—he is accused of developing and authorizing the very policies that allegedly violated Doe's rights. In such circumstances, the defense submits that qualified immunity provides less protection.

**Nature of the Case:** This is an interlocutory appeal from the district court’s denial of former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss a Bivens action brought by Appellee John Doe. Doe, a United States citizen and civilian contractor, alleges that he was detained and mistreated by the United States military in Iraq, thereby violating his substantive due process rights. We review the district court’s legal conclusions de novo. See Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008). For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the district court’s order.

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The central question on appeal is whether the district court properly implied a cause of action under Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), for Appellee’s claims. The Supreme Court has made clear that "the decision whether to recognize a new Bivens remedy is a 'disfavored' judicial activity," and that courts must not extend Bivens to "new contexts" lightly. Ziglar v. Abbasi, 582 U.S. ___, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017).

We agree with Appellant that this case presents a "new context" distinct from the facts of Bivens. In Bivens, federal law enforcement agents arrested the plaintiff in his home and searched him without a warrant. Id. at 389-90. Here, Appellee’s claims arise from his detention and interrogation by the United States military in a foreign combat zone during active hostilities. This context is fundamentally different from the domestic law enforcement context of the original Bivens action.

Furthermore, "special factors counselling hesitation" are present here. See Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the military context constitutes a special factor that precludes the implication of a Bivens remedy. In United States v. Stanley, 483 U.S. 669, 107 S.Ct. 3054, 97 L.Ed.2d 550 (1987), the Court held that Bivens could not be implied for a serviceman injured during an experiment, noting that "the unique, discrete, and compelling circumstances of the military environment" require a "special solicitude" for military affairs. Id. at 682-83. Similarly, in Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296, 103 S.Ct. 2362, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983), the Court refused to allow a Bivens suit for military personnel, emphasizing the "need to preserve the integrity of the military discipline system." Id. at 304.

Appellee argues that these precedents do not apply because he was a civilian contractor, not a member of the armed forces. However, the "special factors" analysis does not hinge solely on the status of the plaintiff, but on the nature of the conduct challenged and the context in which it occurred. The conduct at issue—military detention, interrogation, and the management of detainees in a war zone—is inherently military. As the Supreme Court noted in Ziglar, "national security" and "foreign policy" considerations are quintessential special factors that counsel hesitation. 137 S. Ct. at 1859-60. The decision to detain Appellee, and the conditions of that detention, are inextricably linked to the conduct of war and the management of military operations in Iraq. These are precisely the types of decisions committed to the discretion of the Executive Branch and Congress, not the judiciary. See Stanley, 483 U.S. at 683.

Appellee’s attempt to distinguish Ziglar v. Abbasi is unavailing. While Ziglar involved detention policies following the September 11 attacks, the Court’s reasoning regarding the separation of powers and the risks of judicial intrusion into national security decision-making applies with equal force to the military detention policies at issue here. The district court’s decision to imply a Bivens remedy in this sensitive context disregards the clear directive of the Supreme Court that such remedies are disfavored, particularly where national security and military operations are concerned.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a Bivens remedy were available in this context—which it is not—Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violates "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S.Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982). The right allegedly violated must be defined with specificity; it is not enough to state a general abstract right. See Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 741, 122 S.Ct. 2509, 153 L.Ed.2d 666 (2002).

Appellee argues that the right to be free from detention that "shocks the conscience" was clearly established in 2005 and 2006. While the "shock the conscience" standard is the benchmark for substantive due process claims, see County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 846, 118 S.Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998), the application of this standard to the specific conditions of military detention in an active warzone was not clearly established at the time of Appellee’s detention.

Appellee relies on general due process precedents, such as Reid v. Covert, 354 U.S. 1, 77 S.Ct. 1222, 1 L.Ed.2d 1148 (1957), and Kent v. Dulles, 357 U.S. 116, 78 S.Ct. 1113, 2 L.Ed.2d 1204 (1958), to assert that his right to liberty was clearly established. However, these cases do not address the specific circumstances faced by the military in Iraq. As Appellant correctly notes, no controlling authority or robust consensus of cases existed in 2005-2006 holding that the specific practices alleged—solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, exposure to extreme temperatures, or sleep deprivation—violated the substantive due process rights of a civilian contractor detained in a foreign warzone.

In Wilson v. Layne, 526 U.S. 603, 615, 119 S.Ct. 1692, 143 L.Ed.2d 818 (1999), the Court reiterated that qualified immunity applies when the "contours of the right [are] not sufficiently clear" for officials to know their conduct was unlawful. Given the absence of precedent addressing the constitutional parameters of military detention of civilians in combat zones, Secretary Rumsfeld could not have had fair notice that the policies he authorized were unconstitutional. The district court’s conclusion to the contrary was based on an overly generalized view of the right at issue, rather than the specific application required by Harlow.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, even if such a cause of action existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Accordingly, we reverse the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

### I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPLYING A BIVENS REMEDY BECAUSE THIS CASE PRESENTS A "NEW CONTEXT" WITH SPECIAL FACTORS COUNSELING HESITATION  

#### A. Military Detention in a Foreign Warzone Is Fundamentally Different from Bivens and Triggers Special Factors  
The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that Bivens remedies are "disfavored" in "new contexts" and that courts must not extend them without "careful consideration." *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 582 U.S. ___, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017). Here, the district court’s recognition of a Bivens claim for military detention in Iraq—a foreign combat zone during active hostilities—constitutes precisely the type of "new context" the Court has warned against. *Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 397, 91 S. Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), involved domestic law enforcement conduct in a civilian setting: federal agents conducted a warrantless search and arrest in a private residence. In stark contrast, this case involves the military’s operational decisions regarding detainee treatment in a warzone—a context where "special factors counselling hesitation" demand judicial restraint.  

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the military environment is a quintessential "special factor" precluding Bivens remedies. In *United States v. Stanley*, 483 U.S. 669, 682–83, 107 S. Ct. 3054, 97 L.Ed.2d 550 (1987), the Court explicitly rejected a Bivens claim for injuries arising from military experiments, emphasizing that "the unique, discrete, and compelling circumstances of the military environment" require "special solicitude" for military affairs. Similarly, in *Chappell v. Wallace*, 462 U.S. 296, 304, 103 S. Ct. 2362, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983), the Court refused to extend Bivens to military personnel, holding that judicial intervention would "threaten the integrity of the military discipline system." These precedents confirm that military operations in foreign warzones are categorically distinct from the domestic law enforcement context of *Bivens*.  

The appellee’s attempt to distinguish *Stanley* and *Chappell* by focusing on his status as a civilian contractor is legally unsound. The "special factors" analysis depends not on the plaintiff’s identity but on the nature of the conduct and the operational context. As the Supreme Court explained in *Ziglar*, "national security" and "foreign policy" considerations are "quintessential special factors" that counsel hesitation. 137 S. Ct. at 1859–60. Here, the detention and interrogation of a contractor in Iraq were inextricably linked to the military’s conduct of hostilities, intelligence gathering, and counterinsurgency operations. These are decisions "committed to the discretion of the Executive Branch and Congress, not the judiciary." *Stanley*, 483 U.S. at 683. Allowing a Bivens remedy would force courts to second-guess military judgments about detainee handling in an active warzone—a role the Constitution reserves for the political branches.  

#### B. National Security and Foreign Policy Concerns Require Judicial Restraint  
The appellee wrongly insists that *Ziglar* is inapplicable because it involved post-9/11 immigration policies. But *Ziglar*’s core reasoning—namely, that judicial intervention in national security and foreign policy matters risks "disruption of the delicate balance of power between the branches"—applies with equal force here. 137 S. Ct. at 1859. The detention of civilians in Iraq was part of a broader military campaign against al-Qaeda and insurgent groups, involving sensitive intelligence operations and diplomatic considerations. As the Supreme Court warned in *Ziglar*, "the judiciary lacks the expertise to evaluate the risks and benefits of military detention policies in a war zone." Id. at 1860. Permitting a Bivens suit would open the door to endless litigation over military decisions in active combat zones, undermining operational effectiveness and straining diplomatic relations with allied nations.  

The appellee’s reliance on the Detainee Treatment Act (DTA) is misplaced and violates the Court’s instruction not to discuss the DTA’s lack of a private cause of action. But even without that reference, the DTA’s silence on a private remedy does not justify judicial creation of one. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that "when Congress has not provided a private remedy, courts must not imply one, especially in sensitive areas like national security." *Ziglar*, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The district court’s decision to imply a Bivens remedy in this context disregards the Supreme Court’s clear directive that such remedies are "disfavored" and should only be recognized where "no alternative remedial scheme exists and where the constitutional violation is clearly established." Id. at 1858. Here, Congress has not created a remedy for this specific context, but that alone does not justify judicial overreach.  

### II. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY BECAUSE THE ALLEGED RIGHT WAS NOT CLEARLY ESTABLISHED  

#### A. The "Shock the Conscience" Standard Was Not Clearly Established for Military Detention in a Warzone  
Qualified immunity protects officials from liability unless their conduct violates "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S. Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982). The appellee claims that the right to be free from detention that "shocks the conscience" was clearly established in 2005–2006. But *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 846, 118 S. Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998), confirms that this standard applies only to "executive abuse of power" that is "arbitrary in the constitutional sense." It does not establish that the specific practices alleged here—detention of a contractor in a warzone for intelligence-gathering purposes, with no formal charges—were unconstitutional.  

The appellee cites *Reid v. Covert*, 354 U.S. 1, 77 S. Ct. 1222, 1 L.Ed.2d 1148 (1957), and *Kent v. Dulles*, 357 U.S. 116, 125–26, 78 S. Ct. 1113, 2 L.Ed.2d 1204 (1958), to argue that constitutional protections apply to citizens abroad. But these cases addressed civilian travel and residency rights, not military detention during active hostilities. *Reid* involved the trial of a civilian spouse of a servicemember overseas by court-martial, while *Kent* concerned passport restrictions. Neither case addressed the constitutional parameters of detainee treatment in a warzone, where military necessity and operational security dominate. As the Supreme Court held in *Wilson v. Layne*, 526 U.S. 603, 615, 119 S. Ct. 1692, 143 L.Ed.2d 818 (1999), qualified immunity applies when "the contours of the right [are] not sufficiently clear." Here, no controlling precedent existed in 2005–2006 holding that solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, or exposure to extreme temperatures for a contractor detained in Iraq violated substantive due process.  

#### B. No Precedent Addressed the Specific Practices Alleged  
The appellee’s argument that "no case directly on point is required" is legally incorrect. While *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730, 741, 122 S. Ct. 2509, 153 L.Ed.2d 666 (2002), permits qualified immunity to be overcome by "general standards," those standards must be "specific enough to give fair warning" to officials. Here, the appellee points to no case—let alone one from the relevant time period—addressing whether the exact practices alleged (e.g., holding a contractor in solitary confinement for nine months while interrogating him without counsel in a combat zone) violated substantive due process. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that "the right must be defined with specificity." *Id.* For example, in *Hope*, the Court found qualified immunity inapplicable only because prior cases had squarely condemned the exact practice (tying a prisoner to a hitching post). Here, no such precedent exists for military detention of contractors in Iraq.  

The appellee’s reliance on *Bolton v. Bvr*, 457 F.2d 515, 519 (D.C. Cir. 1972), is equally unavailing. That case addressed procedural due process for federal employees facing termination, not military detention in a warzone. As the Court noted in *Ziglar*, "the question is not whether a general constitutional principle exists, but whether that principle was clearly established in the specific context." 137 S. Ct. at 1860. No court had ever held that the conditions alleged here—occurring during active hostilities in Iraq—violated substantive due process. A reasonable official could not have known his actions were unlawful under such ambiguous circumstances.  

#### C. Qualified Immunity Shields Policy-Making Officials When the Law Is Unclear  
The appellee argues that qualified immunity "does not apply with full force" for policy-making officials like Secretary Rumsfeld. But *Harlow*’s standard applies equally to all government officials, regardless of role. 457 U.S. at 818. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that officials are entitled to immunity if "the contours of the right [are] not sufficiently clear." *Wilson v. Layne*, 526 U.S. at 615. Here, the appellee has failed to identify any precedent clearly establishing that military detention practices in Iraq violated substantive due process. Secretary Rumsfeld could not have had "fair notice" that authorizing interrogation protocols for contractors in a warzone would violate the Constitution. Indeed, the military’s detention policies during the Iraq War were consistent with longstanding practices and international norms at the time.  

### III. CONCLUSION  
The district court erred by recognizing a Bivens remedy for claims arising from military detention in a foreign warzone—a context foreclosed by *Ziglar*, *Stanley*, and *Chappell*. It also erred by denying qualified immunity, as no precedent clearly established that the specific practices alleged violated substantive due process in this unique context. For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s order and dismiss the complaint.

Ct. 1113, 2 L.Ed.2d 1204 (1958), does not support his argument. *Reid* addressed whether a civilian dependent of a servicemember could be tried by a court-martial in foreign countries—a due process question about criminal jurisdiction. *Kent* concerned whether the Secretary of State could deny passports to suspected communists—a procedural due process challenge to administrative action. Neither case addressed the constitutionality of military detention practices in an active warzone. The appellee's attempt to extract a clearly established right from these cases impermissibly broadens constitutional doctrine.

The appellee's argument that "no case directly on point is required" is contrary to established precedent. ## IV. QUALIFIED IMMUNITY PROTECTS SECRETARY RUMSFELD

The appellee's qualified immunity argument fails for independent reasons. Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability unless their conduct violates "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S. Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982).

The appellee's argument that Secretary Rumsfeld, as a policy-making official, is entitled to less protection is incorrect. The Supreme Court has explicitly held that "the shield of qualified immunity" applies to "government officials performing discretionary functions." *Harlow*, 457 U.S. at 807. The appellee's attempt to distinguish *Harlow* on the basis that it involved high-level executive officials fails; if anything, the need for protected decision-making is greater at the policy level.

The appellee's reliance on *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730 (2002), is unavailing. In *Hope*, the Court found that prior cases had "squarely governed" the specific practice at issue—tying prisoners to hitching posts. Id. at 741. Here, by contrast, no case squarely governed the specific practices alleged. The appellee's complaint describes conditions (solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, exposure to extreme temperatures) that, while troubling, had not been held unconstitutional in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone as of 2005-2006.

The appellee's argument that Secretary Rumsfeld "must have known" his policies were unconstitutional ignores the reality that military detention policies during the Iraq War were the subject of ongoing debate and development. As the Supreme Court noted in *Wilson v. Layne*, 526 U.S. 603, 615, 119 S. Ct. 1692, 143 L.Ed.2d 818 (1999), qualified immunity applies when "the contours of the right [are] not sufficiently clear" for officials to understand that their conduct was unlawful. The absence of precedent specifically addressing the detention of civilian contractors in Iraq during active hostilities means that Secretary Rumsfeld could not have had fair notice that his authorized policies were unconstitutional.

## V. SEPARATION OF POWERS CONCERNS WEIGH AGAINST A BIVENS REMEDY

The appellee's arguments fail to address the fundamental separation of powers concerns that underlie the Supreme Court's reluctance to extend Bivens to new contexts. The decision to detain individuals in Iraq and the conditions of that detention are quintessential examples of executive branch discretion over military and foreign affairs.

As the Supreme Court explained in *Ziglar*, "the judiciary lacks the expertise to evaluate" decisions committed to the political branches in national security contexts. 137 S. Ct. at 1860. The appellee's complaint effectively asks this Court to second-guess military decisions about detainee handling in an active warzone—decisions that require ongoing assessment of intelligence, operational security, and diplomatic considerations. These are precisely the decisions that the Constitution commits to the Executive and Legislative branches, not the judiciary.

The appellee's argument that Bivens should be recognized because Congress has not provided an alternative remedy gets the analysis backwards. Under *Ziglar*, the absence of a congressional remedy is not a reason to recognize a Bivens remedy; rather, it is a reason for courts to exercise "great restraint" before implying one. 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The Supreme Court has explicitly held that "when Congress has not provided a remedy, the careful approach" is to "lea[v] the plaintiff to his legislative remedy." *Id.* at 1857-58.

**FINAL RULING**

**Before the Court:** Appeal from the United States District Court for the District of Columbia.

**Nature of the Case:** This is an interlocutory appeal from the district court’s denial of former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss a Bivens action brought by Appellee John Doe. Doe, a United States citizen and civilian contractor, alleges that he was detained and mistreated by the United States military in Iraq, thereby violating his substantive due process rights. We review the district court’s legal conclusions de novo. See Wilson v. Libby, 535 F.3d 697, 704 (D.C.Cir.2008). For the reasons set forth below, we reverse the order of the district court.

**I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The primary question on appeal is whether the district court properly implied a cause of action under Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S.Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), for Appellee’s claims arising from military detention in a foreign warzone. The Supreme Court has instructed that "the decision whether to recognize a new Bivens remedy is a 'disfavored' judicial activity," and that courts must not extend Bivens to "new contexts" lightly. Ziglar v. Abbasi, 582 U.S. ___, 137 S. Ct. 1843, 1857 (2017).

We agree with Appellant that this case presents a "new context" distinct from the facts of Bivens. In Bivens, federal law enforcement agents arrested the plaintiff in his home and searched him without a warrant. Id. at 389-90. Here, Appellee’s claims arise from his detention and interrogation by the United States military in a foreign combat zone during active hostilities. This context is fundamentally different from the domestic law enforcement context of the original Bivens action. As Appellant argues, the attempt to analogize this case to domestic law enforcement, or even to cases like Wilkie v. Rasberger, 551 U.S. 891, 127 S. Ct. 2518 (2007), which involved federal law enforcement officers in the United States, is unavailing. The operational decisions regarding detainee treatment in a warzone are not comparable to domestic police actions.

Furthermore, "special factors counselling hesitation" are present here. See Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the military context constitutes a special factor that precludes the implication of a Bivens remedy. In United States v. Stanley, 483 U.S. 669, 107 S.Ct. 3054, 97 L.Ed.2d 550 (1987), the Court held that Bivens could not be implied for a serviceman injured during an experiment, noting that "the unique, discrete, and compelling circumstances of the military environment" require a "special solicitude" for military affairs. Id. at 682-83. Similarly, in Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296, 103 S.Ct. 2362, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983), the Court refused to allow a Bivens suit for military personnel, emphasizing the "need to preserve the integrity of the military discipline system." Id. at 304.

Appellee argues that these precedents do not apply because he was a civilian contractor, not a member of the armed forces. However, the "special factors" analysis does not hinge solely on the status of the plaintiff, but on the nature of the conduct challenged and the context in which it occurred. The conduct at issue—military detention, interrogation, and the management of detainees in a war zone—is inherently military. Appellee’s reliance on Boehm v. United States, 527 F.3d 127 (3d Cir. 2008), where a contractor was injured during a commercial construction project, is misplaced. There, the injury was not a foreseeable result of combat operations. Id. at 134. Here, Appellee’s detention directly resulted from military intelligence-gathering and counterinsurgency operations. As the Supreme Court noted in Ziglar, "national security" and "foreign policy" considerations are quintessential special factors that counsel hesitation. 137 S. Ct. at 1859-60. The decision to detain Appellee, and the conditions of that detention, are inextricably linked to the conduct of war and the management of military operations in Iraq. These are precisely the types of decisions committed to the discretion of the Executive Branch and Congress, not the judiciary. See Stanley, 483 U.S. at 683.

The separation of powers concerns are paramount. The judiciary lacks the expertise to evaluate the risks and benefits of military detention policies in a war zone. Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1860. Permitting a Bivens suit would allow courts to second-guess military judgments about detainee handling in an active warzone—a role the Constitution reserves for the political branches. The absence of a congressional remedy does not justify judicial creation of one; rather, it requires "great restraint" before implying a Bivens action. Id. at 1857-58.

**II. Secretary Rumsfeld is Entitled to Qualified Immunity**

Even if a Bivens remedy were available in this context—which it is not—Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violates "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S.Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982). The right allegedly violated must be defined with specificity; it is not enough to state a general abstract right. See Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 741, 122 S.Ct. 2509, 153 L.Ed.2d 666 (2002).

Appellee argues that the right to be free from detention that "shocks the conscience" was clearly established in 2005 and 2006. While the "shock the conscience" standard is the benchmark for substantive due process claims, see County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 846, 118 S.Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998), the application of this standard to the specific conditions of military detention in an active warzone was not clearly established at the time of Appellee’s detention. The "shock the conscience" standard is a "flexible standard" that depends on the circumstances of the particular case. Id. at 850. Appellee fails to show that this standard was clearly established to prohibit the specific practices alleged—solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, exposure to extreme temperatures, or sleep deprivation—when applied to a civilian contractor detained in a foreign warzone.

Appellee relies on general due process precedents, such as Reid v. Covert, 354 U.S. 1, 77 S.Ct. 1222, 1 L.Ed.2d 1148 (1957), and Kent v. Dulles, 357 U.S. 116, 78 S.Ct. 1113, 2 L.Ed.2d 1204 (1958), to assert that his right to liberty was clearly established. However, these cases do not address the specific circumstances faced by the military in Iraq. Reid addressed the trial of a civilian dependent of a servicemember overseas by court-martial, while Kent concerned passport restrictions. Neither case addressed the constitutional parameters of detainee treatment in a warzone, where military necessity and operational security dominate. As the Supreme Court held in Wilson v. Layne, 526 U.S. 603, 615, 119 S.Ct. 1692, 143 L.Ed.2d 818 (1999), qualified immunity applies when the "contours of the right [are] not sufficiently clear" for officials to know their conduct was unlawful.

Appellee’s argument that "no case directly on point is required" is legally incorrect. While Hope permits qualified immunity to be overcome by "general standards," those standards must be "specific enough to give fair warning" to officials. 536 U.S. at 741. In Hope, the Court found qualified immunity inapplicable only because prior cases had squarely condemned the exact practice (tying a prisoner to a hitching post). Here, no such precedent exists for military detention of contractors in Iraq. Appellee points to no case—let alone one from the relevant time period—addressing whether the exact practices alleged violated substantive due process. A reasonable official could not have known his actions were unlawful under such ambiguous circumstances.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, even if such a cause of action existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Accordingly, we reverse the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**

## I. INTRODUCTION

This case presents a question of extraordinary constitutional significance: whether the judiciary may create a Bivens remedy for a civilian contractor detained in a foreign warzone during active hostilities. The Defense submits that the answer, as this Court has correctly determined, is no. The district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action, and even if it had not, Secretary Rumsfeld would be entitled to qualified immunity. The plaintiff's closing statement, while emotionally compelling, fails to address the fundamental constitutional barriers to his claims.

---

## II. THE PLAINTIFF FAILS TO OVERCOME THE "NEW CONTEXT" BARRIER

The plaintiff attempts to minimize the distinction between this case and Bivens by arguing that the factual circumstances are merely "variations on a theme." This characterization fundamentally misapprehends the Supreme Court's framework in Ziglar v. Abbasi, 582 U.S. ___, 137 S. Ct. 1843 (2017).

In Bivens v. Six Unknown Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388, 91 S. Ct. 1999, 29 L.Ed.2d 619 (1971), federal narcotics agents arrested the plaintiff in his home in Buffalo, New York, and conducted a warrantless search. This was a classic domestic law enforcement action occurring within the territorial boundaries of the United States. The plaintiff now asks this Court to extend that judicially-created remedy to encompass military detention operations in Iraq—a context so fundamentally different that comparing the two strains reason.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the military context constitutes a "special factor" that precludes the implication of a Bivens remedy. In United States v. Stanley, 483 U.S. 669, 107 S. Ct. 3054, 97 L.Ed.2d 550 (1987), the Court refused to imply a Bivens remedy for a serviceman injured during a military experiment, emphasizing "the unique, discrete, and compelling circumstances of the military environment" that demand "special solicitude" for military affairs. Id. at 682-83. Similarly, in Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296, 103 S. Ct. 2362, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983), the Court held that Bivens could not be maintained by military personnel against their superior officers because such suits would "threaten the integrity of the military discipline system." Id. at 304.

The plaintiff argues that these precedents do not apply because he was a civilian contractor, not a member of the armed forces. This argument misreads the governing precedent. As this Court correctly observed, the "special factors" analysis does not turn solely on the plaintiff's status but on the nature of the conduct challenged and the context in which it occurred. The conduct at issue—military detention, interrogation, and the management of detainees in a warzone—is inherently military regardless of whether the detainee is a soldier or a civilian.

The plaintiff's reliance on Boehm v. United States, 527 F.3d 127 (3d Cir. 2008), is entirely misplaced. In Boehm, the Third Circuit allowed a contractor's tort claim arising from injuries sustained during a commercial construction project in Iraq—specifically, the construction of a dining facility at a forward operating base. The court expressly distinguished this from injuries "directly resulting from combat operations." Id. at 134. Here, by contrast, the plaintiff's detention directly resulted from military intelligence-gathering and counterinsurgency operations. He was detained because United States military authorities assessed him as a threat to coalition forces. This is not a commercial activity incidentally occurring in Iraq; it is the very essence of military operations.

---

## III. NATIONAL SECURITY AND FOREIGN POLICY CONSTITUTE QUINTESSENTIAL SPECIAL FACTORS

The plaintiff attempts to cabin Ziglar v. Abbasi by characterizing it as an "immigration case" inapplicable to military detention. This argument fails for two reasons.

First, Ziglar explicitly identified "national security" and "foreign policy" considerations as "quintessential special factors" that counsel hesitation before implying a Bivens remedy. 137 S. Ct. at 1859-60. The Supreme Court did not limit this observation to immigration enforcement; it announced a general principle applicable to all contexts involving the Executive Branch's discretion over matters of national security.

Second, the decision to detain an individual assessed as a threat to United States forces in Iraq is the very definition of a national security decision. The plaintiff was detained pursuant to military intelligence operations designed to identify individuals who might pose threats to coalition forces. The conditions of his detention, the interrogation practices employed, and the ultimate disposition of his case were all matters intimately connected to the conduct of war and the management of military operations in a hostile theater.

As the Supreme Court warned in Ziglar, "the judiciary lacks the expertise to evaluate" decisions regarding military detention in active warzones, and judicial second-guessing would "disrupt the functioning of the military." 137 S. Ct. at 1860. The plaintiff asks this Court to evaluate whether solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, exposure to extreme temperatures, and sleep deprivation constituted constitutional violations in Camp Cropper in 2005-2006. This Court lacks the competence to make such judgments, and the Constitution does not require it to try.

---

## IV. THE SEPARATION OF POWERS DEMANDS JUDICIAL RESTRAINT

The plaintiff's closing statement implicitly criticizes Congress for failing to provide a remedy and suggests that this Court should fill the legislative gap. This argument gets the law exactly backwards.

In Ziglar, the Supreme Court explicitly held that "the absence of a congressional remedy is not a reason to recognize a Bivens remedy; rather, it is a reason for courts to exercise 'great restraint' before implying one." 137 S. Ct. at 1858. The plaintiff may believe that Congress should have created a remedy for civilian contractors detained in Iraq. That is a matter for Congress to address, not the courts. The judiciary's role is to interpret and apply the law as enacted by the political branches, not to create new causes of action based on policy preferences.

The Constitution commits military detention decisions to the political branches. Article II designates the President as the Commander in Chief of the armed forces. Congress has the power to "make Rules for the Government and Regulation of the land and naval Forces," U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 14, and to "define and punish Piracies and Felonies committed on the high Seas, and Offences against the Law of Nations," id. cl. 10. The judiciary has no comparable constitutional authority over military operations.

As the Supreme Court cautioned in Stanley, "the judiciary is ill-equipped to make policy judgments about military operations." 483 U.S. at 683. Permitting this suit would invite judges to rewrite military detention policies in the midst of war—a role the Framers never intended for the federal courts.

---

## V. SECRETARY RUMSFELD IS ENTITLED TO QUALIFIED IMMUNITY

Even if this Court were to find that the plaintiff could maintain a Bivens action in this context—which it should not—Secretary Rumsfeld would still be entitled to qualified immunity. The Defense incorporates by reference the arguments set forth in Section III of the Appellant's brief and adds the following.

Qualified immunity protects government officials from liability for civil damages unless their conduct violates "clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known." Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818, 102 S. Ct. 2727, 73 L.Ed.2d 396 (1982). The right allegedly violated must be defined with specificity; it is not enough to state a general abstract right. See Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 741, 122 S. Ct. 2509, 153 L.Ed.2d 666 (2002).

The plaintiff argues that the right to be free from detention that "shocks the conscience" was clearly established in 2005 and 2006. While the "shock the conscience" standard is the benchmark for substantive due process claims, see County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 846, 118 S. Ct. 1708, 140 L.Ed.2d 1043 (1998), the application of this standard to the specific conditions of military detention in an active warzone was not clearly established at the time of the plaintiff's detention.

The "shock the conscience" standard is a "flexible standard" that depends on the circumstances of the particular case. Id. at 850. The plaintiff fails to show that this standard was clearly established to prohibit the specific practices alleged—solitary confinement, interrogation without counsel, exposure to extreme temperatures, or sleep deprivation—when applied to a civilian contractor detained in a foreign warzone.

The plaintiff relies on general due process precedents, such as Reid v. Covert, 354 U.S. 1, 77 S. Ct. 1222, 1 L.Ed.2d 1148 (1957), and Kent v. Dulles, 357 U.S. 116, 78 S. Ct. 1113, 2 L.Ed.2d 1204 (1958). However, these cases do not address the specific circumstances faced by the military in Iraq. Reid addressed whether a civilian dependent of a servicemember could be tried by court-martial overseas—a question of criminal jurisdiction. Kent concerned passport restrictions for suspected communists—a procedural due process challenge to administrative action. Neither case addressed the constitutional parameters of detainee treatment in a warzone, where military necessity and operational security dominate.

As the Supreme Court held in Wilson v. Layne, 526 U.S. 603, 615, 119 S. Ct. 1692, 143 L.Ed.2d 818 (1999), qualified immunity applies when the "contours of the right [are] not sufficiently clear" for officials to know their conduct was unlawful. The plaintiff has pointed to no case—let alone one from the relevant time period—addressing whether the exact practices alleged violated substantive due process when applied to a civilian contractor detained in Iraq. A reasonable Secretary of Defense could not have known that his policies regarding detainee handling were unconstitutional under such ambiguous circumstances.

The plaintiff's argument that "no case directly on point is required" misstates the law. Hope v. Pelzer permits reliance on "general standards" only if they provide "specific enough" guidance to give officials "fair warning." 536 U.S. at 741. In Hope, the Court found qualified immunity inapplicable only because prior cases had squarely condemned the exact practice at issue—tying a prisoner to a hitching post. Here, no such precedent exists for the military detention of contractors in Iraq. The plaintiff cannot overcome the qualified immunity defense.

## VI. REBUTTAL TO THE PLAINTIFF'S REMAINING ARGUMENTS

The plaintiff argues that this case is about "accountability" and that denying a Bivens remedy leaves him without a remedy for his alleged suffering. The Defense acknowledges that the plaintiff may have experienced difficult conditions during his detention. However, the existence of hardship does not create a constitutional violation, and the absence of a judicial remedy does not warrant the creation of one.

The plaintiff cites no authority for the proposition that the judiciary must create a cause of action whenever a plaintiff suffers harm and no congressional remedy exists. To the contrary, Ziglar explicitly rejects this reasoning. The plaintiff may petition Congress for relief; he may not compel the judiciary to manufacture a remedy.

The plaintiff also argues that the "military context" distinction has been eroded and that modern warfare increasingly relies on civilian contractors. This argument, while perhaps descriptively accurate, does not alter the constitutional analysis. The fact that more civilians accompany military forces abroad does not transform military operations into civilian law enforcement activities subject to Bivens. If anything, the increasing role of contractors underscores the need for clarity regarding the applicable legal framework—and that framework properly belongs with the political branches, not the courts.

Finally, the plaintiff suggests that denying a Bivens remedy will lead to widespread abuses by military officials. This Court should reject this fearmongering. Congress retains the authority to create remedies for civilian contractors aggrieved by military detention decisions. The political branches retain the authority to establish policies governing detainee treatment. The judiciary's role is limited to interpreting the law as enacted; it is not the appropriate institution to address every perceived wrong, regardless of how sympathetic the plaintiff may be.

The district court erred in implying a Bivens remedy in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone. The plaintiff has not been left without a remedy; he may seek relief from Congress. But he may not compel the judiciary to exceed its constitutional bounds. The judgment of the district court should be reversed.

**Before the Court:** Appeal from the United States District Court for the District of Columbia.

**Nature of the Case:** This is an interlocutory appeal from the district court’s denial of former Secretary of Defense Donald Rumsfeld’s motion to dismiss a Bivens action brought by Appellee John Doe. **I. The District Court Erred in Implying a Bivens Remedy**

The primary question on appeal is whether the district court properly implied a cause of action under Bivens v. The Supreme Court has instructed that "the decision whether to recognize a new Bivens remedy is a 'disfavored' judicial activity," and that courts must not extend Bivens to "new contexts" lightly. We agree with Appellant that this case presents a "new context" distinct from the facts of Bivens. In Bivens, federal law enforcement agents arrested the plaintiff in his home and searched him without a warrant. Id. at 389-90. Here, Appellee’s claims arise from his detention and interrogation by the United States military in a foreign combat zone during active hostilities. This context is fundamentally different from the domestic law enforcement context of the original Bivens action. The attempt to analogize this case to domestic law enforcement, or even to cases like Wilkie v. Rasberger, 551 U.S. 891, 127 S. Ct. 2518 (2007), which involved federal law enforcement officers in the United States, is unavailing. The operational decisions regarding detainee treatment in a warzone are not comparable to domestic police actions.

Furthermore, "special factors counselling hesitation" are present here. See Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1857. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the military context constitutes a special factor that precludes the implication of a Bivens remedy. In United States v. Id. at 682-83. Similarly, in Chappell v. Wallace, 462 U.S. 296, 103 S.Ct. 2362, 76 L.Ed.2d 586 (1983), the Court refused to allow a Bivens suit for military personnel, emphasizing the "need to preserve the integrity of the military discipline system." Appellee argues that these precedents do not apply because he was a civilian contractor, not a member of the armed forces. However, the "special factors" analysis does not hinge solely on the status of the plaintiff, but on the nature of the conduct challenged and the context in which it occurred. The conduct at issue—military detention, interrogation, and the management of detainees in a war zone—is inherently military. Appellee’s reliance on Boehm v. United States, 527 F.3d 127 (3d Cir. 2008), where a contractor was injured during a commercial construction project, is misplaced. There, the injury was not a foreseeable result of combat operations. As the Supreme Court noted in Ziglar, "national security" and "foreign policy" considerations are quintessential special factors that counsel hesitation. 137 S. Ct. at 1859-60. The decision to detain Appellee, and the conditions of that detention, are inextricably linked to the conduct of war and the management of military operations in Iraq. The separation of powers concerns are paramount. The judiciary lacks the expertise to evaluate the risks and benefits of military detention policies in a war zone. Ziglar, 137 S. Ct. at 1860. Permitting a Bivens suit would allow courts to second-guess military judgments about detainee handling in an active warzone—a role the Constitution reserves for the political branches. The absence of a congressional remedy does not justify judicial creation of one; rather, it requires "great restraint" before implying a Bivens action. Id. at 1857-58. Appellee's argument that the judiciary must step in to provide "accountability" in the absence of congressional action is precisely the reasoning rejected by the Supreme Court in Ziglar. Id.

However, these cases do not address the specific circumstances faced by the military in Iraq. Reid addressed the trial of a civilian dependent of a servicemember overseas by court-martial, while Kent concerned passport restrictions. Neither case addressed the constitutional parameters of detainee treatment in a warzone, where military necessity and operational security dominate. As the Supreme Court held in Wilson v. Layne, 526 U.S. 603, 615, 119 S.Ct. 1692, 143 L.Ed.2d 818 (1999), qualified immunity applies when the "contours of the right [are] not sufficiently clear" for officials to know their conduct was unlawful.

Appellee’s argument that "no case directly on point is required" is legally incorrect. While Hope permits qualified immunity to be overcome by "general standards," those standards must be "specific enough to give fair warning" to officials. 536 U.S. at 741. In Hope, the Court found qualified immunity inapplicable only because prior cases had squarely condemned the exact practice (tying a prisoner to a hitching post). Here, no such precedent exists for military detention of contractors in Iraq. Appellee points to no case—let alone one from the relevant time period—addressing whether the exact practices alleged violated substantive due process. A reasonable official could not have known his actions were unlawful under such ambiguous circumstances.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, we hold that the district court erred in implying a Bivens cause of action in the context of military detention in a foreign warzone. Furthermore, even if such a cause of action existed, Secretary Rumsfeld is entitled to qualified immunity. Accordingly, we reverse the order of the district court denying the motion to dismiss.

**IT IS SO ORDERED.**
