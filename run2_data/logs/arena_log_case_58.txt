# DEFENSE OPENING RESPONSE

## In the Court of Appeals of Wisconsin

---

**HONORABLE COURT:**

Defendant-Respondent Labor and Industry Review Commission ("the Commission") respectfully submits this opening response in defense of its decision denying worker's compensation benefits to Claimant-Appellant McRae. The Commission determined that McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment at the time of his accident because he was engaged in a normal commute from his residence to a job site, using his personal vehicle without reimbursement, and without any business-related purpose. The Commission correctly applied established Wisconsin law regarding the "traveling employee" doctrine, and its decision should be affirmed.

---

## I. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF THE CASE

The question presented is straightforward: whether a routine commute from a worker's personal residence to a job site—without reimbursement, without a special errand, and without any business-related purpose—constitutes a compensable injury under Wisconsin's worker's compensation statute. Under well-established precedent, it does not.

McRae was a union painter who drove his personal vehicle from his home in Twin Lakes to a job site in Milwaukee on January 7, 2005. He received no reimbursement for this commute because it fell within a five-county area specified in his union contract. His paid time began upon arrival at the job site. These undisputed facts demonstrate that McRae was engaged in a ordinary commute to work—not a special mission or traveling employee situation.

The Commission correctly interpreted Wis. Stat. § 102.03 and applied the established "coming and going" rule. This Court should affirm.

---

## II. STANDARD OF REVIEW

The plaintiff urges this Court to apply "great weight deference" to the Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03. While the Commission is entitled to deference on questions of statutory interpretation within its area of expertise, that deference is not unlimited. As the Wisconsin Supreme Court held in **Maldonado v. Labor & Industry Review Commission**, 2011 WI 10, 331 Wis. 2d 227, 795 N.W.2d 70, even under deferential standards, the Court must still ensure that the agency's interpretation is consistent with the statute's text and purpose. Here, the Commission's interpretation is not only reasonable—it is the only interpretation supported by the statute and precedent.

To the extent the plaintiff suggests that great weight deference requires this Court to accept any interpretation the Commission offers, that characterization is incorrect. As the Court emphasized in **Schaefer v. Labor & Industry Review Commission**, 2005 WI 104, 283 Wis. 2d 302, 699 N.W.2d 853, deference is appropriate only when the agency's interpretation is "reasonable" and supported by the statutory language. The Commission's interpretation here meets that standard, and the plaintiff's attempt to characterize the Commission's reasoning as "fundamentally misapplying" the traveling employee doctrine is without merit.

---

## III. ARGUMENT

### A. The Commission's Application of the "Coming and Going" Rule Was Correct

Wisconsin law long-established that injuries sustained during a worker's commute from home to work are generally not compensable. This is the "coming and going" rule. As the Wisconsin Supreme Court explained in **Koll v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.**, 190 Wis. 2d 180, 526 N.W.2d 120 (Ct. App. 1994), the rationale for this rule is that "an employee is not acting within the course of employment when traveling to and from work."

The plaintiff's reliance on the "traveling employee" doctrine under Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1)(f) is misplaced. That provision extends worker's compensation coverage to employees whose work requires them to travel, but it does not transform ordinary commutes into compensable journeys. The statute contemplates situations where the employee's *work itself* involves travel—think of a traveling salesperson, a delivery driver, or an employee whose job requires moving between multiple work sites in a single day.

The facts here are distinguishable from those scenarios. McRae was assigned to a *particular* job site for each day. He drove his personal vehicle. He received no reimbursement. His paid time began only upon arrival at the job site. These facts demonstrate that McRae was not "traveling" in the sense contemplated by § 102.03(1)(f); he was simply commuting.

The Commission's finding that "there was no business-related purpose to the applicant's commute, he was simply going to work" is supported by the record and consistent with the law. As this Court held in **Peters v. Labor & Industry Review Commission**, 2007 WI App 218, 305 Wis. 2d 757, 733 N.W.2d 724, an injury is not compensable when the employee is merely traveling to the initial place of employment, even if using a personal vehicle.

### B. The "Special Mission" Exception Does Not Apply

The plaintiff appears to argue that McRae's commute should be treated as a "special mission" or "special errand" that would bring the injury within the traveling employee doctrine. This argument fails because the record contains no evidence that McRae was performing any special errand at the time of the accident.

The Commission expressly found that "the applicant was not performing any special errand when the accident occurred on January 7, 2005." This finding is supported by the evidence: McRae was driving his normal route from his home to his assigned job site. He was not carrying tools or materials for work (beyond his personal painting tools, which were for his own use). He was not making a delivery, picking up supplies, or performing any other task for his employer. He was simply going to work.

Wisconsin courts have consistently held that the "special mission" exception requires something more than a routine commute. In **Keller v. Labor & Industry Review Commission**, 2006 WI App 257, 298 Wis. 2d 110, 726 N.W.2d 156, this Court explained that to qualify as a special mission, the employee's travel must be "at the direction and for the benefit of the employer" and must involve "a specific task or duty" assigned by the employer. The mere fact that an employee must travel to reach a job site does not convert the commute into a special mission.

Here, there is no evidence that Porta Painting directed McRae to travel in any particular manner, that it provided any benefit beyond the normal work of painting, or that McRae was performing any specific task for his employer at the time of the accident. The Commission's denial of benefits under these circumstances was correct.

### C. The Union Contract Does Not Change the Analysis

The plaintiff may argue that the five-county area specified in McRae's union contract is somehow relevant to the analysis. It is not. The fact that the union contract provided for reimbursement of travel expenses *outside* a five-county area simply confirms that travel *within* the five-county area was intended to be the employee's personal responsibility—the very definition of a non-compensable commute.

If anything, the union contract reinforces the Commission's conclusion. The employer and union expressly contemplated that employees would bear the cost of travel within the five-county area. This negotiated framework reflects the parties' understanding that such travel is a personal, not a work-related, expense. To now allow worker's compensation benefits for injuries during that same travel would contradict the parties' own agreement and undermine the purpose of the reimbursement provision.

### D. The Traveling Employee Doctrine Requires a Business Purpose

The fundamental flaw in the plaintiff's argument is his assertion that the traveling employee doctrine is "designed to protect workers like Mr. McRae who are engaged in employment-related activities during their commutes." This misstates the doctrine. The traveling employee doctrine protects workers whose *work* requires them to travel—not workers who simply commute to a fixed job site.

As the Wisconsin Supreme Court held in **Birkett v. Industrial Commission**, 6 Wis. 2d 85, 94 N.W.2d 161 (1959), the traveling employee doctrine applies when "the employee is engaged in a journey which is itself a part of the service to be performed." The journey must be "at the employer's direction" and "for the employer's benefit." Here, McRae's commute was not for his employer's benefit; it was simply the means by which McRae reached the location where he would begin performing his work. The journey itself was not a service to Porta Painting.

This distinction is critical. The traveling employee doctrine exists because some employees' jobs inherently involve travel—as a necessary part of their work, not as a prelude to it. A delivery driver, for example, begins performing work the moment they start driving their route. McRae, by contrast, did not begin performing work until he arrived at Aldrich Chemical Company and began painting. His commute was preparatory, not work itself.

---

## IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Commission respectfully requests that this Court affirm its decision denying worker's compensation benefits to McRae. The Commission's interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03 is consistent with the statute's text, purpose, and the well-established case law of this state. McRae was engaged in a routine commute from his home to his job site when the accident occurred. Under the coming and going rule, as interpreted by the Commission, such an injury is not compensable.

The plaintiff has not identified any error of law in the Commission's decision. The Commission's findings of fact are supported by the record. The Commission's application of the traveling employee doctrine was correct. Accordingly, the circuit court's affirmance of the Commission's decision should also be affirmed.

**PRELIMINARY RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter comes before the Court on appeal from a circuit court order affirming the decision of the Labor and Industry Review Commission (LIRC). The issue on appeal is whether LIRC erroneously interpreted Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) in determining that the claimant, McRae, was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment at the time of his injury. Specifically, the Court must determine whether McRae’s commute from his residence to a single job site constitutes compensable travel under the "traveling employee" doctrine or falls within the "coming and going" rule.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The Court applies great weight deference to LIRC’s interpretation of the workers’ compensation statutes it administers. As established by the Wisconsin Supreme Court, great weight deference is appropriate because the agency possesses specialized knowledge and experience in the field, and the statutory scheme is inherently technical. *State ex rel. Skow v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 144 Wis. 2d 725, 424 N.W.2d 712 (1988); *Schaefer v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 2005 WI 104, 283 Wis. 2d 302, 699 N.W.2d 853. Under this standard, the agency’s interpretation must be upheld if it is reasonable, even if an alternative interpretation might be plausible. *Maldonado v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 2011 WI 10, 331 Wis. 2d 227, 795 N.W.2d 70.

**III. ANALYSIS**

LIRC determined that McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment because he was engaged in a routine commute without a business-related purpose. The Court finds that this interpretation is reasonable and consistent with established Wisconsin precedent.

**A. The "Coming and Going" Rule**

Wisconsin law generally holds that injuries sustained during a worker’s commute from home to work are not compensable. This is known as the "coming and going" rule. The rationale is that an employee is not acting within the course of employment when traveling to and from the initial place of employment. *Koll v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.*, 190 Wis. 2d 180, 526 N.W.2d 120 (Ct. App. 1994). Here, the facts are undisputed that McRae was driving his personal vehicle from his home to a specific job site in Milwaukee. His paid time did not begin until he arrived at the job site. These facts squarely place the incident within the "coming and going" rule.

**B. The Traveling Employee Doctrine and Special Mission**

The claimant argues that the "traveling employee" doctrine applies. However, this doctrine generally applies to employees whose work inherently involves travel, such that the journey itself is a part of the service to be performed. *Birkett v. Industrial Commission*, 6 Wis. 2d 85, 94 N.W.2d 161 (1959). The record indicates that McRae was assigned to a particular job site for the day; he was not a traveling salesperson or a delivery driver whose work consists of continuous travel.

Furthermore, the "special mission" exception, which might render a commute compensable, requires that the travel be at the direction and for the benefit of the employer and involve a specific task or duty. *Keller v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 2006 WI App 257, 298 Wis. 2d 110, 726 N.W.2d 156. LIRC specifically found that McRae was not performing any special errand and that there was no business-related purpose to the commute. This finding is supported by the fact that McRae received no reimbursement for travel within the five-county area and was not performing any specific task for the employer during the drive. *Peters v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 2007 WI App 218, 305 Wis. 2d 757, 733 N.W.2d 724.

**C. Terms of Employment**

The terms of McRae’s employment further support LIRC’s interpretation. The union contract provided reimbursement for travel only outside a five-county area. This implies that travel within that area was considered a personal responsibility of the employee, not a work-related expense or duty. To interpret the statute otherwise would contradict the parties’ agreement regarding compensation for travel.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

LIRC’s determination that McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment is a reasonable interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1). The agency correctly applied the "coming and going" rule and properly distinguished the "traveling employee" doctrine and "special mission" exception based on the facts that McRae was commuting to a fixed job site in his personal vehicle, without reimbursement, and that his paid time commenced only upon arrival. Therefore, LIRC’s decision is affirmed.

## IV. THE SPECIAL MISSION EXCEPTION IS INAPPLICABLE

The defense further contends that the "special mission" exception does not apply. The Commission specifically found that McRae was not performing any special errand and that there was no business-related purpose to his commute. The prosecution's argument that the special mission exception is "irrelevant" actually supports the Commission's conclusion—the exception simply does not apply to these facts.

The prosecution invokes *Gundersen v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 152 Wis. 2d 244, 448 N.W.2d 459 (Ct. App. 1989), but that case is distinguishable. In *Gundersen*, the court found compensability where the employee's job inherently involved traveling to various locations as part of their daily work. **FINAL RULING**

**I. INTRODUCTION**

This matter is before the Court on appeal from a circuit court order affirming the decision of the Labor and Industry Review Commission (LIRC). The issue on appeal is whether LIRC erroneously interpreted Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) in determining that the claimant, McRae, was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment at the time of his injury. The Court has carefully considered the closing arguments submitted by both the prosecution and the defense. Based upon the arguments presented, the facts provided, and the applicable standard of review, the Court affirms the decision of LIRC.

**II. STANDARD OF REVIEW**

The appropriate standard of review for LIRC’s interpretation of Wis. Stat. § 102.03 is great weight deference. As established in *State ex rel. Skow v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 144 Wis. 2d 725, 424 N.W.2d 712 (1988), this standard is warranted because the Commission possesses specialized knowledge and experience in administering workers’ compensation law. This principle was reaffirmed in *Schaefer v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 2005 WI 104, 283 Wis. 2d 302, 699 N.W.2d 853.

Under great weight deference, the Commission’s interpretation must be upheld if it is reasonable, even if an alternative interpretation might be plausible. *Maldonado v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 2011 WI 10, 331 Wis. 2d 227, 795 N.W.2d 70. The prosecution argues that the Commission’s interpretation is unreasonable because it ignored binding precedent regarding employees without a fixed workplace. The defense argues that the Commission’s application of the "coming and going" rule is reasonable and consistent with the distinction between a commute and traveling employment. The Court must determine whether the Commission’s construction of the statute is reasonable.

**III. ANALYSIS**

LIRC determined that McRae was not performing services growing out of and incidental to his employment because he was engaged in a routine commute without a business-related purpose. The Court finds that this interpretation is reasonable under the great weight deference standard.

**A. The "Coming and Going" Rule and the "Fixed Workplace" Exception**

The prosecution argues that the "coming and going" rule is inapplicable because McRae lacked a fixed workplace, relying heavily on *Kuehn v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 195 Wis. 2d 312, 536 N.W.2d 414 (Ct. App. 1995). In *Kuehn*, the court found that a home health nurse who traveled from home to patients' homes had no fixed workplace and thus her travel was compensable. The prosecution contends that McRae, who was assigned to different job sites daily, falls under this exception.

However, the defense persuasively distinguishes *Kuehn*. The Commission reasonably determined that McRae was not a "traveling employee" in the sense contemplated by *Kuehn*, but rather a worker commuting to a single job site each day. While the location of the job site varied, McRae’s primary duty—painting—was performed at a specific destination once he arrived. The travel itself was not the service he was hired to perform; it was the means of getting to the location where the service would be performed. This distinction is critical. The Commission’s finding that McRae was "simply going to work" is a reasonable application of the "coming and going" rule, as articulated in *Koll v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.*, 190 Wis. 2d 180, 526 N.W.2d 120 (Ct. App. 1994). The fact that his destination changed daily does not automatically render his commute compensable under the "fixed workplace" exception, particularly where, as here, the employee’s work is localized to the job site rather than itinerant in nature.

**B. The Traveling Employee Doctrine**

The prosecution further argues that McRae qualifies as a traveling employee under *Birkett v. Industrial Commission*, 6 Wis. 2d 85, 94 N.W.2d 161 (1959), and *Gundersen v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 152 Wis. 2d 244, 448 N.W.2d 459 (Ct. App. 1989). The traveling employee doctrine applies when travel is an integral part of the service itself.

The Commission reasonably rejected this characterization. McRae is a union painter. His employment contract and job description center on painting, not on travel. The fact that he must drive to a location to paint does not make travel an "integral part of the service" in the same way it is for a salesperson or a home health nurse. The Commission’s interpretation that McRae’s travel was merely incidental to his employment, rather than a primary component of it, is a reasonable construction of the statute. The defense correctly notes that adopting the prosecution’s broad interpretation would effectively eliminate the "coming and going" rule for any construction worker or tradesperson who reports to varying job sites, a result the legislature likely did not intend.

**C. The Special Mission Exception and Contract Terms**

Both parties agree that the "special mission" exception does not apply, as there was no specific errand or business-related purpose for McRae’s commute on the day of the accident. *Keller v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 2006 WI App 257, 298 Wis. 2d 110, 726 N.W.2d 156.

Regarding the union contract, the prosecution argues that the reimbursement policy is irrelevant to the statutory analysis under *Peters v. Labor & Industry Review Commission*, 2007 WI App 218, 305 Wis. 2d 757, 733 N.W.2d 724. While *Peters* holds that contractual terms cannot override statutory compensability, the Commission did not use the contract to override the statute. Rather, it considered the contract as evidence of the parties’ understanding that travel within the five-county area was a personal responsibility. This contextual factor supports the reasonableness of the Commission’s conclusion that the commute was not part of McRae’s employment duties.

**IV. CONCLUSION**

The prosecution has presented a plausible alternative interpretation of the law, arguing that the lack of a fixed workplace brings McRae within the *Kuehn* exception. However, under the great weight deference standard, the Court does not decide which interpretation is best, but whether the agency’s interpretation is reasonable. The Commission’s determination that McRae was a commuter subject to the "coming and going" rule, rather than a traveling employee, is reasonable. It is consistent with the distinction between employees whose work is inherently itinerant and those who simply commute to varying job sites. The Commission’s application of Wis. Stat. § 102.03(1) is supported by the record and precedent. Therefore, LIRC’s decision is affirmed.
